# Bachelor of Artificial Intelligence and Robotics Engineering — Video Series Prompts

## Production approach

Design each course as a full university-level lecture video series, rather than forcing 100,000+ spoken words into one video file. Each course prompt below is intended to generate a minimum of 100,000 spoken words, divided automatically into logically sequenced lecture modules/episodes while maintaining continuity.

## Universal video production directive

Use this production specification with every course prompt below:

> **VIDEO FORMAT:** 1280×720 HD, 16:9, 720p, presentation-style educational video.
>
> **AUDIO:** Mono voice output, clear professional academic narration, studio-quality speech, synchronized with visuals.
>
> **VOICE SELECTION:** Before generating the lecture, display a voice-selection/audition interface containing several suitable voices. Allow the user to listen to samples of each available voice and manually select the preferred voice. Do not automatically lock the user into a voice. Preserve the selected voice consistently throughout the entire course.
>
> **LECTURE LENGTH:** The complete course must support at least 100,000 spoken words of lecture narration. Do not compress the curriculum to fit a short video. Divide the course into a coherent sequence of lecture modules, lessons, demonstrations, laboratories, tutorials, revision sessions and assessments.
>
> **ACADEMIC LEVEL:** Bachelor of Artificial Intelligence and Robotics Engineering.
>
> **TEACHING STANDARD:** University lecturer quality. Explain concepts from fundamentals through intermediate and advanced applications appropriate to the course level. Define technical terminology before using it extensively.
>
> **PRESENTATION STYLE:** Professional university lecture presentation. Combine lecturer narration with animated presentation slides, realistic photographs, realistic 3D renders, technical diagrams, mathematical illustrations, flowcharts, graphs, tables, code demonstrations, simulations and laboratory demonstrations wherever appropriate.
>
> **REALISTIC VISUALS:** Prefer realistic images of robots, laboratories, sensors, computers, engineers, industrial environments, autonomous vehicles, manufacturing environments and real-world applications whenever they improve understanding.
>
> **DIAGRAMS:** Use technically accurate diagrams rather than decorative graphics. Animate diagrams progressively so the learner can understand each component and relationship.
>
> **PRACTICALS — MANDATORY:** Every course must contain substantial practical work. Include guided laboratory exercises, demonstrations, worked examples, coding exercises where appropriate, simulation exercises, experiments, troubleshooting tasks, mini-projects and real-world case studies. Do not make the course purely theoretical.
>
> **INTERACTIVITY:** Include periodic knowledge checks, questions, worked problems, practical challenges, quizzes, assignments and project-based activities.
>
> **LECTURE STRUCTURE:** Each module should contain: (1) Learning objectives; (2) Introduction; (3) Core theory; (4) Visual explanation; (5) Worked examples; (6) Practical demonstration; (7) Laboratory/practical exercise; (8) Real-world application; (9) Common mistakes; (10) Knowledge-check questions; (11) Assignment; and (12) Module summary.
>
> **ACADEMIC RIGOR:** Do not merely summarize textbook topics. Teach the reasoning behind concepts, mathematical foundations where relevant, implementation considerations, engineering trade-offs, limitations and real-world applications.
>
> **CONTINUITY:** Maintain consistent terminology, notation, visual identity and academic progression throughout the entire course.
>
> **CODE:** When programming is relevant, display code on screen with syntax highlighting and explain the code line-by-line. Demonstrate execution and show expected output.
>
> **MATHEMATICS:** Render mathematical equations clearly and explain every important variable and assumption.
>
> **PRACTICAL ENVIRONMENTS:** Where possible, simulate or demonstrate practical work using appropriate tools such as Python, C/C++, MATLAB/Simulink, ROS/ROS 2, Gazebo/robot simulators, OpenCV, TensorFlow/PyTorch, embedded development environments, CAD/robotics tools and other appropriate engineering software.
>
> **ASSESSMENT:** Include quizzes, laboratory assignments, mid-course assessment, practical assessment, final examination preparation and a substantial final practical/project component.
>
> **FINAL COURSE PROJECT:** Where appropriate, conclude with an integrated practical project requiring learners to apply concepts from the entire course.
>
> **ACCESSIBILITY:** Use readable fonts, high-contrast presentation elements, captions/subtitles, clear pronunciation of technical terminology and deliberate pacing.
>
> **OUTPUT:** Produce a complete university-quality lecture series suitable for undergraduate distance learning, online learning, classroom projection and recorded university instruction.

## Course-specific video prompts

Each entry below is a course-specific prompt to be used with the universal production directive above. Begin each course by creating a comprehensive 100,000+ spoken-word university lecture series with the stated title and teach the listed topics and practical work.

### Year 1 — Semester 1

#### AI.101 — Introduction to Artificial Intelligence
Teach the historical development, philosophy, foundations, terminology and modern practice of Artificial Intelligence. Cover intelligent agents, problem solving, search, knowledge representation, reasoning, planning, uncertainty, machine learning, neural networks, natural language processing, computer vision, robotics, generative AI and autonomous systems. Explain the difference between narrow AI, general intelligence and hypothetical superintelligence. Discuss symbolic AI, statistical AI, connectionism and modern hybrid approaches. Include realistic demonstrations of AI systems, animated intelligent-agent diagrams, search-tree visualizations, decision-making examples and real-world AI applications. **Mandatory practicals:** Build simple AI agents, implement search algorithms, create a rule-based expert system, implement a basic classifier, experiment with an AI API/model where appropriate, and complete a practical mini-project. Conclude with an integrated practical project demonstrating an intelligent system.

#### AI.102 — Robotics Fundamentals
Teach the fundamental principles of robotics, robot anatomy, coordinate systems, actuators, sensors, motors, gears, mechanical structures, end-effectors, robot controllers, power systems and robot programming. Explain industrial robots, mobile robots, humanoids, drones, service robots and autonomous robots. Use realistic robot images, exploded mechanical diagrams, actuator animations and laboratory demonstrations. **Mandatory practicals:** Assemble/design a simple robotic system, control motors, read sensors, program robot movements, construct a basic mobile robot and perform troubleshooting. Include a final robot-building/design project.

#### AI.103 — Machine Learning
Teach supervised, unsupervised and semi-supervised learning; regression; classification; clustering; dimensionality reduction; feature engineering; model evaluation; overfitting; regularization; cross-validation; decision trees; ensemble methods and introductory probabilistic learning. Include mathematical explanations, datasets, graphs and realistic industry applications. **Mandatory practicals:** Use Python to clean datasets, visualize data, train models, evaluate models, compare algorithms and deploy a basic machine-learning application. Include a complete end-to-end machine-learning project.

#### AI.104 — Computer Vision
Teach image formation, pixels, color spaces, filtering, edge detection, feature extraction, segmentation, object detection, image classification, tracking and introductory deep-learning vision. Use realistic camera footage, annotated images and animated computer-vision pipelines. **Mandatory practicals:** Implement image-processing algorithms using Python/OpenCV, perform object detection, image classification and camera-based experiments. Include a practical computer-vision project.

#### AI.105 — Natural Language Processing
Teach text processing, tokenization, stemming, lemmatization, language models, embeddings, sentiment analysis, text classification, sequence models, transformers, conversational AI and modern large language models. Demonstrate NLP systems with realistic interfaces. **Mandatory practicals:** Build a text-processing pipeline, sentiment classifier, document classifier, chatbot and introductory transformer-based application. Conclude with an NLP project.

#### AI.106 — Control Systems
Teach feedback, open-loop and closed-loop systems, transfer functions, dynamic systems, stability, PID control, sensors, actuators, state-space representations and digital control. Use block diagrams, mathematical models and animated control loops. **Mandatory practicals:** Simulate control systems, tune PID controllers, control simulated motors/robots and analyze system response. Include MATLAB/Simulink or equivalent simulation demonstrations and a final control-system project.

## Global visual style (append to every course prompt)

> **VISUAL QUALITY DIRECTIVE:** Use a sophisticated modern university presentation aesthetic. Do not fill every slide with text. Use a balanced combination of concise lecture points, realistic photographs, realistic 3D robotics imagery, technical diagrams, animated processes, graphs, equations, simulations, code, laboratory footage and engineering illustrations. Whenever a concept involves a physical system, show the physical system. Whenever a concept involves an algorithm, show the algorithm visually. Whenever a concept involves mathematics, show the mathematical model and then connect it to a practical engineering example. Whenever possible, transition from **theory → diagram → demonstration → practical exercise → real-world application**.
>
> **PRACTICAL-FIRST RULE:** Every major theoretical concept must eventually be connected to something the student can implement, simulate, measure, program, construct, analyze or troubleshoot.
>
> **LECTURER PRESENCE:** Maintain the feeling of a real university lecturer teaching an actual class. The narration should be authoritative, conversational, explanatory and intellectually engaging—not robotic, excessively fast or merely reading slide text.
>
> **VOICE:** Before course generation, provide multiple available voice samples for audition. Let the user listen to each voice and select one. Once selected, use that voice consistently for the course.
>
> **AUDIO:** 720p video, 16:9, mono narration, clean speech, balanced volume, synchronized narration and captions.
>
> **COURSE COMPLETION:** Never terminate the course merely because the initial video length has been reached. Continue generating sequential lessons until the required curriculum has been comprehensively taught and the narration target of at least 100,000 spoken words has been achieved.

## Implementation note

If implementing this in an AI video-generation webpage/system, do not hard-code a particular voice into the prompts. Use this flow instead:

**Course selection → Voice audition → User selects voice → Course syllabus generated → 100k+ narration generated in modules → Slides/diagrams/images generated → Practical demonstrations → TTS → Mono audio → 720p video rendering → Module-by-module output → Complete course playlist.**

This also makes it possible to use different voices later without rewriting the course prompts.
