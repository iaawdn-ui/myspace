# Prevention, Early Intervention and Developmental Monitoring

## 7. Counselling Implications

The first counselling implication is one of translation. Adolescents rarely present their difficulties in developmental language; they present a failed test, a furious parent, a lost friend, a phone confiscated, a body they hate, a future they cannot picture. The counsellor's task is to hear the developmental question inside the presenting problem: what task is this adolescent working on, what capacity is being built or blocked, and what in the environment is helping or hindering? When that translation is done well, adolescents often experience immediate relief, not because the problem has vanished, but because it has become intelligible and therefore workable. Intelligibility is itself an intervention.

Second, developmental knowledge disciplines the choice of method. The counsellor considers the adolescent's capacity for abstraction, tolerance for ambiguity, emotional vocabulary, attention span, and position in the family before selecting how to work, not after the method has failed. Early adolescents generally need shorter inferential chains, more activity, more structure and more frequent summary; older adolescents can usually sustain reflective, verbal and future-oriented work, provided trust is established. Across all ages, explanation must be offered in language the young person can repeat back to a parent or friend, because an explanation that cannot leave the room has limited developmental power.

Third, developmentally informed counsellors work on the environment as readily as on the individual. A plan that asks an adolescent to change while every surrounding routine rewards the old pattern is a plan designed to fail and to blame the adolescent for failing. Parents, teachers, coaches, siblings and peers are therefore engaged as co-authors of change wherever consent and safety allow, and the counsellor specifies, for each setting, what will be noticed, what will be responded to differently, and how everyone will know whether it is working. This ecological habit distinguishes graduate practice from well-intentioned conversation.

Fourth, timing and dosage matter. Adolescents live by terms, examinations, seasons, family events and friendship cycles, and an intervention that ignores that calendar will be experienced as one more adult demand. Skilled counsellors negotiate when work begins, how progress will be reviewed, what will happen during examinations or holidays, and how endings will be handled, because predictability is itself regulating for a developing nervous system. They also plan for the ordinary relapses of development: the bad week, the friendship rupture, the disappointing result, so that setbacks are interpreted as information rather than as evidence that nothing works.

## 8. Case Illustration: Chinedu

Chinedu is thirteen years old and was referred in a boarding school. The presenting account, assembled from adults, emphasised behaviour; the developmental account, assembled gradually with Chinedu, emphasised a task in progress and an environment that had not yet adjusted to it. That contrast, between the behavioural referral and the developmental formulation, is typical of the territory covered by prevention, early intervention and developmental monitoring, and learning to move from the first to the second without dismissing adult concern is one of the central skills of this course.

Working over eight sessions, the counsellor assessed universal prevention, selective prevention, indicated prevention, built the timeline described in the Practice Laboratory below, agreed goals with Chinedu before confirming them with the adults, and changed one specified response in each of the home and school settings. Progress was reviewed against a prediction written in advance. The case did not resolve neatly, because developmental cases rarely do; it resolved sufficiently, in a direction the adolescent could explain and sustain, which is the realistic standard for much of this work.

## 9. Assessment and Developmental Formulation

Assessment in this area combines four sources, and no single source is sufficient. The adolescent's own account comes first, because adolescents are the only direct witnesses to much of their developmental lives and because being interviewed seriously is itself developmental. Informant accounts from parents, caregivers and teachers follow, gathered with attention to which settings each informant actually sees. Structured measures are used third, as aids to conversation and as repeatable baselines, never as standalone diagnoses. Fourth comes the record: school history, health information and previous services, read for trajectory rather than for labels.

Timing converts identical events into different developmental experiences. Entering puberty earlier or later than peers, changing schools at twelve rather than fourteen, losing a parent before rather than after a network of friendships is established: each alters what the event means and what resources are available to meet it. Counsellors should routinely construct a simple developmental timeline with adolescents and families, marking transitions, losses, moves, illnesses, achievements and changes of school. The exercise is inexpensive, often moving, and routinely reveals the coincidence of demands that no single-informant account had made visible.

| Assessment source | What it contributes | Principal caution |
|---|---|---|
| Adolescent interview | Meaning, goals, private experience, digital and peer life | Disclosure is gradual and trust-dependent |
| Parent or caregiver report | Early history, home behaviour, family process | Sees home behaviour better than peer or inner life |
| Teacher report | Classroom functioning, peer standing, trajectory | Shaped by classroom fit and expectations |
| Standardised measures | Comparable baseline, progress monitoring | Samples of behaviour, not verdicts on character |
| Records and timeline | Trajectory, transitions, previous help | Labels outlive the evidence that produced them |

## 10. Intervention Planning Across Settings

Second, developmental knowledge disciplines the choice of method. The counsellor considers the adolescent's capacity for abstraction, tolerance for ambiguity, emotional vocabulary, attention span, and position in the family before selecting how to work, not after the method has failed. Early adolescents generally need shorter inferential chains, more activity, more structure and more frequent summary; older adolescents can usually sustain reflective, verbal and future-oriented work, provided trust is established. Across all ages, explanation must be offered in language the young person can repeat back to a parent or friend, because an explanation that cannot leave the room has limited developmental power.

A final property of mechanism is that it is almost always relational. Even processes that appear to be purely internal, such as private reasoning, rumination or self-talk, were typically learned in relationships, are triggered by relationships and are revised in relationships. This is encouraging news for counsellors, because the counselling relationship is itself a developmental environment: a bounded, reliable, attentive relationship in which an adolescent can practise disclosure, disagreement, reflection and repair. When this book describes a technique, therefore, the student should always ask two questions: what process is this technique intended to set in motion, and what in the relationship makes that process possible for this particular adolescent at this particular age.

Intervention plans in this territory should specify, in writing, five things: the mechanism being targeted; the action to be taken in sessions; the action to be taken by the adolescent between sessions; the one specified change of response by adults at home and at school; and the observable change, with its timescale, that will be treated as evidence that the formulation was right. Plans that omit any of the five are incomplete at graduate standard. Plans that include all five can be supervised, evaluated and honestly revised, which is precisely what makes them professional.

## 11. Culture, Diversity and Equity

Equity is the second cultural obligation. Adolescents do not meet services on equal terms: cost, distance, language, disability access, parental working hours, digital access and prior experiences of being dismissed all decide who actually receives help. A developmentally informed service therefore audits its own accessibility with the same rigour it applies to assessment, asking who is not in the room, who dropped out after one session, whose parents were never successfully contacted and whose difficulties were recorded as defiance rather than distress. These are empirical questions, answerable from routine records, and services that ask them regularly become fairer as well as more effective.

Finally, counsellors should examine their own developmental autobiography. Every practitioner was once an adolescent in a particular family, school, culture and historical moment, and that experience silently supplies intuitions about what is normal, shocking, lazy, brave or dangerous. Supervision and reflective practice, treated in Appendix G, exist largely to make those intuitions visible so that they can be used as information rather than obeyed as rules. The adolescent in front of you is not you at fifteen, and the families you serve are not required to repeat, or to repair, your own history.

No developmental pattern described in this book floats free of culture. Cultures differ in what they expect of adolescents, how much autonomy they grant and when, how emotion may be displayed, what respect requires, how gender is organised, what futures are honoured and what help-seeking means. The counsellor's obligation is neither to treat cultural difference as pathology nor to treat it as an excuse for harm, but to understand what a behaviour means within the adolescent's own cultural world before deciding what, if anything, should change. That understanding is achieved by asking, not assuming: adolescents and families are the primary informants about their own values, and the counsellor's expertise lies in the developmental questions, not in pronouncing on the worth of a way of life.

## 12. Ethics, Safeguarding and Limits

Safeguarding overrides confidentiality where there is risk of serious harm, and the counsellor's duty is to act proportionately: to gather enough information to judge risk, to consult supervision promptly, to involve the adolescent in what will happen next wherever possible, to share only what is necessary with those who need it, and to record decisions and their reasons accurately. The developmental point is easily missed: how adults handle a disclosure becomes part of the adolescent's working model of whether adults can be trusted with the truth. Even necessary breaches of confidence can therefore be done in ways that preserve dignity, explanation and continuing relationship.

Ethical practice with adolescents begins before the first intervention, in the clarity of the contract. The young person should know, in words they can test, why they are seeing a counsellor, what will happen to what they say, who will be told what and in what circumstances, how records are kept, and what choices are genuinely theirs. Assent from the adolescent and consent from those with parental responsibility are not bureaucratic preliminaries; they are the adolescent's first experience of the respect for autonomy that the work claims to develop. Where law, school policy or safeguarding duty limits confidentiality, the limits are explained early, calmly and more than once, because adolescents disclose gradually as trust is tested.

:::ethics Ethical checkpoints for this chapter
- Was assent obtained from the adolescent, and consent from those with parental responsibility, in language each party could test?
- Were the limits of confidentiality explained before disclosure, including safeguarding duties?
- Is every proposed action proportionate, recorded and within the counsellor's competence and local policy?
- Has supervision been consulted where risk, conflict of interest or uncertainty is present?
:::

## 13. Strengths, Limitations and Controversies

The approach developed in this chapter has genuine strengths: it is evidence-informed, it respects the adolescent as an active participant, it works on environments as well as individuals, and it produces plans that can be evaluated. Its limitations are equally real. Developmental explanations can be misused to excuse harmful behaviour or to delay necessary protection; measures developed in one culture may misdescribe adolescents in another; and the press of school and clinic workloads tempts practitioners to substitute checklists for thought. The scholarly controversies — about universality, about the size and replicability of reported effects, and about where description ends and medicalisation begins — are live, and students are expected to be able to argue more than one side of each.

| Strengths of the developmental approach | Limitations to be managed |
|---|---|
| Explains behaviour through mechanism, not blame | Can be misused to over-explain or excuse |
| Matches method to developmental level | Requires time, training and supervision |
| Engages family, school and peer contexts | Depends on adult cooperation that may be limited |
| Produces testable, revisable plans | Evidence may not transfer across cultures unexamined |

## 14. Chapter Summary

This chapter has treated prevention, Early Intervention and Developmental Monitoring as a developmental process with identifiable concepts — universal prevention, selective prevention, indicated prevention, early intervention, developmental monitoring, screening ethics — operating through specifiable mechanisms, unfolding along trajectories that differ between individuals, and shaped continuously by family, school, peer, digital and cultural contexts. Its practice conclusion is demanding but simple: assess developmentally, formulate so that the formulation can fail, agree goals with the adolescent first, change the environment alongside the individual, and review outcomes against predictions made in advance.

## 16. Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example

The laboratory that follows is a complete, worked case in the territory of this chapter. It is a composite, constructed for teaching, and every name and detail is fictional. Students should work through it actively: at the end of each step, before reading on, write what you would have done and why. The discipline being trained is not imitation of this counsellor but the habit of making developmental reasoning explicit at exactly the points where practice usually becomes vague.

### Step 1 — Referral and first reading of the case

The referral, when it arrives, rarely names the developmental issue. In this worked example, Chinedu, aged thirteen, was referred in a boarding school because adults were worried and the adolescent was, in the referral's words, 'not cooperating'. The counsellor's first professional act is to slow the referral down: to ask who is worried, about what exactly, since when, in which settings, with what exceptions, and what each adult hopes will be different. Those five questions convert a complaint into assessable information and protect the adolescent from being treated as the whole problem. The counsellor also records, before meeting anyone, the developmental hypotheses the referral suggests, so that later impressions can be tested against them rather than silently replacing them.

### Step 2 — Developmental history and timeline

The counsellor next builds a developmental history with the family and, separately, with Chinedu: pregnancy and early health where records allow, milestones, temperament, schooling history and transitions, friendships, losses, illnesses, family changes, achievements and the onset and course of the present difficulty. Plotted on a single timeline, coincidences become visible that no participant had noticed: demands that arrived together, supports that disappeared together, and periods when the adolescent coped well that reveal what works. The timeline is shared back in plain language, because families who can see their own story developmentally argue less about blame and cooperate more about next steps.

### Step 3 — Focused assessment of the chapter's core concepts

Assessment then turns to the concepts at the centre of this chapter: universal prevention, selective prevention, indicated prevention, early intervention, developmental monitoring, screening ethics. For each, the counsellor gathers three kinds of evidence: the adolescent's own account, obtained in an unhurried interview that begins with open questions; observation or report from at least one other setting; and, where a suitable instrument exists, a brief standardised measure used as a structured prompt rather than as a verdict. Scores are recorded with their context — who completed the measure, when, and in what mood and setting — because developmental measures are samples of behaviour, not X-rays of character. Disagreements between informants are treated as information about settings, not as evidence that someone is lying.

### Step 4 — Writing a formulation that can fail

The formulation is written in a single paragraph that a colleague could test. It names the presenting difficulty, the developmental task in progress, the mechanisms maintaining the difficulty (drawing explicitly on universal prevention, selective prevention, indicated prevention), the strengths and protective factors already present, and a prediction: if the proposed support is right, some specified, observable change should appear within a specified period. If the change does not appear, the formulation, not the adolescent, is the first thing revised. This discipline is what distinguishes graduate formulation from summary, and students are expected to reproduce it in the coursework portfolio described in Appendix B.

### Step 5 — Agreeing goals with the adolescent first

Goals are negotiated with Chinedu before they are negotiated with adults, and the order matters. The counsellor asks what the adolescent would like to be different, what adults are asking for, and where the two lists overlap; work begins in the overlap, where motivation already exists. Goals are stated behaviourally and developmentally: not 'be more confident' but 'ask one question in class each day this week', with a simple way of noticing success. Where adult and adolescent goals conflict, the conflict is named honestly, the counsellor's safeguarding duties are explained, and the areas of genuine choice available to the adolescent are made as large as safety allows.

### Step 6 — Intervention across settings

The intervention plan assigns actions to every relevant setting. In sessions, the counsellor works directly on the chapter's core processes using methods matched to Chinedu's developmental level: concrete, activity-based and highly structured where abstraction is still fragile; reflective, verbal and future-oriented where it is secure. Between sessions, one small practice task is agreed and reviewed without punishment for non-completion, since failure to complete is diagnostic information about the plan's fit. At home and at school, named adults change one specified response each — what they notice, what they say first, and what they do after — so that the adolescent's new behaviour meets a new environment rather than the old rewards for the old pattern.

### Step 7 — Review, revision and the meaning of slow progress

At the agreed review point, the prediction from Step 4 is compared with what actually happened, using the adolescent's report, adult report and any repeated measure. Three outcomes are possible, and each has a professional response: if change is as predicted, the plan is consolidated and responsibility is progressively handed to the adolescent and family; if change is partial, the mechanism is re-examined and the environment-side actions are strengthened before the adolescent is asked for more; if there is no change or deterioration, the formulation is revised, supervision is consulted, and referral or a different level of service is considered openly. Slow progress in development is common and is not, by itself, evidence of failure; unexplained insistence on an unrevised plan is.

### Step 8 — Ending, consolidation and developmental handover

Endings are planned as developmental events, not administrative ones. The final sessions review what changed, what the adolescent now understands about their own development, what early signs of difficulty they can recognise, and what they will do, concretely, when those signs appear. Gains are attributed accurately — largely to the adolescent's own work and to changed responses around them — because attribution determines whether confidence survives after the counsellor leaves. A written summary, agreed with the adolescent, records what helped, what did not, and what a future counsellor, teacher or parent should know, with consent arrangements for sharing made explicit. The case closes with the counsellor recording, for supervision, what the case taught them about prevention, early intervention and developmental monitoring.

## 19. Extended Developmental Analysis: Reading Prevention, Early Intervention and Developmental Monitoring Across Settings

Development does not happen in general; it happens in places, at times of day, before audiences. This extended analysis therefore reads the subject of this chapter — prevention, early intervention and developmental monitoring — across the five settings in which adolescent life is actually lived, and asks of each setting the same four questions: what does this setting demand, what does it reward, what does it make difficult, and what one change within it would most help the adolescents this chapter is about? Students should complete the analysis for one real (anonymised) or composite adolescent before the seminar, because the exercise reliably exposes how much of what is referred as individual difficulty is, in part, setting difficulty wearing an adolescent's name.

Read at school, the processes at the heart of this chapter — above all selective prevention, early intervention and screening ethics — take a distinctive form. The relevant stage is lessons, transitions between classes, group work, examinations, discipline systems and the public judgement of competence, and the significant audience is teachers and school leaders. Three features of this setting decide how development proceeds here. First, its demands are specific: what counts as competence, maturity or trouble at school is defined by this setting's routines and values, not by adolescence in general, which is why the same adolescent can appear capable in one setting and chaotic in another without anyone being mistaken. ## 20. Guided Case Conference and Chapter Consolidation

A case conference is where developmental reasoning is tested socially, because each participant sees a different adolescent. Convene, in imagination, the conference for a case in the territory of this chapter: Funke is discussed by a parent, a teacher, a school counsellor and, crucially, with Funke's own account read first and taken seriously. The exercise below reproduces the movement a well-run conference should make.

**The adolescent's account comes first, and it changes the meeting.** Funke describes what the difficulty feels like from inside, what adults misunderstand, and what would actually help. Conferences that begin here make fewer attributional errors, because every later claim must survive comparison with the young person's own experience of universal prevention and selective prevention. The chair's first duty is protective: to ensure the account is heard as evidence rather than as an excuse to be managed.

**The parent's account supplies history and home process.** Parents contribute the developmental timeline, the family's values and constraints, what has been tried at home and what it cost. Skilled chairs listen here for process — how conflict is repaired, how rules change with age, where worry is highest at 2 a.m. — rather than adjudicating between the parent's and the adolescent's versions of events, since both are usually accurate reports of different settings.

**The teacher's account supplies trajectory and public competence.** School contributes attendance, attainment over time, peer standing, response to previous support and the classroom conditions under which the adolescent succeeds. The developmental question put to school is precise: under what conditions, which this school can actually arrange, does this adolescent do well — and how much of the week currently consists of those conditions?

**The counsellor integrates, predicts and assigns.** The conference closes not with shared concern but with a formulation, a prediction and named actions: the counsellor states the developmental reading in plain language, specifies what change is expected and by when, assigns one action to each setting, agrees what information will be shared with whom and on what consent basis, and fixes the review date before anyone leaves. Conferences that end this way convert goodwill into development; conferences that do not, however warm, mainly redistribute anxiety.

**Supervision questions arising.** What did each account make visible, and what did each conceal? Whose definition of the problem prevailed, and was that justified? What would disconfirm the agreed formulation, and who is responsible for noticing? What did the conference itself teach the adolescent, who will hear of it, about whether adults can think together on their behalf?

**Consolidation task.** Before leaving this chapter, write three short paragraphs in your developmental notebook: the mechanism from this chapter you now understand best, stated without jargon; the claim in this chapter you are least sure the evidence supports, with the study that would settle it; and one thing you will do differently in your next conversation with an adolescent, a parent or a teacher as a result of this chapter. Dated entries of this kind, accumulated across all fifty-eight chapters, constitute the reflective spine of the course portfolio described in Appendix B and are sampled in the assessment described in Chapter 58.

## 21. Lecturer's Closing Remarks and Bridge Forward

Let me close this chapter as I would close the lecture, by saying plainly what I most want you to carry out of the room. The subject of this chapter — prevention, early intervention and developmental monitoring — is not an examination topic that happens to have practical uses; it is a piece of the daily reality of every adolescent you will ever counsel, whether it arrives named in the referral or hidden inside some other complaint. Because so many adult difficulties begin in adolescence, prevention and early intervention are among the highest-yield activities in the field, provided screening is ethical, proportionate and connected to real help. That is the claim of the chapter, and everything in it — the concepts of universal prevention, selective prevention, indicated prevention, early intervention, the mechanisms, the case, the laboratory — was arranged to make that claim observable rather than merely assertable.

Notice, too, what the chapter has asked of you as a reader. It has asked you to hold a general pattern and an individual adolescent in mind at the same time, to prefer mechanisms to labels, trajectories to snapshots and predictions to impressions, and to treat families, schools, peers and digital worlds as part of the case rather than as background to it. Those habits are the course. A student who finishes CAC. 702 with the habits but imperfect recall of detail is well educated for practice, because detail can always be looked up in this book; a student with perfect recall and none of the habits has acquired a vocabulary, not a profession.

Two cautions before you turn the page. First, developmental knowledge is easily misused as a new way of sounding certain about adolescents, and certainty is the enemy this book has opposed from its first chapter: hold what you have learned here as a set of well-supported hypotheses, and let each real adolescent test them. Second, do not let the scale of adolescent need, which this chapter may have made more visible to you, become a reason for professional despair or for its opposite, heroic over-involvement. The evidence reviewed here points the other way: ordinary systems, steadily strengthened — one changed adult response, one repaired routine, one school condition adjusted, one adolescent who discovers that an adult can be trusted with the truth — are how development is actually helped.
