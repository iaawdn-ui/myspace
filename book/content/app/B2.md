# Appendix B2: Case Studies in Anxiety, Mood, and Avoidance

## Case 5: The Assembly

**Presentation.** Sena, thirteen, vomited before a school assembly after being asked to read. She now avoids corridors, group work, presentations, and the dining hall. She can speak comfortably with two friends and at home.

**Data.** Her attendance is 62 per cent. She fears vomiting and being watched swallowing. Parents initially allowed her to remain home on presentation days. A teacher once commented, “We haven't got all day,” when she paused. She has no eating disorder symptoms reported, but medical causes for nausea have not been assessed.

**Tasks.** Conduct differential assessment among social anxiety, panic, specific phobia, trauma response, medical problems, and school-based humiliation. Build a hierarchy that starts with success and includes checking feared outcomes. Analyse parental accommodation and teacher practices. Identify measures beyond attendance.

**Expected integration.** CBT/exposure principles, social cognitive mastery, IPT-A peer issues, school consultation, family work, and developmental sensitivity to shame.

## Case 6: The Empty Calendar

**Presentation.** Jonas, sixteen, stopped attending college after failing two assessments. He sleeps until afternoon, games at night, and says he is “not depressed, just done”. His mother finds unopened letters from college. He has lost contact with friends.

**Data.** He previously wanted to study engineering. He describes every task as evidence that he is behind. He avoids messages because they contain deadlines. He denies suicidal intent but says the future feels “blank”. Appetite and concentration are poor.

**Tasks.** Assess depression, suicide risk, substance use, sleep, learning problems, physical health, and family responses. Construct a behavioural activation plan with mastery and social contact. Use motivational interviewing to explore the apprenticeship/college discrepancy. Identify how academic backlog should be triaged so return does not recreate failure.

**Teaching point.** Avoidance may protect against shame in the short term while removing every source of reward and competence.

## Case 7: The Perfect Score

**Presentation.** Hana, fifteen, studies five hours nightly, cries over minor errors, checks homework repeatedly, and refuses to submit work she considers imperfect. Teachers describe her as a model student. She has headaches and insomnia.

**History.** Her older sibling received a prestigious scholarship. Hana says her parents never pressure her, but family conversation often centres on rankings. She fears disappointing teachers and being exposed as “actually average”.

**Tasks.** Formulate perfectionism across cognitive, family, cultural, school, and identity dimensions. Distinguish excellence from compulsive checking. Design experiments in “good-enough” submission, sleep protection, and family feedback changes. Consider obsessive-compulsive assessment.

**Teaching point.** A school may reinforce the very pattern that harms the student because it produces desirable outputs.

## Case 8: The Friend Who Did Not Reply

**Presentation.** Bea, fifteen, has self-harmed twice after friendship conflicts and sends repeated messages seeking reassurance. She describes friends as either perfect or cruel. Adults use the phrase “attention-seeking”.

**Safety note.** This fictional case requires a full current risk assessment in any teaching discussion. Do not treat the vignette as sufficient information for real intervention.

**Tasks.** Apply IPT-A, mentalization, emotion-focused, attachment, and DBT-informed lenses. Identify where each lens helps or risks blame. Develop a crisis plan, family communication plan, and school response that avoids both punitive withdrawal and unlimited reassurance. Examine staff language and team splitting.

**Teaching point.** Behaviour described as attention-seeking is often attachment- and safety-seeking expressed under high arousal. The task is to make safer bids more effective, not to make need disappear.

## Case 9: The Quiet Ward of One

**Presentation.** Kofi, eleven, recently placed in foster care, is polite with adults and refuses school. He hoards food in his room, wets the bed, and becomes distressed when routines change. He has had three schools in two years.

**Tasks.** Use trauma-informed, attachment, developmental, ecological, and educational perspectives. Prioritise placement stability, predictable routines, school transition, medical and continence assessment without shame, and carer support. Decide what trauma-specific work, if any, is appropriate now.

**Teaching point.** Stabilisation is treatment, not waiting. A new therapy relationship cannot compensate for repeated environmental discontinuity unless the system also changes.

## Case 10: The Joke That Travelled

**Presentation.** A private joke image of Priya, fourteen, is altered and shared beyond the intended group. She refuses school, deletes accounts, then creates new anonymous accounts at night. Some students claim she “sent it first” and should expect consequences.

**Tasks.** Address safeguarding, online exploitation or bullying pathways, school response, family support, sleep, peer bystanders, and the distinction between consensual sharing and non-consensual redistribution. Avoid victim blaming. Plan graded school return and digital safety without demanding that Priya educate the school.

**Teaching point.** Digital harm is interpersonal, institutional, and embodied. Individual coping cannot substitute for platform, peer, and school action.
