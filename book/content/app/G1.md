# Appendix G: Supervision and Reflection Templates

## G1. Purpose of Supervision

Supervision protects clients, develops practitioners, and sustains ethical judgement. It is not case gossip, managerial surveillance alone, or a place to obtain permission for decisions the practitioner remains responsible for making. Good supervision integrates clinical formulation, intervention skills, relationship processes, culture and power, risk, organisational context, and practitioner wellbeing.

Students should prepare cases before supervision and identify the decision or uncertainty they are bringing. “Tell me what to do” is less useful than “I am caught between protecting confidentiality and involving a parent; here are the facts, risks, options, and my current leaning.”

## G3. Reflective Practice Prompts

After a session, consider:

- What did I notice first, and what did I not notice?
- Whose language dominated my formulation—the young person's, parent's, school's, theory's, or mine?
- What did I feel in my body, and how did it influence pace or intervention?
- Where did I rush to fix, rescue, withdraw, confront, or please?
- What cultural or power assumption may have shaped my reading?
- What did the young person teach me about what helps?
- What would I do differently with the same information?
- What do I need to learn, read, practise, or refer?
- Is there a record, safeguarding, consent, or follow-up action outstanding?

Reflection is incomplete if it produces only self-criticism. It should produce a testable change in attention, behaviour, or systems.

What was my contribution?

What repair was attempted and how did it land?

What confidentiality, family, cultural, or institutional factor was involved?

What will I do at the next contact?

What supervision or team action is needed?
