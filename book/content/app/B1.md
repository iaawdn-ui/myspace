# Appendix B: Case Study Portfolio

## B1. How to Use This Portfolio

The cases in this appendix are fictional composites created for teaching. Any resemblance to an identifiable person is unintended. They are designed to help students integrate theory, development, culture, ethics, and planning. Names, settings, and details should not be copied into real records.

For each case, students should produce:

1. a concise presenting summary;
2. a five-part formulation;
3. at least two competing theoretical readings;
4. a risk and safeguarding analysis;
5. a sequenced treatment plan;
6. family, school, and service coordination;
7. outcome measures and review points;
8. a young-person-friendly explanation of the plan;
9. an account of what the practitioner might be getting wrong.

The portfolio is not a test of finding one hidden diagnosis. Strong work tolerates uncertainty, identifies missing information, and states what observation would change the formulation.

## Case 1: The Boy Who Arrived After the Bell

**Age and context.** Tunde, thirteen, lives with his mother and younger sister. His father works abroad for long periods. Tunde was a regular attender in primary school. Since moving to secondary school he arrives late three or four days a week, misses registration, and has received repeated detentions. Teachers describe him as bright, funny, and increasingly rude when challenged publicly.

**History.** Tunde changes buses once. The second bus is unreliable. At home, his mother leaves for work before he wakes. His nine-year-old sister depends on him to check that the door is locked and that she has her school bag. Tunde stays up late playing online games with cousins in another time zone. He says mornings feel “impossible” and that detention is “better than walking in when everyone looks”.

**Observations.** He concentrates well in afternoon lessons. He is popular but avoids reading aloud. Screening suggests possible dyslexic difficulties not yet assessed. His mother feels blamed by school letters. She says Tunde “chooses games over his future”. Tunde says adults only contact home when he is in trouble.

**Student tasks.** Separate lateness as behaviour from explanations. Analyse sleep, transport, caring responsibility, public shame, literacy, family communication, and school sanctions. Design a plan that does not make Tunde responsible for adult transport failures. Identify what should change at home, in school systems, and in Tunde's routines. State what information is missing about his father's role, the sister's school arrangements, and the journey.

**Teaching point.** A behaviour can be simultaneously a skill problem, a systems problem, a developmental problem, and a rational response to humiliation. Contingency management without ecological change would be efficient-looking and unjust.

## Case 2: The Girl Who Became the Family Interpreter

**Age and context.** Mei, fifteen, migrated with her parents and grandmother two years ago. She is fluent in the school language and interprets for medical appointments, housing letters, and parent evenings. Teachers praise her maturity. She has developed stomach pain, declining grades, and anger at home.

**History.** Her mother has chronic illness; her father works nights. Mei's grandmother tells stories about the family's former home and worries that Mei is “losing respect”. Mei has stopped attending an art club because appointments and homework translation take priority. She says, “If I don't do it, nobody understands anything. If I do it, I get told things a child shouldn't know.”

**Risk and needs.** No suicidal thinking is reported. She has missed meals before examinations and says pain improves at weekends. Medical assessment has not yet occurred. Her parents value education highly and feel ashamed that services know about family difficulties.

**Student tasks.** Formulate parentification, migration loss, language brokering, gender expectations, somatic symptoms, and school pressure. Identify professional interpreter needs and medical steps. Design family work that redistributes adult responsibilities without shaming parents. Consider how to ask Mei what she wants adults not to know through her interpreting role.

**Teaching point.** Competence can conceal burden. A strengths-based reading that only celebrates maturity will collude with the system extracting resilience from her.

## Case 3: Two Homes, Two Rules, One Phone

**Age and context.** Leo, fourteen, lives alternately with separated parents. His mother permits phone use until 10 p.m.; his father removes all devices on arrival. Leo has begun keeping a second phone given by an older cousin. School reports conflict after weekends at his father's home.

**History.** The separation followed high conflict. Each parent asks Leo what happens at the other home. Leo tells each what reduces immediate questioning. His grades are stable, but he has stomach aches on transition days and has started refusing to go to his father's home, then crying afterward because he misses him.

**Student tasks.** Map loyalty binds, transitions, surveillance, autonomy, peer contact, and inconsistent contingencies. Distinguish safety monitoring from interrogation. Draft a parent-only meeting agenda that does not use Leo as messenger. Identify what information the counsellor should and should not carry between households.

**Teaching point.** Consistency does not require identical households. Children can manage different rules when transitions are predictable, adults communicate directly, and differences are not framed as proof that one home is corrupt.

## Case 4: The Student Who Was “Fine” All Day

**Age and context.** Amelie, twelve, is quiet, compliant, and academically successful. At home she cries for long periods, refuses food on some evenings, and repeatedly asks whether her parents are angry. Teachers are surprised by the referral.

**History.** Her parents separated amicably but both work long hours. Amelie cares for a younger brother after school. She worries about saying the wrong thing and checks doors and messages repeatedly. She says school is easier because “the rules are on the wall; at home I have to guess”.

**Student tasks.** Explore masking, anxiety, reassurance, perfectionism, caring responsibilities, family emotional communication, eating and medical assessment, and the risk of schools dismissing distress because attainment is high. Plan parent work and graded reduction of reassurance without abrupt withdrawal of comfort.

**Teaching point.** Functioning is setting-specific. High achievement can coexist with severe distress and may delay recognition.
