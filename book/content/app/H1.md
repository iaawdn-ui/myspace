# Appendix H: Referral, Documentation, and Crisis Resources

## H1. Principles for Referral

Referral is a clinical intervention, not disposal. A good referral matches need, urgency, competence, setting, culture and language, disability access, family logistics, and young person's preference. It includes a specific question, relevant information, consent or lawful basis, risk communication, and follow-up to confirm receipt.

Refer when needs exceed practitioner competence or scope, when medical or psychiatric assessment is required, when risk cannot be managed in the setting, when specialist treatment is indicated, when conflict of interest arises, or when the young person requests another service and it is clinically appropriate.

Do not refer merely because a case is complex, unrewarding, culturally unfamiliar, or likely to affect outcome statistics. Complexity may require consultation and joint work rather than transfer.

## H2. Referral Decision Checklist

- What decision or treatment can the receiving service provide that this service cannot?
- How urgent is it, and what must happen while waiting?
- Has medical assessment been considered?
- What safeguarding route applies?
- What does the young person want, fear, or expect from referral?
- What information is necessary, and what consent or lawful basis supports sharing?
- Which language, communication, disability, transport, cost, and digital access arrangements are needed?
- Who owns follow-up if the referral is rejected, delayed, or lost?
- How will current support continue during transition?
- What feedback loop will confirm attendance, assessment, and plan?

## H3. Common Referral Pathways

**Primary care/medical:** unexplained physical symptoms, pain, sleep disorders, eating and growth concerns, medication questions, puberty concerns, chronic illness, injury, sexual health, substance effects.

**Child and adolescent mental health services:** moderate to severe mental health difficulties, diagnostic complexity, self-harm, trauma symptoms, eating disorders, psychosis, severe anxiety or depression, neurodevelopmental assessment pathways.

**Emergency/crisis services:** immediate risk to life, serious injury, acute intoxication, psychosis with danger, inability to maintain safety. Use local emergency numbers and procedures.

**Safeguarding/children's social care:** suspected abuse, neglect, exploitation, trafficking, domestic abuse, unsafe care arrangements, missing episodes, serious welfare concerns.

**Education services:** special educational needs, disability accommodations, attendance, exclusion prevention, educational psychology, alternative provision, speech and language, occupational therapy.

**Substance services:** harmful use, dependence, withdrawal risk, drug-related exploitation, family support.

**Sexual health and exploitation services:** contraception, STI testing, pregnancy options under local law, sexual assault care, online exploitation, healthy relationships.

**Housing and family support:** homelessness, eviction, young caring, benefits, food insecurity, domestic abuse support, respite.

**Youth and community services:** mentoring, sport, arts, culturally specific support, LGBTQ+ youth support, disability groups, bereavement support, young carer services.

Local names, thresholds, and contact details must be inserted by each service and reviewed regularly.

## H4. Warm Handover Script

“I want to explain why I am suggesting this referral and what will happen next. Your counselling here does not have to end abruptly unless that is the service rule, and I will tell you if it does. The specialist service can help with ______. I recommend it because ______. Alternatives include ______. The referral will say ______ and will not include ______, except for safety information I have explained. You can tell me if any wording is factually wrong. I will check on ______ date whether they received it. While we wait, our plan is ______. If things become unsafe, contact ______.”

Where the young person disagrees, document their view, assess capacity and risk, explain any duty to proceed, and preserve choices over timing, advocate, language, and information where possible.

## H5. Crisis Resource Card Template

Services should replace bracketed fields with verified local information and review the card at least annually.

**If there is immediate danger to life:** [local emergency number]

**24-hour crisis line:** [name, number, text/web options]

**Child protection/safeguarding:** [local route]

**Sexual assault support:** [service]

**Domestic abuse support:** [service]

**Substance emergency/poison information:** [service]

**LGBTQ+ youth support:** [service]

**Bereavement support:** [service]

**Housing emergency:** [service]

**My trusted adult:** ____________________ Phone: ____________________

**My counsellor/service and hours:** __________________________________

**My safety plan is located:** ________________________________________

A crisis card is useful only if the young person can access it privately and believes the services will respond. Check phone credit, shared-device privacy, language, disability access, and whether contacting a service could alert an unsafe person.

## H6. Documentation Standards

Documentation should be timely, factual, proportionate, and respectful. Separate observation, report, inference, decision, and action. Record consent, information sharing, risk formulation, and review dates. Avoid jargon and moral labels.

Useful records include:

- referral and consent;
- assessment and formulation;
- treatment plan;
- session summaries;
- risk and safety plans;
- parent, school, and inter-agency contacts;
- measures and reviews;
- incidents and safeguarding actions;
- endings, referrals, and closing summaries.

Do not record speculation as fact, copy old conclusions without review, or include unnecessary information about third parties. Remember that young people and families may later read records; respectful writing is both an ethical and relational practice.
