# The Evidence Base: Evaluating Counseling Theories

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain what "evidence-based" means in counseling, and describe how the term's meaning has changed since the 1990s.
- Describe the hierarchy of evidence and state where the various forms of counseling research sit within it.
- Explain the efficacy–effectiveness–implementation gap and illustrate each of the three stages.
- Describe the contribution and the limits of practice-based evidence.
- Critically appraise a systematic review or meta-analysis, including the questions of risk of bias, heterogeneity, publication bias, and ecological validity.
- Explain the specific problems that the counseling research literature presents for the evaluation of work with children and adolescents.
- State what a reasonably prudent practitioner should do with a body of evidence that is thin, contested, or internally inconsistent.
- Use measurement-based practice to make a defensible decision about continuing, modifying, or discontinuing a treatment.

## Chapter Overview

Every theory in this book makes a claim about what helps, and every student in this course will one day be asked by an employer, a regulator, a funder, or a family to explain why a particular intervention was chosen. The purpose of this chapter is to make that explanation possible, and to make it honest.

The chapter begins with the history of the evidence-based movement in counseling, because the term's meaning has shifted substantially and the older usage is still in circulation. It then sets out the hierarchy of evidence and locates the various forms of counseling research within it, and it explains the three-stage model of efficacy, effectiveness, and implementation that now organises the field's thinking about translation. It devotes considerable attention to the specific methodological problems of the counseling literature — the control condition, the outcome measure, the population, and the sampling — because a student who cannot identify these problems will over-trust the literature.

The second half of the chapter is practical. It provides an appraisal framework that can be applied to a systematic review in about forty minutes, a procedure for reading a meta-analysis, a worked case in which a treatment must be continued, modified, or discontinued on the evidence, and the ethical and cultural considerations that bear on the whole enterprise.

The central argument of the chapter is that the evidence base for counseling with young people is thinner, weaker, and more contested than the confident language of the field suggests, and that the correct response to that fact is not scepticism but method: careful appraisal, honest measurement, and a willingness to treat one's own practice as the place where the question is actually being answered.

## 1. The History of "Evidence-Based" in Counseling

### 1.1 The two meanings

The phrase "evidence-based practice" entered the counseling profession in the 1990s, imported from medicine, and it has been used with two distinct meanings that should not be confused.

**Evidence-based practice** means a practice in which the interventions used are those that have been shown, in controlled research, to be effective. This is the meaning most practitioners mean, and it is the meaning the profession's codes increasingly require. Under this meaning, the practitioner's obligation is to deliver interventions that have demonstrated support.

**Evidence-based practice** also, and in some literatures, means a practice in which the practitioner uses the best available research evidence *in conjunction with* clinical expertise and with the client's preferences and circumstances. This is the more demanding meaning, and it is the one the evidence-based practice frameworks in medicine and in the mental health field generally specify. Under this meaning, the research is a necessary input to a judgement, not the whole of the judgement.

The first meaning is tractable and administratively convenient. The second is the professional standard the codes intend, and it is the one that requires the framework of this chapter.

### 1.2 The empirically supported treatment movement

The movement for empirically supported treatments, which developed in the United States in the 1990s around the Division 12 of the American Psychological Association and the work of the Association for the Advancement of Behavioural and Cognitive Therapies, was a direct response to two problems. The first was that the field's research literature, while substantial, was not usable: a practitioner could not determine from the literature which interventions to offer, because the literature was large, heterogeneous, and inconsistently indexed. The second was that the field's status in the mental health marketplace was vulnerable, in a context in which third-party payers were increasingly requiring evidence of effectiveness.

The movement's central methodological contribution was the **empirically supported treatment criteria**: a treatment is a candidate for the empirically supported designation if it has been tested in at least two independent investigations, with a comparison group, with a specified population, with well-defined outcomes, and with a manual describing the treatment. A treatment that meets the criteria is a well-defined treatment; the designation does not assert superiority over other treatments, only that the treatment has been adequately studied.

The contribution is real and the movement should be credited with it. Its limitation is that the criteria are a claim about the state of the research, not a claim about the state of the practice, and the "empirically supported treatment" label came to be used, in both the profession and the payer community, as though it were the latter. A student who learns the criteria precisely will avoid a great deal of confusion.

### 1.3 The dissemination and implementation problem

The third phase of the evidence-based movement, from roughly 2010 onward, has focused on implementation: the recognition that a well-supported treatment is of little use if it does not reach the people who need it, and that the reach of evidence-based practice depends on organisational and system-level factors rather than on the persuasion of individual practitioners. This phase has been shaped by the recognition of the "learning health system", in which routine data collection and the routine comparison of outcomes are used to improve the service, and by the implementation science literature, which distinguishes between interventions that are effective in a trial and those that are effective in a routine service.

## 2. The Hierarchy of Evidence

The hierarchy of evidence, which descends from the most to the least internally valid:

1. **Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.**
2. **Randomised controlled trial.**
3. **Quasi-experimental study with a control group and pre-post measurement.**
4. **Single-case experimental design.**
5. **Controlled cohort or case-control study.**
6. **Uncontrolled outcome studies and case series.**
7. **Expert opinion, consensus, and anecdote.**

Two cautions are required about this hierarchy. The first is that it ranks designs by internal validity, not by usefulness. A well-designed single-case experimental design in a routine service with a young person whose problem is not represented in any trial will be more useful to that young person than a meta-analysis of trials conducted with a different population. The second is that the hierarchy is a hierarchy of *single* studies; a single randomised trial, however well conducted, sits below a body of well-conducted observational evidence about a question that trials cannot answer.

Counseling research occupies an unusual position within this hierarchy, and a student should be able to state where each of the following sits and what each can answer.

| Design | Contribution | Typical limitation in counseling |
|---|---|---|
| Meta-analysis of RCTs | Pooled estimate of effect with confidence interval; comparison across treatments | Publication bias; heterogeneity of interventions; mostly adult outpatient samples |
| RCT | Causal inference about a specific intervention for a specified population | Small samples; manualised delivery; research-clinic conditions |
| Pragmatic / cohort-randomised RCT | Effectiveness in routine services | Fewer published; contamination between arms; practitioner variability |
| Quasi-experimental | Real-world effectiveness where randomisation is impossible | Confounding; baseline differences |
| Single-case experimental design | Individual-level causal inference with immediate feedback | Weak generalisation; reactivity; limited to a single individual |
| Controlled observational study | Association between process and outcome in practice | Selection effects; measurement confounding |
| Uncontrolled outcome study | Description of change over time | No causal inference; expectation effects; regression to the mean |
| Qualitative study | Meaning, process, and the client's own account of change | Not generalisable; analytic subjectivity |
| Practice-based evidence | Real-world effectiveness across a caseload | Case mix confounding; inconsistent measurement |

## 3. The Efficacy–Effectiveness–Implementation Gap

### 3.1 Efficacy

An intervention is **efficacious** if it produces better outcomes than a comparison condition under ideal conditions: trained practitioners, high adherence to protocol, a selected population, a research team invested in the study, and no competing demands on the practitioners' time. The trials that established the evidence base for the cognitive-behavioural therapies were overwhelmingly of this kind.

Efficacy is a real and important achievement, and the treatments that have demonstrated it should be used. But efficacy tells us about a treatment, not about a service.

### 3.2 Effectiveness

An intervention is **effective** if it produces better outcomes than it would have otherwise in the routine conditions of ordinary services. The difference between efficacy and effectiveness is a set of pragmatic factors, and they are substantial:

- **Practitioner competence and experience** vary in services in ways they do not in trials.
- **Case mix** in a service is more heterogeneous than in a trial, and the average trial participant is more likely to respond.
- **Time pressure** in a service is greater, and the treatment is delivered in fewer sessions.
- **Comorbidity** in a service is more common.
- **Engagement** is lower in a service, and drop-out is higher.
- **Fidelity** is lower in a service, because the demands of the setting compete with the demands of the protocol.

The pragmatic trial literature, which is the most important methodological development in the field since 2010, is the source of the most sobering findings in the profession: that average outcomes in routine services are considerably worse than in trials, that the variability between providers within the same model and the same protocol is large, and that a substantial proportion of the variance is attributable to the therapist rather than to the model.

### 3.3 Implementation

**Implementation** concerns whether an effective intervention is delivered, with fidelity, to the people who need it, in the settings where they are. The implementation literature makes a series of distinctions that are directly useful to a student planning a placement or a first post: whether the intervention is *feasible* in the setting, whether it is *adoptable* by the staff who would deliver it, whether it is *usable* in the workflow, and whether the organisation has the leadership, the data infrastructure, and the supervision to support it.

The practical implication is that the best intervention in the literature is frequently not the best choice in a particular service, and that the question "what is the evidence for?" is only the first of three questions. The other two are "what can this service actually deliver?" and "what would it take to deliver it better?" A student who asks all three will be more useful in a placement than a student who knows only the first.

## 4. Practice-Based Evidence

**Practice-based evidence** is generated by the systematic collection of outcome data in routine services and the analysis of that data with quasi-experimental methods, typically comparing outcomes for a provider or a team against a benchmark.

Its advantages are substantial. It describes what actually happens in the service, with the real clients, under real conditions, including the clients who would never have been in a trial. It is sensitive to practitioner variation, which is where much of the real variance in outcome lives. It is sensitive to the effect of the practice on the practitioner, because the data are available at the level of the caseload. And it is continuous, so a service can detect a deterioration in its outcomes long before an external evaluation would.

Its limitations are equally real. The comparison is not randomised, so case mix differences between providers can confound the comparison. Data quality depends on the discipline of the staff in collecting it, and it is often poor. Sample sizes per provider are small, so the confidence intervals around a provider's estimate are wide. And the data reflect the practice that was actually delivered, which is a confound as well as a strength: a provider who delivered a well-evidenced treatment poorly will have poor outcomes, and the data cannot tell you whether the model or the delivery was the problem.

The most useful way to think about practice-based evidence is as a **compass rather than a map**. It tells you which way things are going and whether to keep going, and it does not tell you the terrain.

## 5. The Population Problem

The most serious methodological problem in the counseling research literature, for a book written for students who will work with children and adolescents, is the population problem.

The overwhelming majority of the outcome research has been conducted with adult, outpatient, non-suicidal, largely white samples in the United States, Western Europe, and a small number of other countries. The specific features of the adolescent literature are:

**Smaller studies.** Adolescent studies are typically small, and are therefore underpowered to detect the differences between treatments that the equivalence finding is based on.

**Fewer component and dismantling studies.** The body of evidence that establishes that specific techniques work for specific problems is dominated by adult studies, so the translation to young people is an extrapolation.

**Developmental confounding.** An intervention that has been shown to work with adults may work with young people for a different reason, or may fail, and the adolescent studies are generally not designed to distinguish these possibilities.

**The attrition problem.** Drop-out from therapy is substantially higher with adolescents than with adults, and drop-out is not random: the young people who leave are the young people whose problems are worst, whose engagement was lowest, or whose families were least able to support the work. Any analysis that excludes dropouts — which is the majority of published analyses — therefore systematically overstates outcome.

**The family confound.** Many adult studies are conducted with individuals, and many adolescent studies are conducted with families or in school settings. The settings are not comparable, and the outcomes are not comparable, because the mechanisms differ.

**The consent confound.** Research with young people requires the cooperation of parents and, in some jurisdictions, of the school. The samples are consequently skewed toward families who are engaged enough to consent, which is a systematically different population from the one the practitioner sees.

**The assessment confound.** The instruments used to measure adolescent symptoms were largely developed and normed on adult or on older youth samples, and the developmental appropriateness of a self-report measure for a nine-year-old is a substantive methodological question rather than a technicality.

The correct conclusion is not that the adolescent evidence is worthless. It is that the adolescent evidence base is **narrower than the adult evidence base, and that the field's habit of transferring adult findings to adolescents without comment is a form of unacknowledged extrapolation that a professional should make explicit.**

## 6. The Measurement Problem

The choice of outcome measure in a study is a consequential methodological decision, and the field's standard practice has a systematic bias.

**Symptom scales** such as the depression, anxiety, and conduct inventories used in most trials are reliable, comparable across studies, and resistant to expectancy bias in the client. They are also poor at capturing what matters most to the young person and to the family, and they are not the outcome that the client will care about. Their use systematically favours the common factors position, because a change in the client's own appraisal of their life is a change that specific techniques produce more reliably than the standard scales detect.

**Functional measures** — school attendance, educational achievement, the absence of disruptive behaviour, the presence of a peer relationship, the resumption of ordinary activities — are closer to what the client and the family want, and they are more often improved by specific techniques. They are, however, more vulnerable to the influence of factors outside the therapy, and they are harder to attribute.

**The client's own rating** of progress, captured by instruments such as the Client Outcome Questionnaire and the Session Rating Scale, is the measure most sensitive to the difference between therapies, and it is the measure that the profession's own humanistic tradition has always insisted upon. The practice of collecting it routinely has a second-order benefit, discussed in Chapter 53: it makes the young person's own appraisal visible to the practitioner.

**Observer-rated measures** — parent, teacher, and clinician ratings — are useful precisely because they are not the client's own account, and their disagreement with the client's own account is itself clinically informative. A young person who reports large improvement and a teacher who reports no change presents a formulation question, not a measurement problem.

## 7. The Control Condition Problem

This problem was introduced in Chapter 6 and is repeated here because it is the single most important methodological point for a student to carry into the literature. A trial that compares a specific treatment with a no-treatment or wait-list control cannot separate the specific technique from the common elements, and therefore cannot estimate the specific technique's contribution. A trial that compares the treatment with a credible control that retains the relationship, the attention, and the expectation is the design that can.

The practical consequence for the student is that when reading a claim of the form "treatment X is superior to treatment Y", the question to ask is what the comparison condition was. If it was no treatment, the claim is about the package rather than the technique. If it was a credible alternative treatment, the claim is a genuine head-to-head comparison and is a much more valuable finding. And if it was a credible control condition designed to isolate the technique, the claim is the strongest available and should be read with corresponding care.

## 8. A Critical-Appraisal Framework

The following framework can be applied to a systematic review or meta-analysis in about forty minutes and is sufficient for the critical appraisal strand of this course.

### 8.1 The review question

What exactly was asked? A well-constructed review has a focused, answerable question that specifies the population, the intervention, the comparison, and the outcomes. A review with a vague question will have a heterogeneous answer, and heterogeneity in a meta-analysis is not a finding about the world but a finding about the review.

### 8.2 Search and selection

How was the literature searched, how was it screened, and how many studies were included from how many identified? The flow of studies through the review is the single most informative thing in it, and a flow diagram should be read before the results.

### 8.3 Risk of bias

What did the included studies do well and badly? The principal domains are randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding of assessors, attrition, selective reporting, and, in the psychotherapy literature, fidelity to the protocol. The question to ask is not whether the studies were "good" but whether the flaws are likely to inflate the effect.

The two bias issues of greatest concern are **attrition bias**, in which the dropouts are systematically different from the completers, and **selective reporting**, in which only the outcomes that reached significance are published.

### 8.4 Heterogeneity

Do the studies agree? A meta-analysis that pools studies with a statistical test of homogeneity and finds them heterogeneous should not be reporting a single pooled estimate without exploring the sources of the difference. A very high I² statistic means that the pooled number is an average of things that are not the same, and the clinically useful information is in the strata, not in the average.

### 8.5 Publication bias

How many studies are missing? The standard diagnostics — the funnel plot, the fail-safe N, and the trim-and-fill adjustment — are crude, and the field's estimates of the proportion of unpublished studies in psychotherapy research run high. A meta-analysis with a small number of studies should be read with particular caution, since the diagnostics are least reliable in that case.

### 8.6 Effect size and clinical importance

Is the effect large enough to matter? An effect expressed as a standardised mean difference of 0.2 is small, and its clinical importance depends entirely on the context — on the natural course of the problem, on the severity at baseline, and on the consequences of not treating. The field's tendency to report statistical significance without reporting clinical significance is a serious weakness.

### 8.7 Ecological validity

Would this evidence apply to my case? This is the most important question and the one most often skipped. Ask it explicitly: was the sample young? Were the participants similar to the young person I am seeing? Was the setting similar to my setting? Was the therapist similar to me? Was the treatment manualised and delivered with high fidelity? Would my young person have had the same access to the treatment, the same family support, and the same freedom from competing demands?

### 8.8 Transparency

Are the search strategy, the inclusion criteria, the extraction forms, and the analytic decisions reported in sufficient detail that the review could be replicated? A review in a high-impact journal with no search strategy and no protocol is a narrative review with a pooled number attached.

## 9. The Application of Evidence to an Individual Case

A student who has learned the framework above will still face the situation in which the evidence for the young person in front of them is thin, contested, or absent. The following principles govern that situation.

**Treat the evidence as a prior, not a verdict.** The literature establishes base rates and reasonable approaches; it does not establish what will work for this young person. The clinician's task is to combine the evidence with the case, and the combination must be stated explicitly in the formulation so that it can be reviewed.

**Match the strength of the claim to the strength of the evidence.** If the evidence is weak, the plan should be correspondingly conservative, more measurable, and more frequently reviewed. Weak evidence justifies more frequent monitoring, not more confident assertion.

**Measure and review.** A treatment that is being used in the absence of strong evidence for the particular young person should be monitored systematically from the outset, with a pre-specified review point, and the young person should be told that this is what is happening and why. This is honest, and it is more respectful of the young person's autonomy than a confident assertion unsupported by any evidence.

**Prefer the reversible.** Where several approaches have comparable or absent evidence, prefer the approach whose effects can be observed and adjusted within a short period.

**Be explicit about the uncertainty in the plan.** "We are going to try X because it is the best-supported approach for this kind of difficulty, we will know in four weeks whether it is helping, and if it is not we will change something" is a more professional statement than a confident claim that X will work.
