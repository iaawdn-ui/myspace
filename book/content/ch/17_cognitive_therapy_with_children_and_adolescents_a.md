# Cognitive Therapy with Children and Adolescents

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why the cognitive model as developed with adults cannot simply be applied to a young person, and identify the specific developmental obstacles.
- Describe the developmental modifications required to the thought record and state the rationale for each.
- Design developmentally appropriate behavioural experiments for a child, an early adolescent, and a late adolescent.
- Work effectively with parents of young people receiving cognitive therapy, and describe the specific pitfalls.
- Describe the adaptations of cognitive therapy for the treatment of depression, anxiety, and OCD in young people.
- Apply the cognitive model to a developmental formulation, and identify where a developmental or systemic account is required.
- Evaluate the evidence for developmentally adapted cognitive therapy with young people.
- Identify the specific cultural and ethical issues raised by the developmental adaptation of the cognitive model.

## Chapter Overview

The cognitive therapy developed by Beck was for adult outpatients, and the adaptations required for children and adolescents are not cosmetic. A fourteen-year-old cannot reliably produce abstract propositional thoughts; a nine-year-old cannot reliably introspect into an automatic thought; a seventeen-year-old who has learned to distrust adults' suggestions will comply with the collaborative empiricism while privately rejecting it. A student who applies the adult thought record to a young person will produce a session in which the young person writes down a false statement about a moment that never happened, and the practitioner will believe it is a gain.

This chapter works through the developmental problem in detail and then develops the modifications: of the thought record, of the behavioural experiment, of the parent's role, of the work with the young person's developmental task, and of the relationship between the cognitive work and the body. It also examines the three conditions in which cognitive-behavioural treatments have been most extensively adapted and evaluated — depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder — and it is candid about the limits of the adaptation in each.

The central argument of the chapter is that the developmental modification of a therapy is not a dilution of it. The cognitive model contains a discipline — the collaborative examination of propositions — that is more important than the specific format of the thought record, and the adaptation consists in finding formats that preserve the discipline while fitting the young person's capacity. A practitioner who can do that is doing cognitive therapy with a young person; a practitioner who cannot is doing something else, and should know which.

## 1. The Developmental Problem

### 1.1 The obstacles

Four specific developmental obstacles stand between the adult cognitive model and a young person.

**The obstacle of propositional thought.** Cognitive therapy's central procedure requires the young person to (a) identify a situation, (b) identify the thoughts that occurred in it, (c) state them in a form that can be evaluated, (d) generate alternatives, and (e) assess the alternatives against evidence. The first two steps are within the capacity of most young people from about eight years old. The third requires propositional thought, which is a late-developing capacity and one that is materially affected by arousal, fatigue, and mood. The fourth requires the ability to generate a divergent response to a problem, which is one of the more demanding of the executive functions and one of the most sensitive to the young person's belief that the problem is hopeless — which is to say, the very belief the treatment is addressing. The fifth requires the ability to hold an evaluative stance toward one's own thinking, which is precisely what is impaired in the conditions the treatment targets.

This is the fundamental paradox of cognitive therapy with young people, and the adaptation must address it directly: the very capacities the technique requires are the capacities the condition has impaired.

**The obstacle of introspection.** Automatic thoughts are by definition not noticed before the emotion. A young person at the concrete operational stage who is asked to report what they were thinking in a situation three days ago will construct an answer rather than recall one, and the construction will be a plausible account assembled from the available social narratives about what people think in such situations. This is not deception; it is the developmental limits of retrospective introspection.

**The obstacle of the doubting mind.** A young person who has been told, by parents, by teachers, and by other adults, that their thoughts are "not true" or "not real" or "just a phase" has learned a lesson about the reliability of their own cognition that will interfere directly with the collaborative empiricism. The lesson is not irrational; it has usually been well-founded.

**The obstacle of the power differential.** The collaborative empiricism presupposes a symmetry of attention between the practitioner and the young person. A young person who is in the care of adults, or who is under school discipline, or who has been referred by a parent who is present, cannot assume that the examination of propositions is a joint activity. They will, often without awareness, provide the answers that will keep them safe.

### 1.2 The developmental solution

Each of these obstacles has a corresponding adaptation, and the adaptations are the substance of this chapter.

**Developing the capacity rather than requiring it.** The developmental solution to the obstacle of propositional thought is to treat the capacity as something to be taught within the treatment, which is what the developmental adaptations do: they teach the young person to notice, to label, to evaluate, and to generate, in a sequence, over the course of the sessions.

**Using behavioural and imaginal material rather than verbal material.** The developmental solution to the obstacle of introspection is to work with material that does not require retrospective verbal recall. A young person can be asked what they were feeling and what was happening in their body in a specific recent situation, and can often produce concrete detail about it, even when they cannot produce a thought record.

**Establishing the collaborative stance through action.** The developmental solution to the doubting mind is to demonstrate the joint examination rather than to assert it: to conduct behavioural experiments in which the young person predicts, the test is run, and the outcome is observed by the young person.

**Working with the parent as a co-investigator.** The developmental solution to the power differential is to make the parent a participant in the collaborative examination rather than an observer of it, in the specific ways set out in Section 4.

## 2. The Developmental Model of Cognition

### 2.1 Vygotsky and the zone of proximal development

The most useful theoretical tool for adapting cognitive therapy to a young person is the zone of proximal development. A cognitive task that is too difficult produces helplessness and withdrawal; a task that is too easy produces boredom. The task must be pitched so that the young person can complete it with the support of the practitioner, and the support must then be withdrawn progressively.

The developmental adaptations of cognitive therapy are, in effect, a systematic application of this principle: the young person is not asked to produce a full thought record alone but is helped to produce the components in sequence, with the practitioner modelling the process at first and then fading the support.

### 2.2 Piaget and the operational stages

The concrete operational stage is the stage in which the young person can think logically about concrete material, can take another's perspective, and can conserve. The formal operational stage brings the capacity for abstract and hypothetical reasoning. The developmental adaptations of cognitive therapy map onto these stages with reasonable accuracy, and the mapping is a useful guide for the practitioner.

For the **concrete operational child (approximately 7–11)**, the cognitive work is conducted with concrete situations, with drawings and images, with behavioural experiments that are simple and physical, and with the immediate and the observable. The core belief work is done not as a proposition but as a *picture* and a *feeling*, and the language of the work is "the rule that goes with the picture" rather than "the belief."

For the **early adolescent (approximately 11–13)**, the cognitive work can begin to use propositions, but the propositions must be about concrete and recent situations, and the young person's own words rather than the practitioner's must do the work. The behavioural experiments can be more elaborate and can involve social situations.

For the **middle and late adolescent (approximately 13–18)**, the formal operations are available and can be used, but they are fragile in the presence of strong affect, and the practitioner should plan to do the cognitive work when the young person is not in the middle of the difficulty. This is the point at which the "pre-agreed coping plan for the moment of difficulty" ceases to be a supplement and becomes a precondition.

### 2.3 The development of self-representation

The developmental literature on the self provides the account of why some of the presentations in youth services are so difficult to treat with a cognitive model, and the account is worth understanding.

**The concrete operational child** has a self that is largely constituted by observable actions and by the judgments of others. The child is "good" if others say so, and the cognitive work is about the difference between what the child has done and what others have said, and the child can often make this distinction more readily than an adult expects.

**The early adolescent** has a self that is becoming explicitly a *possible* self, and the adolescent's central developmental work is the reconciliation of the actual self with the possible self. The discrepancy between the two is the source of a great deal of adolescent anxiety and depression, and it is not a "cognitive distortion" in the sense of an inaccurate appraisal; it is an accurate description of a developmental gap. A cognitive therapist who treats it as a distortion is treating a developmental fact as an error.

**The mid-to-late adolescent** is developing a more abstract, more integrated, and more self-authored self-concept, and the developmental work is the integration of the contradictory elements. The developmental literature on this period describes it as a process that is considerably more turbulent than the popular account of the "storm and stress" suggests, and it is a period in which a great many young people consult mental health services.

## 3. The Developmental Thought Record

### 3.1 The five-column version

The standard thought record has seven columns, and the developmental version reduces and simplifies them. The typical developmental thought record for an adolescent has four or five columns.

**What happened?** A concrete, sensory description of the situation, in the young person's own words, and often with a drawing or a diagram.

**What went through your mind?** The automatic thought, in the young person's words, and with the option of a drawing of the thought. The instruction is deliberately simple and the practitioner accepts the answer without requiring elaboration at first.

**How did you feel?** The emotion, named from a visual or written list, with a rating and, if the young person is willing, the location in the body. The visual list of emotions is a substantial practical improvement on the adult version and is easily produced.

**What is the evidence for and against?** For younger children, this is often a concrete question about what happened and what the young person saw, and the "against" column is frequently completed by the practitioner as a collaborative activity, which is itself a demonstration of the collaborative stance.

**What could you have said instead?** For younger children, this is often expressed as an alternative *action* rather than an alternative thought: "What else could you have done?" The action formulation is developmentally easier and is a bridge to the behavioural experiment.

### 3.2 The "said-to" adaptation

A well-established developmental adaptation of the thought record for children and younger adolescents is the **"said-to" version**, in which the young person is asked to complete a set of sentence stems about what they *said* rather than what they thought: "My friend said that..." "My teacher said that..." "I said to myself that..." The "I said to myself" column then becomes the working automatic-thought column.

The adaptation is effective because it moves the young person from a metacognitive task they cannot perform to a recall task they can, and because the speech attribution gives the thought a location in the world that makes it available for examination. A young person who has written down what their teacher said can then examine the belief, because it is now on the page.

### 3.3 The "worst, best, most likely" adaptation

For the behavioural experiment with a young person, the "worst, best, and most likely" prediction scale is a useful developmental adaptation of the standard technique. It provides a structure, it is concrete, and it produces the crucial insight — that the young person's own estimate of the likely outcome is much less catastrophic than their estimate of the worst — without the practitioner having to argue for it.

### 3.4 The cautions

**Do not let the thought record become a test.** The most common error with young people is the drift into a performance of the technique, in which the young person produces increasingly sophisticated entries and learns nothing. The test for this is whether the young person's affect has changed in the session. If it has not, the practitioner has been collecting data rather than working.

**Do not require written work from a child who cannot do it.** A nine-year-old's thought record can be a drawing, a comic strip, a voice recording played back by the practitioner, or a conversation the practitioner records afterwards. The discipline is in the examination of the evidence, not in the format.

**Do not let the record become a compliance exercise.** A young person who has learned that adults want a particular answer will produce it. The practitioner who does not notice is doing therapy on a collaboration rather than on a young person.

## 4. Working with Parents

### 4.1 The case for parental involvement

The developmental and outcome literatures support parental involvement in the treatment of young people's anxiety, and there is a substantial body of evidence that parent involvement in the treatment of adolescent depression improves outcomes and reduces relapse. The reasons are developmental: the parent is a more powerful agent of change in the young person's environment than the therapist is; the parent's own beliefs and responses maintain the young person's difficulties; and the work is a developmental task in which the parent has a developmental role.

### 4.2 The specific cognitive work with parents

The cognitive work with parents is not the same as the cognitive work with the young person, and a student should be able to distinguish four elements.

**The parent's own beliefs about the young person's symptoms.** Parents of young people with anxiety and depression frequently hold beliefs about the cause of the difficulty — that it is "attention-seeking," that it is "a phase," that "it will pass," that it is caused by screens or by a friendship group — and these beliefs are often themselves the subject of a cognitive analysis. The work is to examine the parent's beliefs with the same collaborative empiricism, and to do it without implying that the parent's beliefs are foolish.

**The parent's responses.** The parent's responses to the young person's anxiety or distress are the proximate maintenance factors in many cases: reassurance, avoidance of the feared situation, allowing the young person to avoid school, and the accommodation of the symptom. The cognitive therapist does not tell the parent to stop accommodating; the therapist helps the parent examine what they believe the accommodation is for, which is usually love and usually a reasonable response to the young person's distress.

**The behavioural experiments for the parent.** The parent's own predictions — about what will happen if the young person goes to school, about what will happen if they stop reassuring — can be tested in the same way as the young person's, and this is often a very effective intervention with a parent who is sceptical about the whole enterprise.

**The parent's own difficulties.** Parents of young people with serious difficulties are frequently depressed, anxious, or in unresolved conflict with each other, and the therapeutic work with the parent may need to be the more substantial of the two.

### 4.3 The specific pitfalls

**The triangulation.** The most serious pitfall in parent-inclusive work is the covert alliance between the practitioner and the parent against the young person. A practitioner who works with the mother and then interprets the young person's difficulty to the mother in the young person's presence has enacted the very dynamic that the young person came to escape. The requirement is that the practitioner's work with the parent and the young person be kept structurally separate, and that the young person be told what will be shared.

**The parent who is the problem.** The developmental literature on the family is clear that in a substantial minority of cases the parent's own difficulties, the family's emotional patterns, or the parent's unresolved history are the maintaining factors. A cognitive therapist who works only with the young person's appraisals is doing something in these cases, but is not doing the whole thing.

**The parent who will not engage.** A parent who does not attend, or who attends and is not able to change anything, is a common situation. The requirement is that the practitioner be explicit with the young person about what is and is not possible, and that the young person not be blamed for the parent's absence.

**The young person who does not want the parent involved.** A young person who is seventeen and who has told the practitioner that the session is confidential will not want the parent in the room, and the developmental and ethical requirement is that the young person's preference is respected, subject to the safeguarding and the legal requirements, and that the practitioner explain to the parent what the work involves without disclosing the content.

## 5. The Three Best-Evidenced Adaptations

### 5.1 Depression in young people

The developmentally adapted cognitive therapy for depression in young people is among the most extensively evaluated psychological treatments in the world, with multiple large randomised controlled trials in the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand.

The modifications are: the inclusion of a substantial behavioural activation component, including the scheduling of activities to reverse the withdrawal and the anhedonia; the involvement of parents, in a graduated fashion; the use of a simplified and more concrete cognitive model, with the "evidence for and against" work often done collaboratively and with the young person; the attention to the social and academic consequences of the depression, which are frequently more modifiable than the cognitive content; and the explicit framing of the treatment as a skill to be learned and used in the future, which is developmentally consonant with the adolescent's interest in competence.

The clinical caution is that the behavioural activation component in adolescents must be paced carefully: the adolescent's activity schedule is a developmental task, and the research on the efficacy of activity scheduling with young people indicates that pushing too hard produces a further drop in mood and a further withdrawal.

### 5.2 Anxiety in young people

The adaptations here are the most thoroughly developed, because the anxiety disorders are the conditions in which the cognitive-behavioural approach has been most consistently successful with young people, and because the disorders have the clearest and most reliably replicated treatment components.

The modifications are: the use of a graded exposure hierarchy, with the young person and the practitioner constructing it collaboratively, and with the exposure being concrete and behaviourally defined; the use of modelling, with the practitioner or a peer demonstrating the feared activity; the reduction of parental accommodation, with a graded and collaborative approach; the use of the young person's own hierarchy rather than a standard one; and the use of reward systems for completed exposures, which is developmentally appropriate and which Chapter 28 develops in the school context.

The psychological model in these treatments has been developed specifically to be developmental: the developmental model of anxiety proposed by the developmental psychopathology researchers, in which the child's fear is a rational response to a real lack of control in a specific domain, and in which the treatment is the development of the skills of control and the reduction of the parental response to the fear. Chapter 46 develops this.

### 5.3 Obsessive-compulsive disorder in young people

The adaptation of exposure and response prevention to children and adolescents is a further major development, and it is a demanding one, because the treatment involves a young person deliberately approaching what terrifies them.

The modifications are: the caregiver's role in the exposure hierarchy and in the support for the response prevention; the developmental preparation and the psychoeducation for the young person; the specific attention to family accommodation; the attention to the young person's developmental understanding of the obsessions; and the modification of the response prevention for the young person's developmental stage, including the involvement of a parent in refraining from accommodating.

## 6. Emotion, the Body, and the Developmental Model

Two limitations of the adult cognitive model become particularly consequential in work with young people, and a student should be able to name them.

**The thinness of the emotional account.** The adult cognitive model treats emotion as a response that follows from appraisal, and its techniques are directed at the appraisal. The developmental and clinical literatures on young people suggest that for many young people the most accessible and most useful level of intervention is the emotional one, and that a young person who is asked to appraise before they can feel will not manage the appraisal. The modifications that respond to this are: the beginning of the emotional work before the cognitive work in the session; the explicit teaching of the emotion vocabulary with a visual list; the attention to the bodily location of the feeling as a route into the cognitive work; and the use of the young person's own metaphors.

**The absence of the body.** The cognitive model has, historically, had little to say about physiological arousal, and a great many of the young people who present with panic or with somatic symptoms have a body that is a substantial part of the problem. The modifications are: the explicit inclusion of interoceptive awareness work; the practice of grounding; the attention to sleep; the physiological components of the treatment for panic; and the integration of the somatic work of Part VI.

The modern convergence between the cognitive and somatic traditions is a direct response to this weakness, and a student who is familiar with the somatic material in Part VI will be able to improve their cognitive work with young people considerably.
