## 7. Case Illustration: The Girl Who Could Not Say No

**The referral.** Halima, fifteen, is referred by her school for "anxiety and possible eating disorder." The school reports that Halima has lost a significant amount of weight over the past four months, that she is skipping lunch, that she is "constantly busy" with a very demanding extracurricular programme, that she is "top of the set in everything", and that she has not been eating with her family for months. Halima's mother, who brought her to the appointment, says that Halima "has always been high-achieving" and that "we are a family that does things properly", and that she is "very proud of her."

Halima is polite, articulate, and warm. She answers every question fully. She does not appear distressed. She has lost, by her own account, "no" to nothing in particular — she has simply stopped noticing the requests.

**The parts mapping.** The practitioner asks Halima to describe the internal experience of her week, and then asks a question that is simple and, for this young person, disorienting: "Who is doing all of that for you?" Halima's face changes, and after a pause she says: "I don't know what you mean." The practitioner asks: "When your mother says she's proud of you, who is pleased?" Halima says, in a slightly different voice: "She is. She is proud. That's what matters." And then, in a third voice: "But it doesn't matter, does it? That's the problem."

**The map.** The map emerges over three sessions and is drawn by Halima herself, in a notebook, at the practitioner's suggestion.

*One part is the one who is organised.* She describes this part as the one who knows what has to be done, who gets the forms completed, who gets to school on time, and who gets the grades. She says: "If I don't do it, nobody does it. My mother works, my dad is in Leeds half the week, and Abdi does not do anything." She calls it "the manager" after the practitioner explains the model.

*Another part is the one who cannot say no.* She describes it as "the one who gets asked" and she says: "It just comes out. My cousin asks me to help with her coursework and I say yes before I have finished the sentence." She calls this "the yes part."

*A third part is the one who is hungry.* Halima draws this part as very small, and in a corner, and she says little about it. The practitioner does not press.

*And there is a part that is very quiet.* Halima says, "There's one that doesn't say anything. I don't know. It's just there." The practitioner asks what it does. Halima says: "It's the one that knows."

**The formulation.** The map shows a system in which an Exile — the small, hungry, hidden part — is being protected by two protectors with quite different strategies. The Manager part organises Halima's life so tightly that the Exile is never triggered: no time is available for a hungry feeling, because the schedule is full and the achievements are constant. The "yes" part, the Firefighter, acts on the Exile's affect: it accepts the requests, the commitments, the demands, so that the Exile's distress never has to be felt directly. And the quiet part that "knows" is the young woman's own capacity that has been crowded out of the schedule, which the practitioners describe as her Self in its developmental form.

The developmental reading is crucial and the practitioner works it into the formulation explicitly: this is a fifteen-year-old who is doing a caregiving and a self-regulation job that belongs to an adult, in a household where the adults are themselves stretched, and where "we are a family that does things properly" is a Parent injunction that has been transmitted intact. Halima's weight loss is the solution to the conflict among the parts, and it is working: it is the only part of Halima's experience that is not being managed.

**The intervention.** The practitioner does not go to the Exile, and the practitioner is explicit with Halima about why: "I want to meet the part that is hungry, and I am not going to do that today, or probably next week, because the part that is organising your life is doing a very good job of keeping that part safe, and if I go there too quickly we will both be frightened."

The work proceeds at the level of the protectors. The practitioner asks Halima to spend time noticing the organising part, to notice when it is in charge, and to notice what it protects. And the practitioner asks about the "yes" part: "What happens if you say no? What does the yes part think will happen?" The answer is revealing: "It thinks everything falls apart. It thinks my dad won't come back from Leeds. It thinks my mum will have to give up the job."

**The family work.** The intervention that makes the difference is the family work, and it is the intervention the individual model would not have prompted. The practitioner asks Halima's mother to come to a session, and asks Halima first. The session is not about blame and it is not about the eating. The practitioner asks the mother: "What do you need Halima to be?" The mother is quiet, and then says: "I need her to be the one who holds it all together. I am not sure I could hold it if she didn't." And the practitioner asks Halima: "Can you hear that? What do you do with that?" And Halima says: "That is what the hungry part is about. If I am not the one who holds it all together, then who does?"

This is the moment at which the Exile becomes reachable, and the practitioner says so and does not push: "That is the part we have been looking for. We will come to it. Not yet."

**The trajectory.** The work over the following months includes: the mother engaging with what she needs her daughter to be; Halima's "yes" part being examined, with the gradual capacity to decline one request; the Manager part being examined, with the first small and deliberate inefficiency; and the eventual work with the Exile, in which the young woman is able to say what the hunger is for — the eating, the weight loss, the shrinking, the wish to be small enough to be needed by a family that is also in trouble. The outcome is not a resolution of the family system. The outcome is that Halima gains some weight, that the "yes" part is less active, and that the quiet part that knows is more present in the sessions.

**The ethical dimension.** Three requirements were engaged. The first is the requirement of medical assessment, which the practitioner insisted on at the outset: a fifteen-year-old with significant weight loss requires medical evaluation, and the therapist's framework cannot substitute for it. The second is the requirement of parental consent and of the young person's assent, and the practitioner was careful to work with Halima's mother in a way that did not covertly triangulate the mother against the daughter. The third is the requirement to protect the young person from the "both sides" technique, in which the practitioner invites the young person to differentiate from the parent's position in a way that puts the young person at risk of the parent's subsequent anger. The practitioner slowed the family work and did not proceed with the differentiation until the mother's own capacity to tolerate the differentiation had been assessed.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The outcome evidence

The evidence base for internal family systems therapy has grown substantially over the past fifteen years, and the quality of it has improved.

**The early evidence.** A number of uncontrolled outcome studies and case series from the 1990s reported favourable outcomes, and the small number of controlled studies in the 1990s and early 2000s were generally positive but small.

**The more substantial contemporary evidence.** A series of larger studies, several of them randomised controlled trials, have been conducted across a range of populations and conditions, and the results are among the more encouraging of the recent integrative therapy literature. The conditions and populations studied have included depression, anxiety and related disorders, eating disorders, substance use, chronic pain, and adolescent presentations including self-harm and eating pathology. A systematic review of the therapy in adults found a substantial and growing body of randomised controlled trial evidence with effect sizes comparable to those of established treatments, and the developmental and adolescent literature has grown, though more slowly.

**The online evidence.** The body of randomised controlled trials of online internal family systems therapy is among the largest in the field for any integrative therapy, and it includes a series of large randomised trials with good methodological quality. The findings are consistent with the effectiveness of the model and, importantly for this course, with the acceptability of the model for populations who would not otherwise present for therapy.

**The limitations.** The methodological quality of the studies is variable, and a substantial number are conducted by investigators with an allegiance to the model, which is a legitimate concern in a literature of this size. The long-term follow-up data are limited. The evidence for the family and school applications, which are the most relevant to this course, is much thinner than the evidence for the individual adult application. And the evidence for the psychosis application, which is clinically important, rests on a small number of studies.

### 8.2 What the evidence supports

That internal family systems therapy is an effective treatment for a range of conditions in adults, with effect sizes in the range typically considered moderate to large. That the model can be delivered remotely with comparable outcomes. That the model's premise — that the symptom is the solution of an internal conflict — is a workable and clinically useful frame, as evidenced by the consistency of the outcomes across the range of conditions studied. And that the six-step process is deliverable by trained practitioners with fidelity.

### 8.3 What the evidence does not support

That internal family systems therapy is superior to other treatments for any condition; the comparative evidence does not show this. That the parts model is a metaphysically accurate description of the mind. That the model can be delivered safely to young people with a trauma history without the extensive preparation the structural dissociation literature requires. And that the model can be delivered in a brief or unsupervised form; the evidence base is for the full model with trained practitioners.

### 8.4 The status of the "Self"

The Self is a clinical construct in the model and the published outcome literature measures the process of therapy and the outcome, not the Self as an entity. The Self's status in the theory's non-clinical formulation is a matter of continuing and unresolvable internal disagreement within the movement, between those who regard the Self as a spiritual or transpersonal entity and those who treat it as a secular, developmental, and relational capacity. A student should be aware of the disagreement and should adopt the secular formulation in clinical work, in which the Self is described through the 8 Cs and is understood as a developmental and relational capacity, and should not present the transpersonal formulation to a client as a scientific claim.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Internal Family Systems Therapy

**Strengths.** It offers an account of the symptom as a solution, which is both non-blaming and immediately actionable, and which is very often the first thing a young person needs to hear. The externalisation of the internal provides a structure that a young person can see and work with, and it is compatible with drawing, with play, and with the visual mapping that has become the standard approach with adolescents. It is non-linear and non-pathologising without being vague, and it generates concrete technical steps that a practitioner can learn. It has one of the better bodies of contemporary evidence among the integrative therapies, and it has been unusually well adapted to online delivery. The Exile work is directly informed by the structural dissociation literature, which makes the model considerably safer than it would otherwise be with trauma-exposed young people. And the model's insistence on the practitioner's own position and on noticing when the practitioner has become caught up in a part is a genuinely valuable clinical discipline.

**Limitations.** The model's subtlety is a real barrier: the vocabulary is easy to misuse, and a practitioner who uses the language of parts without the discipline will produce a shallow and often irritating performance. The model can be experienced by a young person as a sophisticated adult game. The family and school applications are poorly studied. The evidence base is heavily weighted to adult populations and to individual work. And the metaphysical dimension of the theory in its original form makes it difficult to use in a professional context in which the practitioner may not hold the metaphysical view, which is a practical problem that the secular model addresses but does not eliminate.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**Cultural considerations.** The model's account of the internal world is, like most of the field's, an account of an individual mind. Its applicability to young people whose psychological difficulty is substantially located in relationship, in family obligation, in community, or in structural conditions is not well developed, and the family's role as a system that includes the young person's parts is a complicated matter in cultures in which the family is the young person's primary context of selfhood. The framework's own developmental account — in which the young person's parts and the parent's parts are two halves of one system — is an interesting contribution here, and it deserves more application than it has received.

**The ethical question of the Exile work.** The model's highest-risk intervention is the work with the Exile, and the ethical requirements are exact: adequate stabilisation; a young person who can consent meaningfully to a procedure they understand; a practitioner competent in the work and in the management of intense affect; support in place for the aftermath; and a plan for what happens if the young person becomes destabilised. The structural dissociation literature is explicit that approaching the emotional part of a dissociated system without adequate stabilisation is harmful, and the ethical requirement stands independently of any model's claims.

**The ethical question of the parts language.** A young person who has a religious or spiritual framework may hear the parts language in ways the practitioner does not intend, and the ethical requirement is to ask the young person how they understand the model and to use their framework where it is helpful. A young person from a family in which mental illness has been attributed to possession or to spiritual influence may find the parts model a relief or may find it an insult, and the practitioner cannot know which without asking.

**The ethical advantage of the model.** The model also has a substantial ethical advantage that should be recorded. For a young person who has been told that they are attention-seeking, manipulative, or defective, the model offers a description in which every part of their experience has a function, and in which the behaviour that has been most condemned is the behaviour that is most clearly a protector. That is not a small thing, and it is one of the strongest reasons for a student to know this model.

**The ethical requirement of the practitioner's self-monitoring.** The model's demand that the practitioner monitor their own state for blending is an ethical requirement, not a technical preference, and it is particularly important with young people, who are acutely sensitive to an adult's consistency and whose experience of the practitioner will determine whether the Exile work can be undertaken at all.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Internal family systems (IFS)** — A therapy developed by Richard Schwartz in which the mind is understood as a natural multiplicity of parts in relationship, with the symptom understood as the solution of a conflict among them.
- **Structural dissociation** — The theory, developed from Janet's work, in which overwhelming trauma produces a division of the personality into relatively autonomous action systems, one of which is fixated in the trauma.
- **Apparently normal part** — In structural dissociation theory, the part of a person that continues to function in the ordinary world and has no access to the traumatic material.
- **Emotional part** — In structural dissociation theory, the part fixated in the trauma and dominated by the traumatic affect.
- **Part** — A discrete aspect of a person's experience with its own perspective, emotional style, agenda, and history; a subpersonality with a legitimate adaptive function.
- **The Self** — The core level of experience in the IFS model, from which the practitioner works; described in the secular formulation through the 8 Cs.
- **The 8 Cs** — Curious, calm, compassionate, confident, connected, courageous, clear, committed; the descriptive qualities of the Self.
- **Blending** — The condition in which a part's perspective runs the show and the person is *in* a part rather than having one.
- **Unblending** — The development of the capacity to experience a part's feelings and thoughts while also experiencing oneself as the one who has them; the model's core technical idea.
- **Exile** — A part carrying the young person's wounds — the hurt, fear, shame, and injustice of earlier experiences — and often the youngest part.
- **Manager** — A proactive protector part whose strategies prevent the Exiles from being triggered; planning, controlling, achieving, suppressing, withdrawing.
- **Firefighter** — A reactive protector part whose strategies extinguish the Exile's activation; dissociation, bingeing, drinking, raging, self-harming.
- **Outsider** — A part that maintains the appearance of things and guards the system against the intensity of the work; includes scepticism and the part that wants to quit.
- **Symptom as solution** — The model's central clinical claim, that the symptom resolves the conflict among the parts and works, at a developmental cost.
- **Six-step process** — Mapping, connecting with Self, understanding protectors, healing the Exile, retrieving, and reworking.
- **Retrieving** — The step in which the Exile gives back the burden it was given and is reoriented towards its original caretakers.
- **Reworking** — The final step in which the parts are reorganised so that the old strategies are no longer required.
- **Visual mapping** — The use of drawing to represent the system of parts; an important development in the child and adolescent applications.
- **IFS in schools** — A brief, educational adaptation of the model for use with young people in school settings.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Internal family systems therapy was developed by Richard Schwartz in the 1980s from two sources: his clinical observation of conflicted patients with eating disorders and substance abuse, and the theory of structural dissociation, which is itself derived from Pierre Janet's nineteenth-century account of how trauma divides the personality.

The model describes three levels of experience: the unblended Self, the parts, and the blending of a part's perspective into the person's everyday state. The five categories of part — Exiles, Managers, Firefighters, Self, and Outsiders — each have a distinct function, and the account of the symptom as the solution of a conflict among the parts is the model's most clinically powerful proposition.

The six-step process moves from mapping, through connecting with the Self and understanding the protectors, to healing the Exile, retrieving, and reworking. The concept of unblending gives the young person a choice they did not have in the moment, and the demand that the practitioner monitor their own state for blending is one of the theory's most valuable clinical disciplines.

The developmental fit with young people is good, and the model is uniquely compatible with the visual mapping that adolescents can work with, with the non-blaming account of the symptom, and with the developmental reality of a self that is under construction. The evidence base has grown substantially, with the strongest material in adult individual work and in the online delivery, and the family and school applications remain thinly evidenced.

The ethical requirements are strict and specific, particularly for the Exile work, where the structural dissociation literature's requirements for stabilisation are the ethical floor. The model's substantial ethical advantage is that for a young person who has been called defective or manipulative, it offers a description in which every part of their experience has a function.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 15.1 — Parts Mapping with a Peer (90 minutes, triads)

One student works on a real or imagined difficulty; one guides the mapping using the questions in this chapter; one observes. The observer's task is to note the moment at which the student uses the parts language fluently and the moment at which the language becomes a way of avoiding contact with the affect. The exercise builds the mapping skill and the self-monitoring skill simultaneously, which is appropriate, since the model requires them together.

### Activity 15.2 — The Manager/Firefighter Analysis (75 minutes, individual, written)

Take a young person you know and write a paragraph for the Manager and a paragraph for the Firefighter, using their own language where you can. Then write the paragraph for the Exile, and note how much you are willing to write about it. The disproportionate reluctance in the third paragraph is data about the young person and about you, and both are worth bringing to the seminar.

### Activity 15.3 — The Unblending Exercise (60 minutes, pairs)

One student reads aloud a short passage describing an internal conflict; the other repeatedly asks: "Who is saying that?" and "What is that part protecting?" The practitioner is required to keep asking until the student can name the parts, describe their functions, and step back from them. The point of the exercise is the moment of unblending, which is genuinely moving to experience and is worth understanding from the inside before attempting to facilitate it for someone else.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model.** Read Schwartz (1995) and then the clinical guide, and read Brehm's workbook alongside them, because the workbook is the most usable account of the process with a young person.

**For the underlying trauma theory.** Read the structural dissociation material, and then read Janet's original work in a modern edition. The theory is more intelligible after Janet than before, and a student who has read Janet will find the contemporary somatic and trauma literature much easier.

**For the evidence.** Read the systematic reviews of the therapy in adults and the large online trials, and read the developmental and school-based studies with attention to their sample sizes and their developmental adaptations rather than to their conclusions.

**For the ethical questions.** Read the trauma literature on the dangers of premature trauma processing with young people, and the literature on the model's use in schools and in families. The school and family applications are where the ethical requirements are most easily breached, because the settings pressure for rapid symptom change.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is schwarz's model of parts and the treatment of partitioned experience in traumatized youth. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is awareness, emotional experiencing, relational presence, agency, and the young person's own meanings. The mechanism to keep in view is how safety, attention, expression, and relational responsiveness allow experience to become available for reflection and choice. The characteristic professional risk is pressuring disclosure, treating emotional intensity as progress, or neglecting risk, family context, and practical action. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in individual sessions where trust, voice, and self-understanding are central, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Priya is fifteen and is seen in a school counselling service for shame after academic failure. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Priya's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Priya explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on awareness, emotional experiencing, relational presence, agency, and the young person's own meanings.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Priya chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Priya has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Priya can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
