# Motivational Interviewing

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain ambivalence and why direct persuasion often strengthens resistance.
- Describe the spirit of motivational interviewing: partnership, acceptance, compassion, and empowerment.
- Use open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summaries with adolescents.
- Elicit and strengthen change talk while softening sustain talk without arguing.
- Describe engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning processes.
- Apply motivational interviewing to school attendance, substance use, health behaviour, family conflict, and treatment engagement.
- Recognise ethical limits, especially with mandated and minor clients.

## Chapter Overview

Motivational interviewing (MI), developed by William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick, is a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation and commitment to change. Its central insight is that ambivalence is normal. An adolescent may want to stop vaping because breathing and sport matter, and want to continue because vaping regulates stress and maintains belonging. A student may want qualifications and also want to avoid classrooms where failure feels public. A parent may want a calmer home and fear that reducing control means losing authority.

When practitioners argue for change, ambivalent people often argue back—not because they are irrational, but because conversation assigns positions. If the counsellor takes the “you must change” side, the young person may defend freedom, pleasure, identity, or scepticism. MI reverses the arrangement: the practitioner evokes the young person's own reasons, values, hopes, and confidence, then supports autonomy in deciding what to do.

## 1. Development and Core Assumptions

MI emerged from addiction treatment in the 1980s, where Miller noticed that counsellor empathy predicted outcomes and that confrontational approaches often increased resistance. With Rollnick, the approach expanded into health care, corrections, education, parenting, and psychotherapy integration.

Four assumptions guide practice:

1. **Motivation is interpersonal.** It rises or falls within conversation.
2. **Ambivalence is not denial.** Mixed feelings contain useful information.
3. **People persuade themselves.** Statements spoken by the client—desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment—carry particular force.
4. **Autonomy is essential.** Change imposed without ownership is fragile and may be ethically unacceptable.

MI is not trickery to make a young person choose the adult's preferred outcome while feeling that it was their idea. That would violate its spirit. Transparency matters: the counsellor may state a concern, provide information with permission, and still respect the person's decision.

## 2. The Spirit of MI

Contemporary descriptions emphasise **partnership, acceptance, compassion, and empowerment**.

Partnership means working with, not on, the young person. Acceptance includes absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, and affirmation. Compassion places the young person's welfare above institutional convenience. Empowerment recognises existing strengths and resources rather than treating the client as empty until professionally filled.

The opposite spirit appears when the practitioner interrogates, labels, threatens, shames, or rushes to fix. Miller and Rollnick call the premature urge to solve the **fixing reflex**. With adolescents, the fixing reflex is amplified by adult responsibility: safeguarding, school targets, parental anxiety, and court or service mandates. MI does not remove those responsibilities; it changes how they are carried.

## 3. Change Talk and Sustain Talk

**Change talk** expresses movement toward change. Preparatory forms include desire (“I want”), ability (“I could”), reasons (“It would help”), and need (“I have to”). Mobilising forms include commitment (“I will”), activation (“I'm ready”), and taking steps (“I already tried”).

**Sustain talk** expresses movement away from change: pleasure, identity, fear, mistrust, practical barriers, loyalty to peers, or doubt. Sustain talk should not be crushed. It should be understood, reflected, and sometimes strategically explored so the young person can hear their own fuller position.

![Change talk and sustain talk as two sides of ambivalence, with counsellor reflections influencing which side becomes more elaborated.](fig69_change_sustain_talk.png)

A useful rule is that the more a young person argues for the status quo in session, the less likely change becomes. The counsellor therefore avoids supplying arguments that force the client into defence. Reflections often do more than questions because they allow the client to hear and revise their own meaning.

## 4. Core Skills: OARS

**Open questions** invite elaboration: “What concerns you most about how things are going?”

**Affirmations** recognise strengths and effort specifically: “You kept coming to appointments even though you expected to be lectured.”

**Reflective listening** forms the engine. Simple reflections repeat or slightly rephrase. Complex reflections add feeling, meaning, values, or the unstated side of ambivalence. A parent says, “He just doesn't care.” A reflection might be: “It's frightening to imagine he doesn't care, because then you feel powerless to reach him.” The aim is hypothesis, offered tentatively, not mind reading.

**Summaries** gather bouquets of change talk and offer them back: “You want your lungs back for football, you're tired of hiding devices from your mum, and you think weekdays might be easier than weekends. Did I miss anything?”

Advice is not forbidden. It is offered using elicit–provide–elicit: ask what the person knows and wants to know; offer information neutrally and with permission where required; elicit their interpretation and next step.

## 5. The Four Processes

**Engaging** establishes a working relationship. With adolescents, engagement includes explaining confidentiality, asking what would make sessions useful or unbearable, and noticing power differences.

**Focusing** identifies a direction. Several concerns may compete: attendance, cannabis, sleep, conflict, self-harm. Agenda mapping allows choice without pretending all can be solved at once.

**Evoking** draws out the young person's motivations, values, discrepancy, hopes, and confidence. This is the distinctive heart of MI.

**Planning** begins when motivation and confidence are sufficient. The practitioner supports a specific, chosen, realistic plan, anticipates barriers, and strengthens commitment without stealing ownership.

The processes are recursive. If planning produces resistance, return to evoking. If focus is unclear, return to engaging. Movement is not failure; it is information.
