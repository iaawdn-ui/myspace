
## 6. Session Plans for Selected Units

### Session A: Functional assessment

**Outcomes.** Students can operationalise a behaviour, generate at least three functional hypotheses, and identify data that would discriminate among them.

**Preparation.** Read Chapters 24 and 26; bring an anonymised ABC observation from practice or use the provided fictional record.

**Activities.** Ten-minute concept review; lecturer models faulty versus testable hypotheses; groups analyse a school incident log; each group designs one observation and one intervention; plenary compares ethical safeguards.

**Assessment.** Exit ticket requiring one operational definition and one disconfirming observation.

### Session B: Family systems

**Outcomes.** Students can draw a structural map, identify triangles, and separate structural from interactional formulation.

**Preparation.** Read Chapters 30–33.

**Activities.** Case reading; small-group mapping; lecturer-led comparison of Bowenian, structural, and strategic plans; practice a family opening statement; critique cultural assumptions.

**Assessment.** Short formative map submitted for feedback, not grading of artistic quality.

### Session C: Trauma-informed practice

**Outcomes.** Students can identify state, phase, safety requirements, and inappropriate timing of trauma processing.

**Preparation.** Read Chapters 38 and 40.

**Activities.** State-recognition exercise using fictional transcripts; phase placement; grounding demonstration with opt-out; design a school environment audit; discuss the difference between trauma-informed care and trauma-specific treatment.

**Assessment.** Written phase justification with criteria for moving forward and backward.

### Session D: Integration capstone

**Outcomes.** Students can produce a coherent, sequenced, culturally responsive plan and defend omissions as well as inclusions.

**Activities.** Complex case distributed in stages; groups revise formulation as new information arrives; cross-group peer review; lecturer models supervision questions.

**Assessment.** Draft capstone formulation for formative feedback before final submission.

## 7. Role Play and Demonstration Ethics

Role play is a laboratory, not group therapy. The lecturer must:

- use fictional cases by default;
- obtain consent for roles and recording;
- permit observer or writer roles;
- set stop signals;
- prohibit use of classmates' real trauma;
- debrief role occupants out of character;
- give feedback on observable skills, not personality;
- provide alternatives for students for whom a scenario is personally activating.

Demonstrations should be interrupted for teaching points rather than performed as virtuoso displays. If a demonstration fails, analyse it. Students learn more from repair than perfection.

## 8. Inclusive Teaching

Accessibility begins before requests. Provide slides and readings in accessible formats, caption videos, describe diagrams, allow assistive technology, avoid colour-only meaning, and state assessment criteria plainly. Offer multiple ways to participate: speech, writing, anonymous response, small group, and asynchronous forum.

Language support includes glossaries, pre-reading questions, and permission to discuss concepts in a stronger language before presenting in academic English where institutional rules permit. International students should not be treated as deficits when their experience exposes the cultural limits of a theory.

Attendance flexibility must balance professional formation with disability, caring, work, health, and transport realities. Participation grades based solely on speaking frequency are inequitable.

## 9. Difficult Classroom Moments

Students may disclose personal trauma, challenge a theory on cultural grounds, defend a prohibited practice, disclose risk, or become distressed. The lecturer should acknowledge, protect privacy, avoid turning disclosure into teaching spectacle, and follow safeguarding routes.

Disagreement is valuable. Distinguish critique of ideas from attacks on people. Ask speakers to identify evidence and implications. Where discussion involves identities represented in the room, do not appoint any student as spokesperson for a group.

Assessment integrity requires clear policy on collaboration, generative AI, plagiarism, and use of client material. Students must anonymise practice examples and never submit identifiable client information.

## 10. Evaluating Teaching

Evaluation should combine student feedback, peer observation, assessment patterns, and lecturer reflection. If many students confuse two concepts, teaching may need restructuring rather than students simply being told to study harder.

Track whether case examples remain diverse, whether skills practice produces competence, whether feedback is timely, and whether assessment tasks align with outcomes. Revise one or two elements at a time so changes can be understood.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Constructive alignment** — Coherence among outcomes, learning activities, and assessment.
- **Formative assessment** — Assessment used to guide learning before final judgement.
- **Retrieval practice** — Actively recalling prior learning to strengthen understanding.
- **Skills laboratory** — Structured practice with fictional material, observation, feedback, and debriefing.
- **Inclusive teaching** — Design that anticipates varied access, language, disability, and participation needs.
- **Capstone formulation** — A culminating case conceptualisation integrating theory, development, culture, evidence, and planning.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Teaching CAC. 701 is itself an exercise in formulation. The lecturer identifies what students need to notice, designs experiences that make theory usable, observes misunderstanding without shame, and revises. A successful course does not produce loyalty to one school. It produces practitioners who can explain their choices, recognise limits, and keep the young person—not the theory—at the centre.
