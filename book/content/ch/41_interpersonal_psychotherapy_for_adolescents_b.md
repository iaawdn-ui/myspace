
## 6. Interpersonal Psychotherapy with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 Why the adolescent adaptation works

The adolescent adaptation works because depression in adolescence is seldom only an internal event. It is lived in bedrooms, classrooms, group chats, changing rooms, family meals, examinations, friendship ruptures, first romantic attachments, and the painful comparison between the young person's actual life and the curated lives of peers. A treatment that asks who matters, what has changed, what is disputed, and what the young person needs from the most important relationship speaks directly to the adolescent's world.

Four adaptations distinguish the adolescent model from the adult model.

**The developmental assessment is more extensive.** Puberty, school transitions, examinations, cognitive development, peer hierarchy, identity formation, digital communication, autonomy, and family dependence must all be assessed. Two young people of the same chronological age may be at very different developmental levels.

**The family is structurally involved.** The adolescent usually still lives at home, depends on adults for transport, money, permission, food, and school decisions, and cannot simply implement an interpersonal plan alone. Parents or carers are therefore brought into psychoeducation, safety planning, and selected joint sessions, while preserving a confidential individual space for the young person.

**The school is an interpersonal system.** Teachers, tutors, attendance officers, coaches, school nurses, and safeguarding leads may all hold pieces of the problem. A dispute that appears to be between mother and daughter may be maintained by a timetable, a seating plan, a public sanction system, or an unrecognised learning difficulty.

**The language is concrete.** Abstract words such as abandonment, transition, and ambivalence are translated into observable exchanges: who texted, who replied, who walked away, who raised their voice, who apologised, who was left out, and what happened next.

### 6.2 Assessment in practice

A good IPT-A assessment usually takes two or three sessions and produces four documents.

First, a **symptom and risk formulation**: diagnosis where appropriate, severity, duration, sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, anhedonia, irritability, self-harm, suicidal thinking, substance use, medical factors, medication, and protective factors.

Second, an **interpersonal inventory**: the important people, rated for closeness, conflict, reliability, support, disappointment, and change.

Third, a **problem-area formulation**: grief, dispute, role transition, or interpersonal deficit, stated in one paragraph and agreed with the young person.

Fourth, a **treatment contract**: number and frequency of sessions, who will be involved, confidentiality limits, safety arrangements, measures, and review dates.

The young person should leave assessment knowing what will happen next. Uncertainty is itself a burden for a depressed adolescent, who may already experience time as slow, effort as pointless, and help as unlikely to arrive.

### 6.3 The four problem areas with adolescents

**Grief.** Adolescent grief is often disenfranchised. The young person may be expected to return to school quickly, may not have been included in rituals, may grieve a grandparent while adults describe the death as expected, or may grieve a parent while also feeling anger, relief, guilt, or disloyalty. IPT-A distinguishes grief for a person from grief for a former self, home, school, friendship group, or imagined future.

**Role disputes.** These are the commonest presentations. A parent wants obedience and safety; the adolescent wants autonomy and trust. A teacher wants compliance; the student wants dignity and escape from humiliation. Two friends want loyalty; a third wants inclusion. The task is not to decide who is right, but to specify the non-reciprocal expectations and negotiate a tolerable next step.

**Role transitions.** Starting secondary school, moving schools, changing friendship groups, parents separating, a new sibling, migration, leaving care, examination failure, injury ending sport, pregnancy, coming out, and the transition from child to adult services are all role transitions. The old role is mourned, the new role is explored, and supports for the new role are built.

**Interpersonal deficits.** Some adolescents are chronically isolated, socially anxious, repeatedly rejected, or unable to read interpersonal cues. The work is slower and more skills-based: initiating contact, joining a group, tolerating silence, repairing after embarrassment, ending exploitative relationships, and building a small number of sustainable relationships rather than pursuing popularity.

### 6.4 Techniques translated into adolescent practice

**Communication analysis** breaks a painful exchange into a chain: trigger, interpretation, feeling, words, tone, response, consequence. A mother says, “You haven't started,” meaning concern about revision; the daughter hears, “You are lazy”; she feels shame; she replies, “Leave me alone”; the mother hears rejection; she threatens to remove the phone; the daughter withdraws; both feel unheard. The analysis makes the sequence visible without turning either person into the villain.

**Decision analysis** lists options, advantages, disadvantages, likely responses, and the young person's values. It is especially useful where every option feels intolerable.

**Role play** is used sparingly and consensually. The young person rehearses asking a teacher for an extension, telling a friend that a joke went too far, or informing a parent that constant location tracking feels like distrust. Rehearsal must never become a performance for the therapist's satisfaction.

**Affect work** links feeling to interpersonal event. The question is not merely “How depressed are you?” but “When your friend left you on read for six hours, what happened inside you, what did you conclude, and what did you do next?”

**Clarification** tests vague statements. “Everyone hates me” becomes who, when, what was said, what was inferred, and what alternative explanations exist. Unlike cognitive disputation, the goal is not to prove the thought false; it is to recover the interpersonal facts.

### 6.5 Working with parents and carers

Parents should not become co-patients by default, nor should they be excluded as obstacles. A useful structure is:

1. A parents-only psychoeducation meeting near the beginning.
2. Individual sessions with the adolescent as the main treatment space.
3. One or two planned joint sessions where a specific communication is rehearsed and attempted.
4. A termination meeting that reviews warning signs and relapse prevention.

The practitioner explains that adolescent confidentiality has limits where safety is concerned, but that ordinary disclosures about friends, sexuality, embarrassment, anger at parents, and ambivalence about treatment will not be relayed verbatim. If a parent demands a transcript, the alliance will usually collapse.

### 6.6 IPT-A in schools

Schools are natural settings for IPT-A because the interpersonal problem often occurs there and because attendance provides routine monitoring. School delivery requires clear boundaries: the counsellor is not a disciplinary officer, session content is not used for behaviour management, and outcome information shared with staff is limited to what the young person has agreed, except where safeguarding overrides confidentiality.

A school-based formulation might focus on a dispute with a teacher, grief after a friend moves away, role transition after exclusion, or social isolation following bullying. The counsellor must be alert to institutional causes. If a young person is repeatedly humiliated by public behaviour charts, private shaming, racist discipline patterns, or inaccessible work, interpersonal therapy must not be used to teach adaptation to an unjust environment. Consultation and advocacy may be required alongside therapy.

## 7. Case Illustration: The Girl Who Stopped Answering

**Presentation.** Amara is fifteen. Her grades have fallen, she sleeps until midday at weekends, and she has stopped replying to messages from her closest friend. Her mother describes her as “withdrawn and oppositional”. Her father, from whom her mother separated eight months ago, describes her as “fine when she's with me”, which increases the mother's sense of failure.

**Assessment.** Amara's most important person is her friend Sofia, not either parent. Four months earlier, Sofia began spending most breaks with a new group. Amara interpreted delayed replies as proof that she had been replaced. She stopped initiating contact to avoid further rejection, then felt guilty for being distant, then became angry when Sofia accused her of “being weird”. The primary problem area is a role dispute intertwined with a role transition: the friendship has changed, and Amara has no script for renegotiating it.

A secondary dispute exists at home. Her mother checks her phone location repeatedly after Amara stayed out late once. Amara experiences checking as surveillance; her mother experiences Amara's silence as danger. Each protects herself in a way that frightens the other.

**Formulation shared with Amara.** “Your depression worsened when the friendship changed. You protected yourself by going quiet, but the quiet made you feel more cut off and more certain that Sofia didn't care. At home, the more worried your mum becomes, the more she checks; the more she checks, the less you tell her. The treatment will focus first on the friendship dispute, then on a specific conversation with your mum about safety and privacy.” Amara agrees, but asks that her mother not be told details about Sofia.

**Middle phase.** Communication analysis reconstructs one exchange in painful detail. Sofia wrote “can't today sorry”. Amara concluded, “She's done with me.” She felt shame, waited three days, and replied with a joke that sounded sarcastic. Sofia replied, “why are you being like this?” Amara left the message unopened for a week.

The therapeutic work does not declare Amara's interpretation irrational. It asks what else “can't today” might mean, what Amara wanted Sofia to know, and what a direct communication would risk and make possible. Amara identifies the wish beneath the sarcasm: “I miss how it was, but I don't want to beg.”

A script is drafted, shortened, and rehearsed:

> “I know things have changed and I don't expect everything to be the same. I miss talking to you. When I go quiet, it's because I think I'm bothering you, not because I don't care. Can we just have lunch on Thursday and see how it is?”

Amara sends a shorter version by text rather than speaking it, which the therapist accepts. Negotiating the medium is part of adolescent practice; insisting on face-to-face purity would have privileged technique over use.

Sofia replies warmly but not effusively. They meet once, awkwardly. The friendship does not return to its previous intensity, but it becomes honest. Amara grieves the old exclusivity while forming a second connection through a lunchtime art group. Depression improves not because the original relationship is restored, but because the interpersonal world becomes larger than one threatened bond.

**Family session.** A joint session addresses location checking. The therapist prevents a general trial about trust. The specific request is negotiated: location sharing during journeys to and from a Saturday activity, no continuous checking at home, a text on arrival, and review after three weeks. Amara agrees to answer a safety text within thirty minutes or explain later why she could not. Her mother agrees not to use location data to interrogate ordinary movements. The agreement is written down.

**Termination.** Amara's mood has improved, sleep is more regular, and attendance is restored. The relapse plan identifies interpersonal warning signs: not replying for more than two days, cancelling all social contact, interpreting silence catastrophically, and escalating phone conflict. She writes her own plan in her own words: “Tell one person early, even if it's just: I'm going quiet because I'm embarrassed, not because I don't care.”

**Interpretation.** The case shows IPT-A's discipline. The therapist did not excavate Amara's childhood, challenge every negative thought, or instruct the mother to be less anxious. The treatment selected a focal interpersonal problem, reconstructed events in sequence, clarified feelings and wishes, rehearsed a real communication, reviewed its actual outcome, and expanded the social field. The depressive symptoms improved as the interpersonal impasse loosened.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

Interpersonal psychotherapy has a strong adult evidence base for major depression and is recommended in several clinical guidelines. The adolescent adaptation has been evaluated in school-based and clinic-based randomised trials, including studies by Mufson and colleagues, showing greater reduction in depressive symptoms and better social functioning than usual care or supportive comparison conditions in several samples.

The Treatment for Adolescents with Depression Study placed youth depression treatments in a large comparative context, although IPT-A was not simply interchangeable with the specific interventions tested there and should not be misdescribed. Later trials and systematic reviews support IPT-A as a credible first-line psychosocial treatment for adolescent depression, particularly where the presentation is closely linked to grief, disputes, transitions, or social deficits.

Evidence is strongest for mild to moderate adolescent depression and for delivery over approximately twelve to sixteen sessions by trained practitioners with supervision. Evidence is weaker for severe depression with acute suicidality, psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe trauma symptoms, eating disorders, substance dependence, or neurodevelopmental conditions requiring substantial adaptation. IPT-A can be part of care in such cases, but rarely the whole of care.

Outcome measurement should include depressive symptoms, functioning, school attendance, interpersonal functioning, self-harm risk, and the young person's own goals. Symptom improvement without restored participation may be insufficient; conversely, a modest symptom change with return to school, friendship, and family life may be clinically meaningful.

Limitations include variability in manual fidelity, dependence on verbal reflection, cultural assumptions about direct communication, and under-representation of some ethnic, linguistic, disabled, care-experienced, and LGBTQ+ populations in trials. Practitioners should therefore treat the model as evidence-informed, not evidence-proof.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations

### Strengths

**Developmental relevance.** Adolescent depression is often organised around belonging, autonomy, peer status, family conflict, and transitions.

**Clarity.** The focal problem area gives treatment a map and prevents aimless exploration.

**Non-blaming language.** A dispute is not a defective personality; a transition is not weakness; grief is not pathology merely because it hurts.

**Practical skills.** Communication analysis, rehearsal, and decision analysis create actions the young person can test.

**Family and school compatibility.** The model can coordinate individual, parental, and school work without collapsing confidentiality.

### Limitations

**It may understate internal processes.** Rumination, trauma memory, obsessive-compulsive mechanisms, psychosis, neurodevelopmental differences, and biological illness may require other interventions.

**Direct communication is not universally safe or culturally congruent.** In some families, direct challenge to an elder is disrespectful or dangerous. Indirect communication, mediation, written messages, or involving a trusted relative may be better.

**It can individualise institutional harm.** Bullying, racism, poverty, inaccessible schooling, and unsafe housing cannot be resolved by asking the young person to communicate more skilfully.

**It depends on a focal problem.** Diffuse distress, severe avoidance, dissociation, or inability to identify relationships may require stabilisation or another approach first.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

Cultures differ in whether feelings are named directly, whether family decisions are individual or collective, how grief is expressed, what privacy from parents means, and how conflict with authority is handled. The practitioner must ask rather than assume: Who should be involved? What may be said directly? What would shame or endanger the young person? What forms of help are trusted?

Ethically, the practitioner must protect confidentiality while meeting safeguarding duties; include parents without surrendering the adolescent's voice; avoid becoming an agent of school discipline; and monitor risk throughout rather than assuming that a relational formulation explains everything. Consent and assent should be revisited when parents join sessions, when school staff are consulted, or when digital communication is reviewed.

Particular care is needed with LGBTQ+ adolescents. A dispute with parents may involve rejection of identity, concealment for safety, or conflict over names, pronouns, clothing, friendships, or faith-community participation. The therapist must not frame acceptance of the young person's humanity as a negotiable communication problem, nor disclose identity information without consent except where a lawful safeguarding duty requires action. Safety planning and affirming support take precedence over generic dispute resolution.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT)** — A time-limited treatment that links mood symptoms to grief, disputes, role transitions, and interpersonal deficits.
- **IPT-A** — The adolescent adaptation of interpersonal psychotherapy, incorporating developmental assessment, family involvement, and school context.
- **Interpersonal inventory** — A structured review of important relationships and the difficulties within them.
- **Role dispute** — A relationship impasse involving non-reciprocal expectations.
- **Role transition** — Distress associated with moving from an old social role to a new one.
- **Interpersonal deficit** — A persistent difficulty forming or maintaining satisfying relationships.
- **Communication analysis** — Detailed reconstruction of an interpersonal exchange to identify triggers, interpretations, feelings, responses, and consequences.
- **Decision analysis** — Structured comparison of options, consequences, values, and likely interpersonal responses.
- **Affect–event link** — The connection between a change in mood and a specific relational event.
- **Focal problem area** — The interpersonal domain selected as the primary target of treatment.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Interpersonal psychotherapy offers adolescents a focused, humane, and practical treatment that connects depression to the relationships in which it is lived. Its four problem areas—grief, disputes, transitions, and interpersonal deficits—translate complex suffering into workable questions. Who has been lost? What expectation is contested? What role has changed? What relationship capacity needs building?

The model does not ask the young person merely to think differently or behave differently. It asks what happened between people, what was meant, what was felt, what was wanted, and what could be tried next. For many adolescents, that is the exact level at which change becomes possible.

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model.** Read Klerman and Weissman for the adult foundations, then Mufson's adolescent adaptation. The movement from adult IPT to IPT-A shows how a treatment changes when dependence, school, family authority, and peer belonging are placed at the centre.

**For evidence.** Read the school-based IPT-A trials and systematic reviews of adolescent depression treatments. Pay attention to comparison conditions: “usual care” in a well-resourced school is not the same intervention everywhere.

**For practice.** Study communication analysis transcripts. The skill lies less in naming a problem area than in slowing an exchange down until feeling, interpretation, and wish can be distinguished.

**For cultural adaptation.** Read work on family hierarchy, indirect communication, migration, faith communities, and collectivist decision-making. Then ask how each IPT-A technique would change without losing its interpersonal focus.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is klerman's model, the social problem-solving curriculum, and the treatment of adolescent depression. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work. The mechanism to keep in view is how threat, arousal, avoidance, meaning, and relational safety shape what a young person can feel, remember, and learn. The characteristic professional risk is moving into traumatic material before stabilisation, confusing calm with shutdown, or making disclosure the price of care. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in stabilisation sessions, trauma-informed schools and services, caregiver work, and specialist treatment pathways, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Sofia is fifteen and is seen in a family support service for repeated classroom escalation. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Sofia's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Sofia explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Sofia chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Sofia has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Sofia can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.
