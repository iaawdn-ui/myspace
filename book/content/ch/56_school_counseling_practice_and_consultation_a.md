# School Counseling Practice and Consultation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the school counsellor's roles in direct counselling, consultation, prevention, advocacy, coordination, and systems change.
- Distinguish counselling from discipline, attendance enforcement, and behaviour management.
- Build ethical confidentiality practices within a school.
- Consult with teachers and families using collaborative problem-solving.
- Develop tiered supports within a multi-tiered system.
- Respond to crises, safeguarding concerns, and school-wide trauma.
- Evaluate school counselling outcomes and equity.

## Chapter Overview

Schools are where adolescents spend much of their social lives, encounter evaluation and belonging, form peer relationships, and become visible to adults outside the family. School counselling therefore cannot be only a quiet room and a waiting list. It includes brief individual work, groups, classroom and whole-school prevention, consultation with teachers, family collaboration, crisis response, referral, advocacy, and examination of the systems that produce distress.

## 1. The Distinctive School Context

School counsellors work inside the institution that may be both resource and stressor. A counsellor can help a student cope with examination pressure while also questioning public ranking practices. They can support behaviour plans while resisting the use of counselling as punishment. They can share functional information with teachers while protecting confidential disclosures.

This dual position requires role clarity. At the beginning of work, explain:

- what counselling is and is not;
- who can refer and why;
- what remains confidential;
- what must be shared for safety;
- what summaries teachers may receive;
- how counselling differs from discipline;
- how students can request or decline help, subject to local policy and safeguarding.

Without clarity, students may experience counselling as surveillance and teachers may expect reports the counsellor cannot ethically provide.

## 2. Tiered Support

A multi-tiered system organises support by intensity.

**Tier 1: universal.** Safe climate, belonging, social-emotional learning, anti-bullying systems, mental health literacy, staff training, accessible routines, and student voice.

**Tier 2: targeted.** Small groups, mentoring, brief interventions, attendance support, family check-ins, and skills programmes for students with emerging needs.

**Tier 3: intensive.** Individual counselling, coordinated care, specialist referral, safety planning, and personalised school adjustments.

Tiering should be a route to help, not a queue that requires failure at each level before support increases. Screening and teacher referral must be monitored for bias: quiet internalising students, disabled students, racialised students, LGBTQ+ students, carers, and students whose families distrust services may be identified differently.

## 3. Direct Counselling in Schools

School counselling is often brief, interrupted by timetables, examinations, staff absence, and holidays. Practitioners need focused assessment, clear goals, session-by-session review, and planned endings between terms.

Confidentiality in corridors matters. Appointment slips, room location, teacher messages, and being collected from class can disclose service use. Systems should allow discreet scheduling.

Evidence-informed school work may draw on CBT, behavioural activation, motivational interviewing, solution-focused practice, IPT-A, trauma-informed stabilisation, family consultation, and group interventions. Choice depends on formulation and competence, not simply available time.

## 4. Consultation

Consultation is indirect service: the counsellor helps a teacher, parent, or team change the environment or interaction around a student. It is not giving orders from superior expertise.

A collaborative consultation sequence includes:

1. establish purpose and confidentiality;
2. define the concern in observable terms;
3. gather context and previous responses;
4. consider student perspective and function;
5. generate options;
6. choose small feasible actions;
7. assign responsibilities;
8. review data and revise.

Teacher consultation must respect workload. A plan requiring six adults to perform ten steps perfectly will fail. One change in instruction delivery, seating, feedback privacy, or break access may be more effective.

## 5. Family Collaboration

Families know routines, history, culture, strengths, and barriers invisible at school. Collaboration begins with listening rather than reporting failures. Interpreters, flexible meeting times, remote options, and accessible documents are equity practices.

Where family and school accounts conflict, the counsellor avoids becoming a messenger of blame. Joint meetings need purpose, preparation, student voice, and agreement about what will be recorded.
