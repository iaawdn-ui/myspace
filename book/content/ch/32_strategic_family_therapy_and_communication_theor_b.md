
## 6. Strategic Work with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The fit

The strategic model fits the adolescent work of this book for reasons that are partly clinical and partly opportunistic.

**It takes the symptom seriously as a solution.** The adolescent's symptom has a function, and the model does not treat the function as a rationalisation. This is congenial with the adolescent's developmental reality: a young person who is failing to attend school, who cannot say no, or who has stopped speaking is usually doing something that has worked.

**It uses the young person's own motivation.** The strategic school's first question is what the young person wants. The "problem is a solution" formulation means that the young person has a goal, and the goal is legitimate, and the work is directed at finding a route to it that does not require the symptom. This is an unusually respectful starting point and it fits the developmental task of establishing an autonomous position.

**It is fast and it is directive.** Strategic work with an adolescent is often brief, and it is often more effective with adolescents than exploratory work, because the directiveness of the approach is experienced as respect rather than as intrusion. Many young people who are wary of therapy respond better to a practitioner who says "I am going to tell you what to do this week" than to one who asks open questions.

**It maps something that can be changed.** The problem-maintaining sequence is a description of the present, and the present is the only thing on which the intervention can act.

### 6.2 The specific adaptations

**Sequence the complaints.** Before the first family session, ask the young person to list, in order, the things they would like to be different, and ask which is the most annoying. A young person who is consulted about the order of the agenda will engage with the agenda; a young person who is presented with a list of the adults' priorities will not.

**Find the "if you were me" moment.** A strategic technique for young people is to ask what the young person would do if they were the parent, and what they would say, and then to explore what would have to be true for that to work. The exercise is engaging, it is often revealing, and it places the young person in the position of an expert, which is developmentally appropriate and is markedly different from the position in which most referrals place them.

**Use the sequence with the school.** The problem-maintaining sequence for a school refusal usually runs through the school as much as through the family, and the intervention at the "the school agrees" point is often the one that matters most, and it requires the practitioner to work with the school, which is consultation work and is described in Chapter 56.

**Watch the paradoxical move carefully with adolescents.** The paradoxical injunction is the technique most likely to be misunderstood by an adolescent and most likely to be misread by a parent, and the current practice is to use the technique only where the young person is present, is told what is happening and why, and has agreed to it in advance. The ethical analysis in Section 10 sets this out in full.

**Use the restriction early.** Restrictions are easy to make, produce observable effects quickly, and give the family a first experience of the sequence changing, which is what makes the rest of the work possible.

### 6.3 The problem with the symptom-as-solution formulation

The formulation has a real risk that a student should be able to name: **the risk that the young person's symptom is read as a manoeuvre.** If the practitioner believes that the young person is maintaining the symptom deliberately, the whole frame becomes an accusation, and the family will hear it as one, and the young person will be confirmed in their belief that the adults are trying to catch them out.

The discipline required is to hold the formulation at the level of the **system** rather than the level of the individual. The question is not "is the young person doing this on purpose?" but "what happens in this family, in response to this behaviour, that makes the behaviour the best available option?" The first question is a question about a person's sincerity, which is usually unanswerable and always unhelpful. The second is a question about a system, which is answerable and is what the practitioner can change.

### 6.4 The reformulation of the referral

A distinctive and useful feature of the strategic model is the **reformulation of the referral**, and a student should be able to perform it. The procedure has four steps.

**Take the referral literally.** What exactly has been asked for, by whom, and in what words?

**Ask what the family is currently doing about it.** The family's own efforts are the most informative data available, and the pattern of what they do and do not do is the sequence.

**Find what the symptom is doing for the young person.** What does it accomplish? What does it prevent? What does it permit?

**Offer an alternative.** The strategic school's formulation ends in a different solution, and the formulation is incomplete until the alternative is named.

A worked example. Referral: "a fourteen-year-old boy who refuses to speak to his mother and is being oppositional." Strategic reformulation: "When he refuses to speak, his mother stops asking and his father steps in and the two of them argue while he is upstairs, and he is not spoken to for the rest of the evening, and the following morning the pattern is that the mother is formal and the father is the one who talks to him about football. The silence is doing three things: it stops the arguments, it preserves his position as the one who has a private channel with his father, and it keeps his mother at a distance that he is not yet able to manage. The alternative we are looking for is a way for him to be left alone without the silence having to be the instrument."

## 7. Case Illustration: The Young Woman Who Was Refusing to Grow Up

**Presentation.** Iris is seventeen, in her final year at a sixth-form college, and has been referred by her mother with a message that the college has passed on: "She has decided she is going to work in a shop and she's not going to get her grades, and she won't listen to reason." The mother's stated worry is that Iris is "throwing her life away" and that the family is going to lose the money that the college has cost.

**The individual session.** Iris's own account is consistent and calm and does not match the referral at all. She says that she has decided to work in a shop because her mother has been talking about "the money" every day for six weeks, and that the message she has received is that the family cannot afford the college and that she is expected to sacrifice. She says that she has done the calculations and that she would rather work and be independent than stay and be a burden. She says she is not angry with her mother. She says she has already applied for two jobs.

She also says the thing that changes the case: "If I got the grades, I'd have to go to university, and then I'd be at home for another three years doing a subject I don't want, and I'd have to leave again. Staying at home and working is the only way I can see of getting out of here for good."

**The sequence.** The practitioner maps the sequence, and the mapping is done with Iris, in the session, and it is the most important part of the assessment.

The sequence runs: Iris mentions a possible job → her mother becomes anxious and the conversation turns to money → the mother says something about the cost of the college and about how the family is managing → Iris's face closes → Iris stops responding → the mother raises her voice and cites the money again → Iris leaves the room → the mother interprets the leaving as the proof that Iris does not care → the mother mentions it to Iris's aunt, and to the college, and to Iris's father, who is absent from the house and is contacted by telephone → Iris hears, from her aunt, that her mother has "given up" on her → Iris confirms to herself that she is a burden and that leaving is the only option that is not a burden.

The sequence has a specific and important feature: **the money is not actually the problem.** The mother's anxiety about money is real and is a genuine constraint, and the money is the vehicle on which the anxiety is expressed, and the escalation it produces is what Iris experiences as being told to sacrifice.

**The reformulation.** The strategic reformulation is: *Iris is not refusing to grow up. Iris has found a solution to a problem that the family has not been able to name, which is that the family has no route by which Iris can leave. The current solution is to leave by dropping out, which delivers her independence at the price of her future options. The alternative is to find a route by which she can leave with her future intact, and to do it in a way that does not require her mother to be told that she is the reason Iris is leaving.*

**The intervention.** Three elements.

*Element one: the mother, alone.* The practitioner sees the mother on her own, and the conversation is about the money, and the practitioner is careful: the money is real, the family's financial situation is what it is, and the college costs what it costs, and none of that is disputed. What the practitioner then works on is the *timing* of the money conversation. It occurs every day, it occurs in relation to Iris, and it occurs in a form that is a complaint. The practitioner asks the mother what she would like to happen with the money, and the mother says she wants Iris to finish the year, and the practitioner asks what she wants to happen if Iris does not, and the mother says "I don't know" and then says, after a pause, "I suppose I'd have to find a way." The practitioner returns to this every session: the money is solvable, and the money is not the real issue.

*Element two: the reframe.* The practitioner offers a reframe to the mother, and it is offered as a hypothesis and it is the sentence that changes the case: "I think Iris is not refusing to grow up. I think she is trying to leave, and the only route she can see is the one that costs you the most. And I think she has worked out that if she leaves with the grades, she will have to leave anyway, and be back."

The mother says: "She never said that." The practitioner says: "No. But she has been working it out for months, and she has been doing the arithmetic at two in the morning rather than at the kitchen table, and I think it would help if we brought it out."

*Element three: the directive.* The strategic school's directive is what makes the reframe operational, and it is specific and dated and given with attribution. The practitioner tells the mother: "For the next two weeks, you are not going to talk to Iris about money. Not once. If she raises it, you will say, 'We are going to talk about this with Ms Adeyemi on Thursday, and until then I am not discussing it.' You will not raise it, and you will not hint at it, and you will not ask her whether she has thought about the money. I am telling you to do this because I am the person in this room who has the most experience of this particular problem, and I am telling you because it is the only way we will find out whether the money is the problem."

**The outcome.** On the Thursday, Iris says almost exactly what the practitioner predicted: she does not want to work in a shop, she is frightened that the family will not be able to pay for a university, and she does not know how to say that without it sounding like an accusation. The financial plan is then made with the mother's help and with the college's financial support office, which has funds that nobody had asked about, and the practical problem — which was real — turns out to be solvable.

Iris completes the year with three A-levels and applies to university. The relationship between her and her mother is not warm; it is functional, and the practitioner regards that as the realistic outcome and says so to both of them.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the strategic model completely. The symptom was a solution; the sequence was mapped in the family's own words; the reframe was specific and was offered as a hypothesis; the directive was specific, dated, attributed, and designed to produce a testable result. And the most important clinical move was the refusal to accept the referral's framing — the practitioner did not treat Iris as a problem and her mother as an adult with legitimate concerns, and treated the referral itself as a symptom of the family system.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The status of the evidence

The strategic school has a modest and difficult evidence base, and the reasons are instructive.

**There is little controlled outcome research on the strategic model as a whole.** The school emerged from a research group that was interested in the description of communication rather than in the evaluation of treatment, and the tradition has been more clinical than experimental.

**The research that does exist on the strategic school's specific techniques is largely negative or inconclusive.** The studies of the paradoxical injunction and the paradoxical prescription have not produced support for the techniques as general interventions, and the ethical concerns have reduced their use considerably.

**The evidence for family-based and systemic approaches that are strategic in their core is good.** The family-based treatment of adolescent eating disorders, the behavioural family therapy approaches, and the community-based systemic interventions all share the strategic premise that the problem is maintained by a sequence and that changing the sequence changes the problem. This is the most useful way for a student to think about the evidence: the strategic model is best understood not as a distinct intervention with its own evidence but as a way of thinking that is embedded in a good deal of the effective family-based work.

**The solution-focused work that grew from the strategic school has become the best-evidenced of the family-derived approaches**, and Chapter 35 develops it. The solution-focused approaches are the strategic school's most successful and most ethical descendant, and the reason is instructive: the solution-focused work took the strategic school's insight about sequence and reframing and removed the elements that were hazardous, retaining the reframe, the directive, and the emphasis on the future.

### 8.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The allegiance effects are very large** in the family literature generally and in the strategic school in particular, and the field's own reviews have been unusually candid about this.

**The outcomes are proximal** and the distal outcomes are rarely measured.

**The "problems as solutions" framing is difficult to evaluate**, because a formulation that explains the symptom as functional is in some respects unfalsifiable, and a student who adopts the model uncritically may find that every outcome can be explained as the problem continuing to serve its function.

**The evidence base for the techniques has not caught up with their use**, and the field has had to rely on clinical judgement and ethical argument for the assessment of the more controversial interventions.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of the Strategic Model

### 9.1 Strengths

**It takes the symptom seriously.** The reframing of the referral is a humane and clinically productive act.

**It identifies what is changeable.** The problem-maintaining sequence is a description of the present, and the present is where the leverage is.

**It uses the young person's motivation rather than fighting it.** The strategic model's respect for the young person's own goal is a genuine clinical asset.

**It is fast.** Strategic work with adolescents is often brief, and brevity is a clinical advantage with a population that does not stay.

**It is directive, and some young people need that.** The model's willingness to instruct rather than to explore is a feature with a population that experiences exploratory therapy as evasive.

**It produces testable interventions.** A directive is designed to produce a result that confirms or disconfirms the hypothesis, which keeps the work honest.

### 9.2 Limitations

**The symptom-as-solution formulation can become an accusation.** The risk is real and it has damaged young people in real cases.

**The technical repertoire contains interventions that are ethically indefensible in their original form**, and the ordeals in particular should be understood as history.

**It is a model of the family as a system of messages, and it under-accounts for what the family members actually feel.** A family that is a system of communication is a family that is not in pain, and the model has no very good answer to the young person who is in pain.

**The evidence is thin**, and the field has been more successful in the successor schools than in the school itself.

**It under-accounts for the structural and the material.** A young person who cannot attend school because the school is unsafe, or because the family has no transport, is not maintaining a solution.

**The techniques are easy to deploy badly.** A directive issued without the skill to design it is a manipulation, and a reframe that does not fit is a lie.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 The ethics of directive work

The directive is the technique in this chapter that most requires ethical care, and the principles are worth stating precisely.

**A directive is an exercise of professional authority and must be given as one.** The strategic therapists insisted that a directive be attributed to the therapist and not disguised as a suggestion, and this is ethically correct: a disguised directive is manipulation, and the young person is entitled to know that an adult with authority is telling them what to do.

**A directive must be for the young person's benefit or be one they have agreed to.** The strategic school's controversial directives have typically been in the young person's interest in the model's terms, and the question "in whose interest" is precisely what the ethical analysis must examine rather than assume.

**A directive must be safe.** The ordeals were not. Any contemporary directive must be satisfiable, must not expose the young person to risk, and must not require a family member to suffer.

**A directive must be transparent in its purpose.** The young person should know, as far as is possible, what the directive is designed to change and what it is designed to test.

### 10.2 The ethics of the team of the strategic

The team of the strategic — instructing one family member to covertly act as the therapist's ally — is the technique in this book that most directly confronts the principle of informed consent, and the contemporary professional position is clear.

The technique involves deliberately managing information about a person's own behaviour in a way that the person does not know. In family work, where the family's confidentiality is already a negotiated and partial thing, the practice of adding a covert layer has been widely criticised, and the criticism is well founded.

Three requirements follow for any contemporary use. The person whose information is being managed must be informed of what is being done and must have the opportunity to object. The people who are being asked to act as allies must understand exactly what they are being asked to do, including the fact that they will be concealing it. And the person who is the object of the intervention must be able to end the arrangement.

A student who is asked to deploy the team of the strategic without these safeguards should treat the request as a request for training and supervision, and should not comply without them.

### 10.3 The ethics of deception and reframing

The **reframe** is generally defensible, and it is important to distinguish it from deception. A reframe offers a different account that is *true*, or at least more nearly true than the account in place, and that makes the problem more understandable. A deception offers an account that the practitioner does not believe.

The test is simple and the practitioner should be able to state it: **is the practitioner willing for the young person to know how the practitioner arrived at this formulation?** If the answer is no, it is a deception, and the practice is not defensible.

The second test is whether the reframe serves the young person or the adults. A reframe that enables the parents to be more comfortable while the young person remains in the same bind is a reframe in the service of the adults.

### 10.4 Cultural considerations

**Communication styles vary.** Directness, indirectness, the use of silence, the degree of explicitness in disagreement, and the conventions of respect all vary across cultures and across families, and a practitioner who reads a family as double-bound may be reading a culture as double-bound.

**Hierarchy and the strategic directive.** The strategic model's directives are issued by the professional to the family, and in a context in which the professional's standing is not established, or in which the family's internal hierarchy is the real authority, a directive may be ignored or may cause offence, and the practitioner should understand the refusal as information.

**The family as a unit.** The strategic model assumes that the family is the meaningful unit of the problem. Where the young person's primary attachment is to a kinship network, a peer group, a religious community, or a cultural group that the practitioner does not see, the model will miss the actual system.

**Language and the metacommunication.** The analysis of communication is unusually sensitive to language, and a practitioner working across languages will find that the metacommunication is in a language the young person does not share, or that the family code-switches in ways that conceal the pattern from a non-fluent observer. The practitioner should involve an interpreter and should treat the interpreter as a clinical colleague rather than as a logistics function.

**The economic framing.** The strategic model's willingness to say that the money is not the real problem is a luxury in a setting in which the money is the real problem, and the practitioner must establish the material facts before offering the reframe.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Strategic family therapy** — The school developed from the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto, in which the family is understood as a system of communication and the clinician intervenes by changing the sequences that maintain the problem.
- **Problem-maintaining sequence** — The specific, mapped, and dated chain of events that keeps the problem going, and the central clinical object of the model.
- **Symptom as a solution** — The claim that the symptom is the best available solution to a problem the family cannot otherwise solve, and that its side effects are the reason for referral.
- **Problem as a dilemma** — The formulation in which the problem is simultaneously a genuine problem and a solution, and the two cannot be separated.
- **Directive** — A specific, dated, behavioural instruction given by the therapist to a family member, attributed to the therapist, and designed to produce a testable result.
- **The attributes of a directive** — Specificity, attribution to the therapist, and a date; a directive issued as advice is a wasted instruction.
- **Paradoxical injunction** — A directive asking a family member to do deliberately what the symptom involves, so that the meaning of the behaviour changes and the maintaining sequence is broken.
- **Paradoxical prescription** — A task that is deliberately difficult and probably impossible, whose failure exposes the family to the inadequacy of its current solution.
- **Ordeal** — A task of serious cost undertaken for the sake of a change in the identified patient; now almost entirely discredited.
- **Reframe** — The reinterpretation of the problem, offered as a hypothesis, that makes the symptom more understandable and more tractable.
- **The test of a reframe** — Whether the practitioner would be willing for the young person to know how the formulation was reached, and whether the reframe serves the young person or the adults.
- **Restriction** — An instruction to suspend a family member's behaviour that has become part of the maintaining sequence.
- **Team of the strategic** — The technique in which a family member is instructed to act as the therapist's covert ally in changing another family member.
- **Communication theory** — The analysis of the patterned exchanges between people, developed by Bateson and colleagues and supplying the vocabulary of the family therapy field.
- **Double bind** — Two incompatible messages received under a restriction on the recipient's ability to respond, and a punishment for attempting to resolve the contradiction by noticing it.
- **The four conditions of a double bind** — A contradictory pair of messages, a repeated pattern, a restriction on the response, and a punishment for attempting to resolve the contradiction.
- **Bateson's paradox** — A self-referential contradiction in which the message denies the validity of the communication system in which it is transmitted.
- **Symmetrical interaction** — A pattern in which the participants' behaviours mirror each other, tending to escalate.
- **Complementary interaction** — A pattern in which the participants' behaviours are complementary, stable, and mutually reinforcing.
- **Metacommunication** — A communication about a communication, carrying a commentary on the relationship in which it is transmitted.
- **Restoring content statements** — The clinical task of enabling a family to make statements that can be checked against reality.
- **You cannot not communicate** — The first of the rules of communication; every behaviour, including silence, is a message.
- **The communication audit** — The exercise in which a family member records the messages received and sent, so that a diffuse sense of being controlled becomes a specific and examinable set of statements.
- **The developmental double bind** — The requirement that the adolescent be a child in dependence and an adult in responsibility, in which every response is a symptom.
- **Reformulation of the referral** — The four-step procedure of taking the referral literally, examining the family's own efforts, identifying what the symptom accomplishes, and naming an alternative.
- **The "if you were me" question** — A strategic technique for adolescents that places the young person in the position of an expert and elicits their own solution.
- **The irreversibility of communication** — The rule that a message cannot be recalled and that a poorly worded remark will be understood and cannot be forgotten.
- **Directiveness with adolescents** — The strategic school's willingness to instruct rather than to explore, which some young people experience as respect and others as intrusion.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

The strategic school began from the question of what maintains the problem now, and its answer — that a symptom continues because it solves something — produced a distinctive model and a distinctive repertoire. The problem-maintaining sequence is the central clinical object and is mapped in the family's own words with specific instances, and every element of the sequence is a candidate for change. The formulation of the symptom as a solution follows directly, and it ends in the identification of an alternative.

Communication theory supplied the analytic vocabulary. The double bind was described with its four conditions, and the observation was made that the developmental, the love–control, and the two-message forms are the ones that appear in work with adolescents. The symmetrical and the complementary patterns were described with the clinical consequence that the complementary patterns are the ones in which a problem is most easily maintained. The metacommunication was described, and the first substantive task in work with a family locked into a metacommunicative spiral was identified as the restoration of the ability to make content statements. The four rules of communication were set out with their clinical consequences, and the irreversibility rule was identified as a constraint on the practitioner's own conduct.

The techniques were described in detail: the enactment of the sequence, with its distinctive purpose of testing rather than merely diagnosing; the directive, with its attributes of specificity, attribution, and date; the paradoxical injunction; the paradoxical prescription; the ordeal, with the explicit statement that it is now discredited; the reframe, with its clinical test; the restriction; and the team of the strategic. The chapter was explicit about which of these survive into contemporary practice and which do not.

The adaptations for young people were described, with the four complaints, the "if you were me" technique, the extension of the sequence into the school, the caution about the paradoxical move, and the use of the restriction early. The risk of the symptom-as-solution formulation was named: that it can become an accusation, and that the discipline required is to hold the formulation at the level of the system rather than the level of the individual.

The case of Iris demonstrated the model. The referral — that she was refusing to grow up — was reformulated: she was trying to leave, and the only route she could see cost her mother the most. The sequence was mapped with Iris in the room, and the mapping revealed that the money was the vehicle for the mother's anxiety and not the problem itself. The reframe was offered to the mother as a hypothesis, and the directive — not to raise the money for two weeks — produced the conversation that the whole case needed. The outcome was functional rather than warm, and the practitioner said so to both parties.

The evidence was presented accurately: little controlled research on the model, weak support for its controversial techniques, good support for the family-based and systemic approaches that are strategic in their core, and the best-evidenced of the successors being the solution-focused work of Chapter 35. The strengths and limitations were set out, and the limitations included the accusatory risk, the ethically indefensible elements of the historical repertoire, the under-accounting of feeling, the thin evidence, and the under-accounting of the material and the structural.

The ethical analysis was substantial. The ethics of directive work were set out with the requirement that a directive be attributed, be for the young person or be agreed to, be safe, and be transparent in its purpose. The ethics of the team of the strategic were treated directly, and the three requirements of informed consent, understanding, and the right to end the arrangement were stated. The distinction between a reframe and a deception was drawn with the test of whether the practitioner would be willing for the young person to know how the formulation was reached. The cultural considerations addressed communication style, hierarchy and the standing of the professional's directive, the assumption that the family is the unit, the language and the metacommunication, and the economic framing — including the point that the practitioner's willingness to say that the money is not the real problem is a luxury in a setting in which it is.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 32.1 — Mapping a Sequence (90 minutes, field-based)

Take a problem you have actually encountered — your own, a friend's, a placement case — and map the problem-maintaining sequence in detail, in the specific terms in which it occurs, with dates and times. Then mark every point in the sequence at which a different response by a family member would produce a different outcome. Then write the single reframe that the sequence supports. Most students find that the sequence contains at least five change points, that the referral describes none of them, and that the reframe is easier to write once the sequence is mapped.

### Activity 32.2 — The Communication Audit (60 minutes, individual)

For one day, record the messages you receive and send in one family or one friendship, with the content and the metacommunication. Then identify the double binds you are in, if any, and identify the messages that cannot be commented on. Then write what a person in that family could actually do differently tomorrow, in one specific situation. The exercise is the fastest way to understand why a family cannot change a pattern, and it is usually unexpectedly humbling.

### Activity 32.3 — Writing a Directive (75 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, write a directive for a realistic adolescent situation. The group must specify: the exact behaviour, the date, the person to whom it is addressed, the attribution, what happens if it is not complied with, and what the directive is designed to test. Then rewrite it as advice, in order to see how much authority is lost. Then rewrite it so that it is covert, in order to see what is lost there, and discuss whether the second version is defensible. The third step is the important one, and the group discussion of it is usually the most valuable part of the exercise.

### Activity 32.4 — The Reframe Audit (60 minutes, individual)

Take a reframe that you have heard used in a placement, a staff room, or a supervision meeting, and audit it. Is it true? Is it more nearly true than the account in place? Does it serve the young person or the adults? Would the young person recognise it as an accurate description of their own experience? Would the practitioner be willing for the young person to know how the reframe was arrived at? The five questions are the chapter's ethical analysis compressed into an exercise, and they apply to every reframe a student will ever hear.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the communication theory.** Read Bateson and colleagues (1964) and then Watzlawick and colleagues (1967), which is the most quoted and most useful book in the family therapy field. The double bind and the metacommunication are best learned from the original formulations.

**For the model.** Read Haley (1963) and Madanes (1981). The later strategic work of the Milan group, in Andolfi and Canestri (2008), is the most useful contemporary account of the strategic tradition in practice.

**For the successor schools.** Read de Shazer (1985) and Walter and Peller (1992) for the solution-focused work of Chapter 35, and read the narrative therapy literature for Chapter 36. The strategic school's most successful descendants are more widely used than the school itself, and a student who knows the descendants will use the school's insights in a more defensible form.

**For the ethics.** Read the ethical guidelines for the family therapies and the critical literature on the team of the strategic and on the ordeals. The critical literature is essential and it is not difficult to find.

**For the evidence.** Read the family-based treatment evidence in the adjacent areas, and the solution-focused evidence. The strategic model is best understood through the evidence for what it contributed to.
