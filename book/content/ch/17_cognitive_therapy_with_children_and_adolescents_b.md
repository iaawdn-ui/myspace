## 7. The Developmental Formulation

### 7.1 From the cognitive conceptualisation to the developmental formulation

The cognitive conceptualisation of Chapter 16 is necessary but insufficient with a young person, because it locates the difficulty inside the young person's thinking and locates the developmental context nowhere. A developmental formulation adds four things.

**The developmental position.** The formulation states where the young person is in relation to the tasks of their stage, and what the difficulty consists of in developmental terms. A formulation that says "Nadia's core belief is that she is unlovable" is incomplete; the developmental formulation adds that Nadia is at the stage at which the identity task is in progress, that the core belief has the function of protecting against the developmental risk of committing to an identity that will be disapproved of by her family, and that the belief is thus not merely inaccurate but developmentally costly in a way that a cognitive intervention alone will not reach.

**The developmental trajectory.** The formulation states the expected course with and without intervention, and it states whether the developmental task is progressing.

**The level of the intervention.** The formulation states at what level the cognitive work should be conducted — the concrete, the imaginal, or the propositional — and this is a technical decision with clinical consequences.

**The developmental and structural context.** The formulation states what the young person's development is being obstructed by, beyond the young person's own thinking: the school's response, the family's pattern, the material conditions, the discrimination.

### 7.2 A worked formulation

Take Kofi, fourteen, who is referred for "school refusal and low mood." The cognitive conceptualisation establishes: the hot spot is the maths lesson; the automatic thought is "everyone will see that I can't do it"; the emotion is a hot, tight feeling in the chest; the behaviour is to leave the room; the intermediate belief is "If I make a mistake in front of people, I will be humiliated"; the core belief is "I am stupid"; the coping behaviours are avoidance and reassurance-seeking from his mother.

The developmental formulation adds the following.

*The developmental position.* Kofi is in the school-age-to-adolescent transition, and the industry-versus-inferiority task is the relevant one; the avoidance is serving to prevent the confirmation of the "inferiority" position, and the confirmation is being actively engineered by the school. The avoidance is not a failure of courage; it is a rational response to an environment in which the risk of humiliation is high and repeated.

*The trajectory.* The trajectory is towards increasing withdrawal, narrowing of the social world, and a rising risk of the young person being classified and streamed, with the developmental consequence that the school leaving outcomes and the young person's sense of competence both deteriorate. The developmental literature on school refusal and on chronic absenteeism is consistent about this trajectory and about the narrowing of the young person's developmental world.

*The level of the intervention.* The cognitive work is at the concrete and imaginal level, with a graded behavioural experiment at the centre, and the young person's mother is involved with the first two steps of the accommodation reduction.

*The context.* The school has a streaming policy that places Kofi in a lower set, the mathematics teacher has a reputation for public correction, and Kofi's mother's anxiety about his school attendance has led to a series of health appointments for "tiredness" and "stomach aches" that have not been examined for their function. The formulation therefore specifies that the work is not only with Kofi's appraisal but with the school, with the mother, and with the physical symptoms.

The result is a formulation with a different plan from the cognitive one alone: the graded exposure of the mathematics classroom, the work with the teacher about the public correction, the graded reduction of the mother's checking and reassurance, and the medical review of the stomach aches, alongside the cognitive work on the "I am stupid" belief.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

**The evidence is strongest for the developmentally adapted treatments of depression and anxiety, and it is stronger than for any other psychological treatment with young people.**

**Depression.** The developmentally adapted cognitive therapy for depression in young people has been evaluated in multiple large randomised controlled trials across several countries, with a meta-analytic pooled effect in the moderate range, and with a re-analysis excluding developer-investigator trials producing a smaller but still clinically meaningful effect. The evidence for parental involvement, for the combination of behavioural activation with the cognitive work, and for the treatment delivered in school settings is good. The evidence for the long-term maintenance of the gains is thinner, and the follow-up data in several trials show a partial erosion of the treatment effect over the following year, which has led to the development of maintenance and booster strategies and to the current preference for the treatment of depression as a longer-term and developmentally embedded piece of work.

**Anxiety.** The developmental adaptations of the cognitive-behavioural treatments of anxiety are among the most evaluated interventions in the child mental health literature, and the effect sizes are moderate to large. The parent involvement component has been demonstrated to be a mediator of outcome in several trials, and the exposure and modelling components carry the effect.

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder.** The adapted exposure and response prevention has a strong evidence base in young people, with large effects, and with a body of research on the family accommodation component and on the developmental predictors of outcome.

**The limitations.** The population problem persists in all three literatures. The samples are disproportionately white, female, affluent, and without neurodevelopmental conditions or chronic physical illness, and the evidence for young people with these characteristics is much thinner, and in the case of neurodevelopmental conditions the treatments frequently need substantial modification, with the modifications often under-researched. The generalisability of the trials to ordinary services is a question that the pragmatic and practice-based literature raises, and the developmental adaptations have not been subjected to the same scrutiny that the adult treatments have.

**The evidence for the developmental adaptations of the thought record as such is much thinner than the evidence for the treatments that contain them.** The field has generally evaluated packages rather than components, and a student should be aware that the specific claim "this format of the thought record is effective with this age group" is much less well supported than "this package is effective."

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Developmental Cognitive Therapy

**Strengths.** The cognitive model provides a testable account that a young person and a family can engage with, and the collaborative empiricism is a relational stance that is compatible with the developmental requirement that a young person be treated as a thinker. The evidence base is the strongest in the field, the treatments are manualised and deliverable in schools and in brief forms, and the digital versions reach young people who will not attend a clinic. The approach is teachable, and a graduate student can learn the core procedures competently within a year.

**Limitations.** The model is thin on emotion, on the body, on development, and on the social and structural context, and each of these thinnesses is consequential with young people. The developmental adaptations are considerable, and the packages that are evaluated are complex enough that the practitioner must be trained in them rather than simply applying the adult model. The approach can produce compliance rather than learning, and the compliance is more difficult to detect with a young person than with an adult. And the historical tendency of the field to treat the young person's thinking as the site of the problem, and therefore to locate the solution in the young person's head, has had measurable consequences in the services in which it has dominated.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 Culture in the developmental adaptation

The developmental modifications of the cognitive model are themselves culturally marked in ways that are easy to miss. The "safer" version of the young person is assumed to be a young person who can tolerate the examination of their thinking, and the techniques that are used to promote this — the thought record, the behavioural experiment, the exposure, the mood chart — are all techniques that presuppose a young person who is willing to be observed and to have their predictions tested.

Several of the young people for whom the evidence is weakest are those for whom these presuppositions are least secure: a young person who has been racially profiled by a school, a young person who is undocumented, a young person who has been told by an authority that their account is not believed, a young person with a communication difference who has been told that they "express themselves badly." For these young people, the collaborative empiricism must be established with unusual care, and the practitioner's first task may be to establish that the examination of propositions will not be used against them.

The emotional vocabulary list developed for the developmental adaptation is also culturally specific. The list is derived from English-language emotion research conducted largely with Western participants, and the categories in it — the distinctions among "ashamed," "embarrassed," and "guilty," for example — do not map cleanly onto the emotional categories of every language. A practitioner working with a young person who is more comfortable in another language should develop the vocabulary with the young person and their family rather than impose the list.

### 10.2 The ethical question of the behavioural experiment with a young person

The behavioural experiment is the technique with the greatest potential for ethical difficulty in the cognitive tradition, because it deliberately arranges for the young person to encounter the feared situation and to be exposed to the risk that the belief is confirmed.

The ethical requirements are: that the experiment be genuinely designed to be capable of disconfirming the belief; that the young person understand what will happen and why, in a form they can grasp; that the young person have a genuine choice about whether to proceed; that the practitioner be confident that the support is adequate if the experiment is difficult; that the consequences of a disconfirmation be considered in advance; and that the young person not be exposed to a risk that the practitioner has not assessed.

The most common breach is the fourth. An experiment that is too ambitious, conducted to demonstrate the treatment's effectiveness rather than to test the belief, is a common error in services with outcome targets, and it is the developmental equivalent of the premature confrontation discussed in Chapter 11.

### 10.3 The ethical question of the parent

The ethical requirements in work with parents are specific. The young person must know what will be shared with the parent, and the practitioner must be able to work with a parent without disclosing the young person's disclosures. The requirement of confidentiality with minors is a legal requirement that varies by jurisdiction, and the practitioner must know the law in the jurisdiction in which they practise. The requirement against the covert alliance between the practitioner and the parent is an ethical requirement and not merely a technique. And the requirement that the young person's own account of their difficulties be treated as primary, even when the parent and the practitioner agree about it, is the requirement that most often fails in practice.

### 10.4 The ethical advantage

The ethical advantage of the cognitive approach with young people is worth recording. It offers a young person a way of understanding and of influencing their own experience, which is a considerable gift, and it does so in a framework in which the young person is the investigator and the adult is the collaborator. For a young person who has been told that they are the problem, the model says: you are the one who has been studying this problem, you have collected a great deal of data, and some of your conclusions need revisiting. That is a very different conversation from the one the young person has usually had.

## 11. Case Illustration: The Thought Record That Was Not a Thought Record

**The referral.** Priya, eleven, is referred by her school for "anxiety and refusing to do PE." Priya's mother is a single parent of three, working full time, and Priya is the eldest. The school reports that Priya has become "withdrawn in PE", that she has been "crying" twice, and that she has refused on four occasions. There is a note in the file from the school counsellor: "P talks about feeling 'different' and says her skin feels wrong. Mum says she's always been a worrier."

**The first session.** The practitioner does not begin with the thought record. She begins with a body outline that she has drawn on a large sheet of paper, and she asks Priya to colour in the places where she feels things. Priya colours her chest and her stomach. The practitioner asks, "And when you are in the changing room and the other girls are talking, what happens?" Priya says: "My tummy goes like this." She makes a gesture. The practitioner asks her to draw that shape on the body outline. Priya does, and the shape is a tight spiral.

**The work.** Over the next three sessions, Priya and the practitioner work with a series of small, concrete, sensory-level "thought records" that are adapted for a child who is eleven and who has never been asked to introspect about her thinking. The record has four questions, written in Priya's own words on a card that she keeps in her pocket:

1. *What was happening?* (Priya draws it.)
2. *What did your body do?* (Priya circles on the body outline.)
3. *What did you think?* (Priya writes one sentence, or draws a word balloon.)
4. *What did you do next?*

The fourth question is the one that produces the most change, and it produces it because the behavioural link becomes visible. Priya's pattern: something in the changing room is said — often something about her body, and specifically about her skin, which is darker than some of the other girls' — and then Priya's stomach tightens, and then she goes to the toilet and stays there until the lesson is nearly over, and the consequence is that she is relieved and that the other girls leave her alone.

**The formulation.** The cognitive conceptualisation is: the hot spot is the changing room; the automatic thought is "they're talking about me"; the intermediate belief is "if they see my skin they will talk about it and I will be humiliated"; the core belief is "I am different and that is bad"; the coping behaviour is avoidance, and it works.

The developmental formulation adds: Priya is at the stage at which the peer group is becoming the primary developmental context and the developmental task of the school-age years — the acquisition of competence and the establishment of a peer position — is being obstructed by the avoidance, which is removing her from the very context in which the task is performed; the avoidance is also removing her from the physical education, which is where she is physically competent, and the developmental cost of that is the loss of a domain of mastery at exactly the age at which a domain of mastery is being established.

And the formulation specifies the level of the intervention: the cognitive work is at the concrete, imaginal, and behavioural level, and the work is with the school, because the reference to Priya's skin is not a distortion to be corrected but a description of what is happening in the changing room.

**The work with the school.** The practitioner contacts Priya's PE teacher and asks a functional question: "What happens in the changing room?" The teacher is surprised and thinks about it, and then says: "Oh — we talk about our skin. The other girls, they talk about their skin, and I don't think they notice, but Priya's in the middle of it." The teacher, who has never thought about it, changes what happens: the girls are asked to change in a particular way, or the changing is staggered, or the class is asked to stop talking about their bodies. No one needed to be accused of anything.

**The outcome.** Priya returns to PE. Her "thought records" become more verbal over the weeks, and she begins to write a fifth question on her card: "What could I try?" The core belief does not shift entirely, and the practitioner does not press. The significant change is behavioural and contextual, and the practitioner records the case accordingly.

**The teaching point.** The cognitive work was not the difficult part and was not the most important part. The "thought record" was a four-question card with a body outline and a spiral, and it was not a thought record at all in the adult sense. The most important interventions were the body outline, which gave a non-verbal young person access to her own experience, and the question to the teacher, which changed the environment in which the difficulty was being maintained. A practitioner who had applied the adult thought record to Priya would have produced a sophisticated-looking record in which the hot spot was the changing room, the automatic thought was mis-stated, the core belief was described in adult language, and nothing at all would have changed.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Developmental adaptation** — The modification of a treatment's procedures to fit a young person's cognitive, emotional, social, and autonomy capacities; a condition of validity rather than a dilution.
- **Propositional thought** — The capacity to think in abstract propositions; late-developing, arousal-sensitive, and required in the standard form of the cognitive techniques.
- **Retrospective introspection** — The ability to report one's own thinking in a past situation; a developmentally limited capacity on which the adult thought record depends.
- **The doubting mind** — The learned distrust of one's own thinking that results from having been told that one's thoughts are not true, and that interferes directly with the collaborative empiricism.
- **The "said-to" thought record** — A developmental adaptation in which the young person reports what was said rather than what was thought, shifting the task from metacognition to recall.
- **The four-question record** — A simplified developmental thought record in a child's own words, typically covering the situation, the body, the thought, and the next action.
- **The body outline** — A pictorial instrument for locating feelings and sensations in the body; a developmental alternative to the verbal emotion rating.
- **Worst, best, most likely** — A developmental adaptation of the behavioural experiment's prediction rating, which permits the young person to discover that their own estimate of the likely outcome is less catastrophic than their estimate of the worst.
- **Developmental model of anxiety** — The account in which a child's fear is a rational response to a real lack of control in a specific domain, and in which the treatment develops control and reduces the caregiving response to the fear.
- **Parent accommodation** — The parent's reinforcement of the young person's avoidance and reassurance-seeking; a primary maintenance factor and a primary target in the anxiety adaptations.
- **Behavioural activation with young people** — The scheduling of activity to reverse the withdrawal and anhedonia of depression; requires careful pacing to avoid a further drop in mood.
- **Streaming** — The school practice of grouping students by attainment; a systemic factor that must be addressed in the formulation of a young person's academic difficulties.
- **The optimisation of the problem** — A specific cognitive therapy technique in which the problem is imagined to be as bad as possible, used with catastrophising.
- **Socratic questioning** — The technique of examining a belief by asking questions rather than by making statements; a cognitive technique that is a poor fit with a pre-operational child.
- **The mood chart** — A daily record of mood and activity across a range of activities, used both to generate behavioural activation and to provide between-session data.
- **Public correction** — A classroom practice in which a teacher corrects a student publicly; a specific and modifiable maintaining factor in the academic anxiety of a young person.
- **Cognitively mediated maintenance** — The account in which the young person's own cognitive processes maintain the disorder, as distinct from the accounts in which the maintaining factors are primarily external.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

The adult cognitive model cannot be applied to a young person without substantive modification, and the reason is a paradox that the adaptations must address: the very capacities the technique requires — propositional thought, retrospective introspection, evaluative self-stance, divergent thinking — are among the capacities the condition has impaired, and several of them are late-developing and arousal-sensitive.

The developmental solutions are systematic: develop the capacity rather than require it, use behavioural and imaginal rather than verbal material, establish the collaborative stance through demonstrated action rather than through assertion, and involve the parent as a co-investigator rather than as an observer.

The developmental adaptations of the thought record are concrete and simple: the "said-to" version, the four-question record in the young person's own words, the body outline, and the worst–best–most-likely prediction scale. The work with parents has four distinct elements — the parent's beliefs about the symptom, the parent's responses, the parent's own predictions, and the parent's own difficulties — and four characteristic pitfalls, of which the covert alliance with the parent is the most serious.

The three best-evidenced adaptations are for depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder, and the evidence in each is strong, though the population limitations are consistent and the follow-up data in the depression literature show a partial erosion of the effect. The developmental formulation adds the developmental position, the trajectory, the level at which the intervention should be conducted, and the structural context, and the case of Priya shows what the adaptation looks like in practice: a body outline, a four-question card, and a telephone call to a teacher who had never thought about what happened in the changing room.

## 14. Review and Discussion Questions

1. State the four developmental obstacles to applying the adult cognitive model to a young person, and the developmental solution for each.
2. Explain the paradox at the centre of cognitive therapy with young people, and state how the adaptations address it.
3. Apply the zone of proximal development to the design of a thought record for a nine-year-old.
4. Describe the "said-to" adaptation and state the reason it works.
5. List the four elements of the cognitive work with parents, and distinguish them.
6. Describe the four pitfalls of parent-inclusive work, and state which is the most damaging and why.

8. Explain the developmental model of anxiety and its implications for the treatment of a young person who is afraid of the school changing room.
9. What does the developmental formulation add to the cognitive conceptualisation, and why is each of the four additions clinically necessary?
10. In the Priya case, why would the adult thought record have produced nothing, and what did the body outline do that it could not?

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 17.1 — The Four-Question Record (90 minutes, pairs)

Design a four-question thought record for a nine-year-old, a twelve-year-old, and a sixteen-year-old, in each case using language the young person would actually use. Then take the youngest version and use it on yourself, in relation to a real moment from the past week. The exercise develops the design skill and the bodily self-awareness, and the two are not separable.

### Activity 17.2 — The Parental Beliefs Audit (75 minutes, individual, written)

Take a young person you know well, or a memory of one. Write down the beliefs the parent holds about the young person's difficulty — at least five, in the parent's probable words. Then, for each belief, write: what does this belief protect the parent from? The exercise is the cognitive work with parents in miniature, and it is usually the first thing a trainee understands about parental involvement.

### Activity 17.3 — The Compliance Watch (90 minutes, triads)

One student plays a practitioner working with a young person who is being compliant; one plays the young person; one observes for any moment at which the practitioner could have noticed the compliance. The observer's task is to note the specific verbal or behavioural cues that signal it, and to note what the practitioner did. Then reverse the roles. The skill being built is the detection of performance, and it is one of the hardest in the course.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the clinical adaptations.** Read Reinecke and Roberts (2004) and Kirk and Kutchins (2013), and use Beck (2011) alongside them for the adult procedures. The gap between the two is instructive in itself, and a student who has read both will not accidentally import the adult format.

**For the developmental rationale.** Read the developmental meta-analyses for depression, anxiety, and OCD, and read the treatment adaptation literature rather than the treatment manuals. The adaptations are where the developmental understanding is visible, and the manuals tend to present them as settled technique rather than as theoretically informed modifications.

**For the parent work.** Read the parent involvement literature in the anxiety and depression trials, and read the family systems literature in Part V, which approaches the parent's own difficulty from a different direction.

**For the critique.** Read the literature on the treatment of young people with neurodevelopmental conditions and with chronic physical illness, and see how the model's assumptions fail and how the adaptations have been made. This is where the next generation of developmental cognitive work is being done.
