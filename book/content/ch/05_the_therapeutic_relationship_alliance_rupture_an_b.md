## 7. The Relationship Across the Theoretical Traditions

## 8. Repair in Practice: A Sequence

The following sequence is offered as a usable clinical procedure. It is drawn from the integrative literature and it is generic across orientations, though the interpretation of the rupture's content will be orientation-specific.

**Step 1: Attend to your own state.** Before attending to the young person, notice your own arousal, your own defensiveness, and your own pull towards a particular action — to explain, to reassure, to press, to change the subject, to end the session early. This step is easy to omit and it is the step that most improves the quality of what follows. A practitioner who is flooded cannot empathise, and a practitioner who is defensive cannot hear.

**Step 2: Verify the discrepancy.** Compare what the young person is doing with what they were doing earlier or elsewhere in the session, and with what they told you last week. The discrepancy is the data: not the behaviour itself, but the change in the behaviour.

**Step 3: Name it tentatively.** Offer the observation as a hypothesis, in the young person's terms, with an invitation to correction. "You've been very quiet since we started talking about your dad. Is that right? Have I got something wrong?" The offer must be genuinely open. A practitioner who has already decided that the young person is "in a rupture" will be heard to be making an announcement.

**Step 4: Take your share.** If there is any part of the rupture to which your conduct contributed — you pushed, you were late, you disagreed sharply, you went somewhere they did not want to go, you were distracted, you spoke over them — say so, briefly, without apology that requires the young person to reassure you. "I think I pushed too hard on that. I'm sorry. I'd rather you tell me if it's too much."

**Step 5: Explore.** Only now, with the frame restored, go to the content. The most important question at this point is not "what are you feeling?" but "what happened in here?" — because the rupture is in here, and the young person may have learned more about the relationship in the last three minutes than in the previous six sessions.

**Step 6: Repair the practical as well as the emotional.** If the rupture concerned the time of day, the length of the session, the presence of the parent in the waiting room, the homework, the school form, or anything else concrete, fix it. A young person who has been disappointed by the practitioner on a practical matter will not proceed to the emotional repair until the practical repair has occurred, and the practical repair is frequently the one that matters more.

**Step 7: Return explicitly.** "Where were we?" and then, ideally, a lighter agenda than the one that was interrupted.

**Step 8: Review the pattern.** After the session, write down what happened, your interpretation, and what you will do differently. If the same rupture recurs in the same form across three or four sessions, the interpretation is probably wrong, and the case needs consultation.

## 9. The Relationship in Family, Group, and Systemic Work

The therapeutic relationship in work with a family or a group is not one relationship but a network of them, and the network is the object of the work.

**In family work.** The practitioner is in a room with the people whose patterns are being examined, and the practitioner's conduct is itself part of the system. A practitioner who is warmly supportive of the mother and cool with the adolescent has, in the language of the systemic tradition, taken a side, and has enacted the very triangulation the family brought to the room. The practical implication is that a family practitioner must be able to notice their own positioning, and must be willing to repair it in front of the family, because a repair enacted in the room is a demonstration of what repair looks like. The parallel-process material that becomes available when a family member treats the practitioner as a confidant is among the most valuable data in family work, and it is also a hazard, because the practitioner who becomes the confidant of one member has changed the system in the act of observing it.

**In group work.** The group is a miniature social world, and the relationship between the leader and the members is replicated in the relationships among the members. A group member who is silent in the group and animated at the break is giving a group-formulation practitioner exactly the information required. Group work also provides a unique relational opportunity: a young person who cannot tolerate intimacy with an adult can sometimes tolerate it with a peer, and the observation of that tolerance is itself a form of assessment and a form of intervention. Chapters 55 and 56 of the curriculum map address group work with young people, and the common factors in group are discussed in Chapter 6.

**In consultation.** In school consultation the practitioner is in a relationship with a teacher, and the alliance is not with the student at all. The literature on consultation is consistent that the outcome of consultation is determined principally by the consultation relationship and the consultee's perception of the consultant's competence and credibility, and that consultation is frequently delivered as advice-giving under another name, which reliably produces resistance. A consultant who asks what the teacher is already trying and what has been tried will be received very differently from one who arrives with a plan. Chapter 56 develops this material.

**In telehealth.** The medium affects the relationship in specific, examinable ways: loss of the subtle embodied cues that constitute much of empathic accuracy; the young person's awareness that the practitioner is in a separate room with a computer; the presence of family members within earshot; the absence of a waiting room; and, for some young people, the relative safety of a mediated space. The literature on teletherapy alliance is smaller than the literature on in-person alliance but broadly consistent, and the recommendation that the practitioner attend explicitly to the medium — asking what the room looks like, whether the young person is alone, whether they can speak freely — is one of the more useful practical findings. Chapter 55 develops this in detail.

## 10. Case Illustration: The Girl Who Stopped Talking

**The referral.** Priya, fifteen, is referred by her school for "non-engagement" after a period of attendance at 80 per cent and a fall to 55 per cent over six weeks. She has not refused to attend; she attends and then sits in the corridor outside the counselling office for the entire session. The school counsellor, who has seen her for four sessions, reports: "She'll say 'fine' if you ask how she's doing. Doesn't talk. Ate lunch in the library this week with a book, so she's engaging with something. Parent in for the first session and did all the talking."

**The background.** Priya lives with her mother, her father, and a younger brother of eleven. She is in the second year of a new school following a house move in year ten. Her father is a doctor; her mother left paid work to care for the children and has recently begun a training course. She was previously a competitive swimmer and gave it up in the summer. There is no history of mental health treatment. The mother reports that "she's fine, she's just quiet, she was always quiet."

**The first session.** The practitioner, working in a school-based service, arranges to see Priya in a small room rather than in the counselling office, and to see her without the parent present. Priya comes, and sits, and says very little. After a few minutes she says: "Is this going to be like the other one?" The practitioner asks what she means. Priya says: "Are you going to ask me about my feelings?" And then: "Because she did, and I just said everything was fine, and then I had to sit there for forty minutes."

**The analysis.**

The first rupture in this case occurred before the practitioner arrived, in the previous service, and it is a rupture of the specific kind that the previous practitioner could not have identified because the young person had not spoken. A client who asks a question like "are you going to ask me about my feelings?" is performing a displacement: the topic under discussion is the previous sessions, and the affect belonging to that relationship is being carried into a complaint about the new practitioner's likely method.

The second rupture is occurring in the present session, in the form of a defensive silence that reproduces the structure of the previous one. The practitioner who responds to the silence with a series of open, feeling-oriented questions — "How are you feeling right now?", "What is that like?" — will reproduce the previous service's failure exactly. The likely mechanism is that Priya has learned that feeling questions in this setting lead to a demand that she produce a feeling account, which she is unwilling and unable to supply, and which she experienced as being disbelieved when she did supply a minimal one.

**The repair.** The practitioner's next move is the sequence of Section 8 applied in the present, and it is not subtle:

> "No, I don't want to do that. I think that question is a trap and I'd rather we didn't. I want to know how you spend your time — what you do, who you talk to, what the days look like. That's the kind of thing I'm interested in. And if I ask you something and you'd rather I didn't, tell me and I'll stop."

This move does several things at once. It answers the question directly, which is what Priya asked. It names the trap and thereby validates her previous experience. It offers a different structure, which addresses the learning from the previous service. It asserts the practitioner's own view (the feeling question is a trap) rather than merely accommodating, which protects the practitioner from a psychodynamic reading in which the silence is itself the symptom. And it gives Priya a second method for managing the interaction — she can say "not that one" — which is a form of the agency that the previous service did not offer.

**The working alliance.** The next several sessions should be spent on the working alliance rather than on the presenting problem, and the goal should be the explicit and practical one of establishing predictability: the same time, the same room, the same ten minutes at the start. The outcome measure should be the Session Rating Scale, which asks the young person to rate the session on four dimensions, and which — as the practitioner should tell her — is a tool she can use if the sessions are not working. Making the feedback instrument available to the client is a specific, and here highly indicated, application of the feedback principle.

**The rupture that will come.** Somewhere in the fifth to eighth session, a rupture will occur, and its most likely content is the father. The case material makes it a reasonable prediction that the withdrawal began in earnest after the father's schedule changed to include more evening work and the mother began her training course, and that the school move and the end of swimming are coincident rather than causal. The rupture will probably arrive as a complaint about the practitioner — that she is a waste of time, that she does not understand, that the sessions are pointless — or as an abrupt cancellation. This is the moment the sequence is for. The practitioner should not retreat, and should not interpret the father's schedule as the content. The content of the rupture is the relationship; the father's schedule is the material from which the young person is building the current interaction.

**The systemic caution.** The mother's presence in the first session and the report that "she was always quiet" are both significant, and the systemic reading — that Priya's silence is a function of a family in which one member has become the spokesperson for the whole — should be held in mind and should be examined, but not now. Attempting a systemic intervention in the first three sessions with a young person who has already experienced one service that did not listen to her is a second violation, and a more damaging one. The relationship comes first.

**The ethical considerations.** Three issues are engaged. The first is confidentiality: Priya will need to know precisely what is shared with the school, and the practical answer in a school-based service is that information is shared with the school only with her agreement, and that the practitioner is required to pass on information where there is a risk of serious harm. The second is the limits of the school's request: the school has asked for a "non-engagement" intervention, and the practitioner's obligation is to Priya's interests, not to the school's administrative convenience. The third is the question of whether the previous service's approach constitutes a failure of care that warrants a complaint, and whether the practitioner has any obligation to raise it. The professional answer is that the practitioner has a duty to the young person in the room, and that the duty includes not repeating the failure, but that there is generally no duty to initiate an inquiry into a previous service unless the young person or a third party has raised a safeguarding concern.

## 11. Evidence and Research Support

**The alliance–outcome relationship is among the best established findings in the field.** The strength of the evidence is unusual and has survived three decades of methodological criticism, including the demonstration that the correlation is not entirely attributable to a third variable.

**Rupture is common and its management is consequential.** The research literature indicates that ruptures occur in a substantial majority of episodes, that they are more frequent in the early sessions, that they are more common in longer treatments, and that successful resolution is associated with better outcome. The finding that the experience of rupture and repair can itself be therapeutic — that a rupture successfully worked through deepens the alliance and is remembered by clients as a turning point — is consistent across the psychodynamic, integrative, and person-centred literatures.

**Specific techniques add to the relationship.** Component studies and dismantling studies indicate that specific procedures account for outcomes over and above the relationship in conditions for which they are indicated. The dismantling of the components of behavioural and cognitive treatments for anxiety, depression, and conduct problems has been extensive, and in each case the removal of the specific component reduces the outcome for that component's target. The implication is that the relationship is necessary and not sufficient.

**Feedback improves outcomes.** The literature on routine outcome monitoring in routine services is unusually consistent: practitioners who receive structured feedback on their clients' progress produce better outcomes than those who do not, and the effect is larger when the feedback is provided systematically and repeatedly than when it is provided once. The effect is present in the adult literature, is smaller but detectable in the adolescent literature, and is generally larger in services where staff are less experienced.

**The limits of the evidence.** Much of the alliance research has been conducted with adults, with self-report measures that are vulnerable to social desirability, and with a focus on the alliance as a unitary construct rather than on its components, whose contributions may differ. And the developmental literature on the alliance with young people, while growing, remains thinner than the adult literature, which means that some of the specific claims made in this chapter about adolescents are extrapolations supported by developmental theory rather than by direct evidence.

## 12. Strengths and Limitations of a Relationship-Centered Account

**Strengths.** It identifies the condition under which all other work becomes possible. It gives the practitioner a diagnostic instrument that is usable in the moment and does not require a formal assessment. It integrates across theoretical orientations, so that a practitioner who changes orientation does not lose the core skill. It is teachable in a way that most clinical concepts are not. And it supplies a mechanism for continuous learning, because the rupture carries information about the work as it is actually being experienced.

**Limitations.** The relationship is not directly manipulable: the practitioner cannot simply decide to relate well, and the literature gives no reliable manual for producing a good alliance. The relationship can be over-valued, producing a practice in which everything is negotiated and nothing is challenged. The emphasis on the relationship can obscure the need for structure, for challenge, and for the discomfort that some young people need in order to change. And the research base is dominated by adult samples, so the specific claims about adolescents are less well established than the field's confident tone suggests.

## 13. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**Culture and the alliance.** The alliance is not a universal. Studies of the therapeutic alliance across cultures have found that the alliance–outcome relationship holds in many cultural settings, but that the *expression* of a good alliance varies, and that some of the components Western instruments treat as essential are less uniformly valued across cultures. In settings with strong family or collective orientation, the young person's expectation may be that the practitioner will speak to the family rather than to the young person; the Western assumption of individual confidentiality can itself be a rupture. And where mistrust of institutions is a well-founded response to the young person's experience — with the mental health system, with schools, with authorities — the practitioner begins the work already behind. Chapter 48 addresses the power differential in detail, and Chapter 49 addresses the specific dynamics with LGBTQ+ youth.

**The ethical asymmetry of work with minors.** Three features of the relationship with a young person make it ethically unlike the relationship with an adult. The young person's consent is complicated by the legal requirement of guardian permission, which produces a situation in which a person who cannot lawfully consent to the therapy is nonetheless in the room and is being asked to be honest in it. The practitioner holds more power than in adult work, and the power is institutional as well as personal. And the young person is more likely to be harmed by a breach, and less likely to have the resources to report one.

**Three obligations follow.** First, the practitioner must be scrupulous about confidentiality and must explain, concretely and in advance, what will and will not be shared, with whom, and why. A young person who does not know the answer to "who else knows what I say?" cannot give meaningful consent to anything. Second, the practitioner must not allow the therapeutic relationship to become the only relationship in the young person's life, and must actively work toward the young person's connections with peers, with family, and with the ordinary institutions of their daily life. Third, the practitioner must anticipate the exploitation risk and manage the setting accordingly: no closed doors, no touch, no sessions in the practitioner's car, no private electronic messaging channels, no gifts, and a clear statement to the young person and the guardian about the boundaries that apply.

**The last of these is worth a further word.** A young person who has been harmed by an adult will often test the boundary, and the test may be subtle and may be a bid for contact rather than an attempt at exploitation. The practitioner's response should be warm, unpanicked, and absolutely clear, and the practitioner should not be discouraged by the discomfort of delivering it.

## 14. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Therapeutic alliance** — The collaborative and affective bond between practitioner and client, including the shared commitment to the work; the construct most consistently associated with outcome.
- **Working alliance** — The task-focused component of the therapeutic relationship: the shared agreement about the goals and the division of labour.
- **Rapport** — Interpersonal ease and mutual liking; the most superficial component of the relationship and the one most often mistaken for the alliance.
- **Therapeutic bond** — The affective component of the relationship: the sense of a genuine, caring, and reliable connection.
- **Necessary condition** — A factor without which the mechanism of change cannot operate; the relation of the alliance to technique.
- **Rupture** — A breakdown in the working alliance, overt or covert, in which the collaborative frame is interrupted.
- **Covert rupture** — A rupture expressed through lateness, minimal responding, withdrawal, or other indirect behaviour rather than through open conflict.
- **Repair** — The process by which a ruptured collaborative frame is restored.
- **Displacement** — The transfer of affect belonging to a relationship onto a less threatening target; in the clinical setting, the sign that a rupture is occurring.
- **Parallel process** — The systemic hypothesis that the therapeutic relationship enacts the client's other relationships; the observation that what happens in the room reflects what happens elsewhere.
- **Enactment** — The repetition, in the therapeutic relationship, of a relational pattern formed elsewhere.
- **Working-through** — The repeated, exploratory processing of a relational pattern across sessions with the aim of modifying it.
- **In-session rupture** — A rupture occurring in the current session, as distinct from one that has led to a cancellation, an absence, or a termination.
- **Session Rating Scale** — A brief four-item measure of session quality, including overall alliance, used for routine feedback; typically completed in under two minutes.
- **Working Alliance Inventory** — A widely used self-report measure of the working alliance with subscales for goal, task, and bond.
- **Client Outcome Questionnaire** — A brief measure of session-level progress, developed within the client-centred tradition and used in outcome monitoring.
- **Structured feedback** — The systematic provision of a client's ratings to the practitioner, shown to improve outcomes in routine services.
- **Collaborative goal setting** — The practice of constructing goals jointly, associated with stronger alliance and better outcome.
- **Interpersonal rupture** — A rupture in which the young person withdraws, complies superficially, or becomes hostile.
- **Boundary management** — The practices by which a practitioner protects a young person from exploitation in the therapeutic setting.
:::

## 15. Chapter Summary

The therapeutic relationship is a necessary condition of change and the medium through which every theory's technique operates. Its components are distinguishable: the working alliance, the therapeutic alliance, rapport, and the therapeutic bond, and the confusion of rapport with alliance is a common clinical error.

The alliance–outcome relationship is one of the most robust findings in the field, with meta-analytic correlations in the region of 0.25 to 0.30 in adult samples and smaller but detectable effects in adolescent samples. The relationship is partly causal and partly a marker of practitioner skill and of the client's circumstances, and the effect is greater for outcomes in the domain of the therapeutic focus. Specific technique contributes over and above the relationship, which is why the relationship is a necessary condition and not a sufficient cause.

Rupture is an ordinary, expectable feature of the work, and its content is the most valuable information available about the young person's experience of the therapy. Four models of rupture — psychodynamic, behavioural, humanistic, and integrative — offer different and partially competing accounts, and the differences have clinical consequences. Repair is a learnable sequence: notice, name tentatively, take your share, explore, repair the practical, return to the task, review the pattern.

The relationship with a young person is not the adult relationship. It is constrained by developmental limits on verbal and metacognitive processing, by institutional power asymmetry, by the young person's shorter time horizon, by the developmental instability of the identity the alliance is formed with, by the presence of adults who are not the client, and by the requirement that the practitioner's behaviour be consistent with what the young person has been told by other adults.

## 16. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Explain the distinction between the alliance and rapport, and give a clinical example of a young person with high rapport and a poor working alliance.
2. State the logical argument for the relationship being a necessary condition of change, and state the strongest objection to it.
3. Summarise what is known about the direction of causation in the alliance–outcome relationship. What would it take to establish the direction experimentally, and why has that not been done?
4. Describe the developmental constraints that the alliance literature places on working with adolescents, and identify which of them you think is most often overlooked.
5. Compare the psychodynamic, behavioural, and humanistic models of rupture. What does each one make possible, and what does each risk missing?
6. What is the displacement, and why is the rule "the displacement is the rupture" clinically useful?
7. Work through the repair sequence in Section 8 as it would apply to a young person who becomes angry and says "this is stupid" and wants to leave.
8. Why is it a mistake to treat a signed assent form as the completion of informed consent?
9. Identify three specific risks that attend the therapeutic relationship in work with minors, and state the practice that addresses each.
10. In what ways does the alliance in family work differ from the alliance in individual work, and what does the practitioner have to do differently?

## 17. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 5.1 — The Rupture Video (2 hours, individual, then triad)

Record a 12-minute mock session in which a student plays a fourteen-year-old who has "nothing to say." Play it back with a peer who takes the role of observer, using the Section 8 sequence as the observation framework. The observer records: the moment the rupture became recognisable; the practitioner's first response; whether the practitioner named it; whether the practitioner took a share; whether the repair was completed; and where the exchange was lost. Rotate roles so that each student has practised both the practitioner and the young person. This is the single most useful activity in the first year of the course, and the reason is that the rupture is invisible to the practitioner in the moment and visible on tape.

### Activity 5.2 — Session Rating Scale Administration (60 minutes, paired)

Practise administering the four Session Rating Scale items to a peer, covering all four dimensions and the final global item, in under two minutes, in a way that a fifteen-year-old would find unremarkable. Then administer the same items to the peer as if the peer were a distressed adolescent, and note what changes in your delivery. Finally, have the peer rate the session and discuss the discrepancy between your intention and their rating. The last step is the whole exercise: the gap between intention and experience is the rupture.

### Activity 5.3 — The Parallel Process Journal (3 hours, ongoing, 10 hours, reviewed)

For a period of ten days, keep a brief evening journal. Each entry records one interaction in which you were the more powerful party, and answers three questions: what did I want? what did I assume the other person wanted? what did the other person actually seem to want? Read the journal at the end. This exercise develops the capacity to notice one's own contribution to relational difficulty, which is the foundation of the repair sequence and the foundation of the self-examination required in Chapter 48.
:::

## 18. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory of the relationship.** Read Rogers' 1957 paper as a theory rather than as a technique, and read the work of the relational and intersubjective theorists alongside it. Then read the contemporary integrative accounts, which specify what the relationship must contain for different populations.

**For the empirical literature.** Read a meta-analysis of the alliance–outcome relationship in adults, then the corresponding adolescent literature, then a study of the directionality question. Note what the adolescent literature does and does not establish; the answer to the second question is more limited than the confident practice of the field suggests.

**For rupture and repair.** Read the Safran and Muran treatment, and then the research literature on the association between rupture resolution and outcome. Then read the developmental and cross-cultural material on rupture with young people, which is thin and growing.

**For the ethical material.** Read the sections of the professional codes that bear on boundaries, on the limits of confidentiality, and on work with minors, and then read the child welfare literature on the specific risks in therapeutic settings with young people. The codes tell you what is required; the child welfare literature tells you why the requirements are as specific as they are.
