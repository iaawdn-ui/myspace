
## 5. The Precautions

### 5.1 The ethical floor

The precautions are the practical expression of the ethics of the work, and a student should be able to state them as rules.

**Do not force the disclosure of traumatic material.** The disclosure is not the treatment. The treatment is the young person's capacity to live with their history, and a young person who is pressed to disclose has been given a second experience of powerlessness over the very material that was taken from them. The consent to the processing phase must be explicit, informed, ongoing, and revocable.

**Do not use a trauma-focused intervention with a young person who is not stabilised.** The specific protocols — the EMDR of Chapter 39, the trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy — are contraindicated in an unstabled young person, and using them is a technical error with clinical consequences.

**Do not require the young person to remain in a session in which they are outside their window of tolerance.** The session is ended, and the ending is planned rather than improvised, and the young person is not left to manage their own dysregulation alone.

**Do not treat the disclosure of abuse as the goal.** The goal is the young person's safety, their development, and their functioning. A young person who has disclosed and who is safe and who is developing has been successfully treated, whatever they have or have not disclosed.

**Do not use the trauma frame to override the young person's own account of themselves.** A young person who says they are fine and who declines to describe their history has the right to say that, and a practitioner who insists is doing so in the name of the trauma.

**Do not make the disclosure a condition of support.** This is the requirement of the safeguarding framework, and a practitioner must know the local law and the local guidance, and must be clear with the young person at the outset what the limits of confidentiality are.

### 5.2 The clinical precautions

**Do not begin with the trauma narrative.** The first task is the history, the current functioning, the strengths, the resources, and the context, and the trauma narrative is a later task.

**Do not work with the memory before the affect can be tolerated.** A young person who cannot stay present in the room cannot process a memory, and the work on the tolerance comes first.

**Do not assume the timing.** The young person who has disclosed in week two has not begun processing, and the practitioner who mistakes a disclosure for a readiness to process will do harm.

**Do not work with a young person who is still in an unsafe environment.** A young person who is returning to a house in which the abuse continues cannot be in phase two, and the practitioner who works on the memory with such a young person has misunderstood the model.

**Do not neglect the body.** The trauma is in the body, and the somatic work of Chapter 43 is frequently the work that allows the cognitive and the narrative work to proceed.

**Do not neglect the caregiver.** A young person with a trauma history whose caregiver is not able to provide safety will not be helped by the individual work, and this is the most common reason for the failure of phase-based treatment with young people.

### 5.3 The organisational precautions

The trauma-informed principle is a property of the organisation, and a student preparing for practice should know what to look for in a service and what to advocate for.

**The physical environment.** Sight lines, exits, seating that does not put a person with their back to a door, the lighting, the noise, the privacy, and the absence of waiting in a queue with the people who triggered the referral.

**The front door.** Who is greeted, by whom, and what is said. A young person who has been referred by a school and who arrives to be questioned in a waiting room with the school's staff in it has been re-traumatised before the first session.

**The consent and the information.** A young person should be told, before the first session and in writing, what will happen, what will be confidential, what the limits are, and what their choices are.

**The staffing and the supervision.** A service that works with this population and does not have specific supervision of the staff is exposing the staff.

**The language of the organisation.** The language of the service's documents, of its letters to parents, and of its reports to a school is a trauma-informed practice question, and the language of a report about a young person who has disclosed abuse can determine the young person's future.

**The avoidance of retraumatisation in the systems.** A young person with a trauma history who is moved between four different professionals in a year has been subjected to a form of retraumatisation, and continuity is a clinical intervention.

## 6. Trauma-Informed Practice with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The developmental modifications

**Safety is established through the body before it is established through the relationship.** A young person who has been abused by adults will not feel safe in a room with an adult in it, however warm the adult, and the safety that is achieved first is the physical safety: the predictable routine, the door that is not locked, the knowledge of where the exits are, the reliability of the times, and the small, kept promises.

**The relationship is the treatment and it takes longer.** The relational safety with a young person with a developmental trauma history is not established in the first month, and the practitioner should be prepared for a period in which the young person is polite and formal and does not bring anything. The period is not a failure of rapport; it is the young person testing, and the test is over months.

**The disclosure is a clinical event, not a milestone.** A disclosure should be met with a predictable, calm, and brief response, and the practitioner should have prepared what they will say in advance, because the young person will be watching the practitioner's face.

**The developmental impact is the target, not the memory.** A young person does not need to remember the event in order to be affected by it, and the treatment of the developmental consequences — the regulation, the attachment, the self-concept, the developmental tasks — is often the more important work.

**The work with the caregivers is central.** Where a young person remains with a caregiver, the caregiver's capacity to provide safety is the determinant of the outcome, and the practitioner who works only with the young person is working against the grain.

**The work is developmental in its tasks.** The trauma work with an adolescent includes the developmental tasks of Part V: the differentiation, the peer relationships, the identity, the academic engagement, and the future. A young person whose trauma is processed and who is then not helped to build a life will not stay well.

### 6.2 The presentations

**The young person with a chronic somatic presentation.** The somatic work of Chapter 43 is the entry point, and the somatic presentation is often the most legitimate and least frightening route into the treatment.

**The young person with a dissociation presentation.** The dorsal vagal state is the clinical fact, and the practitioner should be able to recognise it and should not attempt to work with the content while the young person is in it.

**The young person with an attachment-based presentation.** The attachment work of Chapter 34 and the mentalisation work of Chapter 42 are the natural approaches, and both are compatible with the trauma work.

**The young person with an eating presentation.** The family-based approach and the emotion-focused work of Chapter 37 are both relevant, and the somatic and the trauma work may be necessary.

**The young person in a care placement.** The placement is the trauma, and the clinical task is the stability of the placement and the availability of a consistent adult.

### 6.3 The specific working with young people who have been sexually abused

This presentation requires its own rules, and a student should know them.

**The assessment of the safety is the first and the most important task**, and it is ongoing.

**The parent's role must be established explicitly**, because the parent may be the perpetrator, and the practitioner must know which before the work begins.

**The do-not-contact orders and the supervision plans** are the framework in which the work is done, and the practitioner must understand them.

**The young person's own account of the abuse is not the only evidence and does not need to be complete.** The clinical work does not require the young person to have told the story in detail, and the pressure to disclose a detailed narrative is a pressure that the ethical frameworks explicitly reject.

**The behavioural manifestations must not be over-interpreted.** A young person who is hypervigilant, who is intrusive, who has sexualised behaviour beyond what is age-typical, who is oppositional, or who is enuretic, is presenting a set of responses to an abnormal environment, and the responses are not evidence of the young person's character.

**The father's role** requires particular care, and the professional position is that the involvement of a non-offending parent is generally beneficial and the exclusion of a non-offending parent is generally harmful, subject to the specific facts of the case and the local law.

## 7. Case Illustration: The Boy Who Could Not Be in the Room

**Presentation.** Samuel is twelve and is referred by a school safeguarding lead after a disclosure made to a teaching assistant about a stepfather. He has not been in school for eleven weeks. The referral states that he is "non-compliant with school attendance" and that his mother is "not engaging".

**The first contact.** The practitioner does not make the first contact by telephone with the mother. The first contact is a letter to the mother, and the letter says what the practitioner can and cannot do, and it says that the practitioner will also be writing to Samuel, and that the practitioner would like to speak with Samuel directly and separately. This is a standard and important procedural point: the young person is contacted directly and is not dependent on the adult who may be implicated.

**The direct contact.** Samuel does not respond to the letter, and the practitioner does not press. Three weeks later, a letter arrives, handwritten, containing four words: "I don't want to talk." The practitioner treats this as communication rather than as refusal, and the reply is short: "That's okay. I won't ask you to talk about anything you don't want to. I would like to meet you somewhere you feel okay, and to know what would help. You can reply to this letter with anything, or with nothing."

**The first meeting.** Samuel comes, with his mother, and the practitioner arranges the meeting in a room that is not the clinical room: a small room in the community centre with a window and a door that is not locked, and two chairs at an angle rather than across a desk. The practitioner says at the outset: "I am not going to ask you about your stepfather. Not today and not unless you want to. I am going to ask you about your week, and about what would make school better, and about what you need. You can stop at any time, and we can stop the whole thing, and if you stop, that is not a problem."

Samuel's dorsal vagal state is evident: he is flat, he is monosyllabic, and he does not look at the practitioner. The practitioner does not attempt rapport. She asks about the walk to the community centre, and the answer is that the walk is twenty minutes and he comes alone. The practitioner asks what he sees on the walk, and he names three specific things. The work in this session is entirely orienting, and the practitioner does not attempt any of it, and the session is twelve minutes long, and it ends with a specific arrangement: the same time and the same place next week.

**The phase one work.** The next three months are phase one, and the work is unglamorous and is the work that makes everything else possible.

**The physical safety.** A written safety plan, with the specific details of who Samuel lives with, when he is with whom, and what happens if his stepfather is in the house. The plan is shared with the safeguarding lead and with the mother's social worker, and the practitioner asks the mother directly, in a way that is careful and clear, whether Samuel's stepfather lives in the home. The mother says that he does not, and the practitioner asks what the arrangement is, and the mother says that he comes on Sundays, and the practitioner asks whether Samuel is ever in the house on Sundays, and the mother says that Samuel stays at his grandmother's on Sundays, and the practitioner asks who arranges that, and the mother says that she does. The practitioner makes no judgement and records the account and continues.

**The practical problems.** The school attendance. Samuel has not been in school for eleven weeks and the referral calls this non-compliance. The practitioner asks what school was like before the referral and finds that Samuel was a good attender until the week of the disclosure, and that the school has not contacted him since. The school has done nothing. The practitioner discovers that the form tutor did not know what had happened and did not know that Samuel was distressed, and the practitioner makes the case to the school for a reintegration plan with a named adult, and the school agrees, and the first step is that Samuel comes to the school for one period with his form tutor, and the school is told that the "non-compliance" label is to be removed from the record and replaced with "ceased attendance following a safeguarding referral", and the school agrees.

**The grounding and the stabilisation.** The grounding work of Chapter 40 is taught explicitly, in the sessions, and it is taught as a set of practical skills for a twelve-year-old. The emotional regulation skills are taught before the disclosure work and are taught to the mother as well.

**The relational work.** The sessions are the same time and the same place, and the practitioner reliably turns up, and for six weeks Samuel says almost nothing beyond single words, and the practitioner does not fill the silence and does not comment on it. In the seventh week Samuel says, without preamble: "There's a thing on Sundays." The practitioner says: "Okay." And he tells the practitioner, over the following four sessions, what happens on Sundays, and the practitioner does not stop him, and the practitioner does not ask questions, and when he finishes the practitioner says: "Thank you for telling me. That was hard to say." And nothing else.

**The work with the mother.** The practitioner's work with the mother is the necessary work, and it is the work that most affects the outcome. The mother is shown the "what is your child worried about" material, and she is taught the grounding skills, and the practitioner asks her the question that the whole case turns on: "What did you think was happening on Sundays?" And the mother says, after a long silence: "I thought he was just being difficult." And the practitioner says: "He was telling you something. He was trying to get you to notice." And the mother cries, and the practitioner stays with her, and the mother's relationship with the safeguarding system — and with the practitioners who have been involved — is repaired a little, and the mother's participation in the plan changes entirely.

**The outcome.** At six months, Samuel is back in school four days a week, has a named adult at school, is seeing his grandmother on alternate Sundays with his mother, and has a written safety plan that he helped to write. The stepfather has not had contact with him. The mother is engaged with the social worker and with the practitioner. The trauma processing work has not yet begun, and the practitioner says clearly that it may not be needed in the form the phase model assumes, and that the work has been the building of a life that is not organised around the secret.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the trauma-informed principle in its pure form. Nothing in it was a trauma-processing intervention. The interventions were: the direct contact with the young person; the removal of the "non-compliant" label from the record; the addressing of the school's failure to re-engage him; the explicit safety assessment; the building of a predictable, bodily, non-verbal relational safety; the work with the mother, which was the decisive intervention; and the careful, unhurried reception of the disclosure when it came. The young person was never asked to talk before he wanted to, and the disclosure that eventually came was the result of eleven weeks of predictability, not of a technique.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The status of the evidence

**The evidence for the individual components is good.** The specific trauma-focused protocols — the trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy and the EMDR — have strong evidence bases, and the first is the best-evidenced treatment for the trauma symptoms of children and adolescents.

**The evidence for the phase model as an integrated package is thin**, and a student should be candid about this. The phases have not been tested as a package in controlled trials.

**The evidence for the stabilisation skills is more limited and more developmental.** The grounding and the stabilisation work of Chapter 40 has a smaller and more recently developed evidence base, and the strongest evidence is for the general principle that the reduction of arousal precedes the cognitive work.

**The evidence for the trauma-informed service-level reforms is largely indirect**, and the field has relied on the plausibility of the principles, on the plausibility of the mechanisms, and on the consistent finding that the reforms improve the experience of service users and the well-being of the staff.

**The evidence for the principles themselves is now much stronger than it was.** The large studies of the health and educational outcomes of adversity, and of the dose-response relationships between the number of adverse experiences and the outcomes, and of the protective effect of a stable adult, have given the principles their epidemiological foundation, and a student should know the shape of this evidence even though the principles are not themselves interventions.

**The evidence for the specific model of the Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up and the CFTSI is good**, and the video-feedback mechanism is understood.

### 8.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The heterogeneity of the interventions makes the meta-analytic findings difficult to interpret**, and the pooled effects conceal substantial variation.

**The samples are dominated by the children of the adoptive or foster care system**, and the young people who are in the general population and who have been exposed to community violence, to domestic violence at a level that does not trigger a referral, and to the loss of a parent, are under-represented.

**The outcome measures are almost entirely symptom measures**, and the developmental outcomes, the educational outcomes, and the quality of life outcomes are much less well documented.

**The fidelity of the phased delivery is rarely measured**, and this is a significant weakness given that the phases are the model's principal mechanism.

**The long-term follow-up is limited**, and the developmental question — whether the gains are maintained into the adult years — is largely unanswered.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Trauma-Informed Practice

### 9.1 Strengths

**It starts with what happened.** The reorientation from the disorder to the history is the most important single change in the field's practice in forty years.

**It is developmentally sound.** The child-is-not-a-small-adult principle has corrected a substantial error.

**It has an evidence base of the right kind.** The epidemiological evidence is strong and the intervention evidence is good for the components.

**It is compatible with every other approach in this book.** A stabilisation phase is compatible with cognitive therapy, with the behavioural work, with the attachment work, with the mentalisation work, and with the somatic work.

**It has changed services.** The principle has influenced the physical environment, the front door, the consent procedures, and the language of the documentation, and these are not trivial changes.

**It has changed the professional self-conception.** The practitioner's role in this model is not primarily to interpret but to support.

### 9.2 Limitations

**It can individualise a structural problem.** This is the most serious criticism and it is addressed in Section 10.

**The definition of trauma is broad and contested**, and a framework that includes everything risks including nothing.

**The phase model can become a way of avoiding the work**, and the young person can be kept in phase one indefinitely.

**The model presupposes that safety can be established**, and in some circumstances it cannot, and the practitioner is then left without a model.

**The culture of the field has become strongly trauma-informed without a corresponding evidence base for many of its specific practices**, and the enthusiasm has sometimes outrun the technique.

**The risk of the "trauma narrative" becoming the young person's identity.** A young person who has been told for two years that they are a trauma survivor and who has been asked to tell the story repeatedly can acquire an identity organised entirely around the trauma, and this is the same error as the diagnostic identity and it is less visible.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 The individualisation of a structural problem

The most serious ethical danger of the trauma-informed principle is that it locates the young person's difficulties in their history, and that this can be used, and is sometimes used, to explain away the conditions in which they live.

A young person who is in an unsafe school, who is in temporary accommodation, whose family is in poverty, who is being treated as a problem by the adults around them, and who has a history of a difficult event, does not need the history to be addressed first. The history is the reason the conditions are what they are, and the conditions are what the young person is currently living in, and the treatment of the conditions is the treatment.

Three safeguards follow.

**Always ask what the young person is living in.** The question "what is happening in your life right now" is as important as "what happened to you", and it is more often actionable.

**Never let the trauma frame substitute for the structural one.** If the young person's difficulty is a product of the conditions, the conditions must be changed, and the frame of the work is a fight about those conditions and not a journey into the past.

**Keep the history in its place.** The trauma-informed principle does not require the trauma to be the centre of the treatment. In many cases the best work is the establishment of a good enough life in the present, and the young person may never need to process the memory at all.

### 10.2 Culture, trauma, and the meaning of the event

**The meaning of an event is culturally constituted.** The same event — a parent's violence, a community's response to a young person's behaviour, a sibling's death, a migration — means different things in different contexts, and a practitioner who applies a universal account of the traumatic event will misdescribe the young person's experience.

**Some events are not traumatic for the young person who experienced them.** The field's framing requires that the impact be assessed in the individual, and a young person who experienced a frightening event and was not frightened by it should not be given a trauma narrative to inhabit.

**The "trauma" of a community and a culture is a different order of thing** from the trauma of an individual, and the collective, historical, and political injuries of a community are not adequately addressed by an individualised model, and a young person whose community has been subjected to violence may be carrying a collective trauma that requires a collective response.

**Culturally specific healing practices** are frequently more available to a young person and more effective than the individual trauma work, and the practitioner should ask about them before assuming that the individual work is the route.

### 10.3 Ethical requirements

**Informed, ongoing, and revocable consent** to each phase and to each intervention within it.

**The young person as the decision-maker.** The principles require the voice and the choice of the young person, and this must be real rather than nominal, and the practitioner must be willing to accept a decision they disagree with.

**The avoidance of compulsory disclosure.** A young person who declines to disclose has the right to do so and has been treated well.

**The limits of confidentiality stated at the outset**, in writing, and the young person must know before they disclose what will happen.

**The avoidance of retraumatisation by the systems**, including the avoidance of unnecessary assessments, of repeated accounts, of unnecessary moves between professionals, and of intrusive procedures.

**The avoidance of the use of the trauma identity.** The young person's account of themselves is not to be replaced by a professional narrative about them.

**Cultural and historical humility.** The practitioner acknowledges the limits of their own framework and the existence of other frameworks in the young person's life.

**The obligations of the professional to the staff.** A service that works with this population has an obligation to supervise the staff and to care for their own dysregulation, and the neglect of this is a clinical error with clinical consequences.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Trauma-informed practice** — A stance in which a service is organised around the understanding that many of the people it serves have been traumatised, and in which the question of what has happened precedes the question of what is wrong.
- **The four core principles** — Safety, trustworthiness and transparency, peer support, and collaboration and mutuality.
- **The six principles of the SAMHSA framework** — The four core principles plus the recognition of cultural, historical, and gender issues and the commitment to empowerment, voice, and choice.
- **The four R's** — Recognise, respond, refer, and re-educate.
- **Trauma-informed organisation** — The service-level reforms in the environment, the front door, the consent procedures, the staffing, and the language of the documentation.
- **Dorsal vagal state** — The immobilised or shut-down state, whose signs are easily mistaken for defiance and in which no cognitive or emotional work is possible.
- **Sympathetic state** — The fight-or-flight state, in which the arousal must be reduced before any content is addressed.
- **Ventral vagal state** — The social engagement state, in which calm and connection are possible and in which the cognitive, emotional, and narrative work can proceed.
- **Window of tolerance** — The range of arousal within which a person can function and in which information can be processed; above it is hyperarousal and below it is hypoarousal.
- **The narrowing of the window** — The developmental and clinical consequence of chronic threat, and the reason that the preparatory work is a precondition for the rest.
- **Developmentally traumatised** — The presentation produced by chronic, relational, repeated threat during development, as distinct from a single-incident trauma.
- **The three-phase model** — Safety and stabilisation, trauma processing, and integration and rehabilitation.
- **Phase one** — The establishment of safety in its broadest sense, the reduction of symptoms, the teaching of coping and grounding, the building of relational safety, and the resolution of practical problems.
- **Phase two** — The deliberate, consented, and carefully paced work on the traumatic memories and their meanings.
- **Phase three** — The consolidation of the changes, the rebuilding of the young person's life, and the re-engagement with education, relationships, and the future.
- **The criteria for phase movement** — The explicit conditions for moving forward and for moving back, and the practical value of the backward criteria.
- **The ethical floor rules** — The prohibitions on forcing disclosure, on using a trauma-focused intervention with an unstabled young person, on requiring a young person to remain outside their window of tolerance, on treating the disclosure as the goal, and on overriding the young person's own account.
- **The single-incident and developmental trauma distinction** — The difference between the adult single-event model and the pattern that produces a different presentation in a child.
- **The developmental trauma criteria** — The criteria proposed for the developmental trauma category, and the debate about whether they belong in a diagnostic manual.
- **The dose-response finding** — The epidemiological finding that the number of adverse experiences is related to the range of health and educational outcomes, and that the relationship is graded.
- **The stable adult as a protective factor** — The single most important protective factor against the impact of developmental trauma, and the reason the work with the caregivers is central.
- **Retraumatisation by the systems** — The retraumatisation that occurs through repeated assessments, compulsory and repeated accounts, intrusive procedures, and unnecessary moves between professionals.
- **The trauma identity** — The risk that a young person who has been told repeatedly that they are a trauma survivor acquires an identity organised entirely around the trauma.
- **Collective trauma** — The historical, political, and communal injuries that an individualised model does not adequately address.
- **The practitioner as a container** — The role of the practitioner in holding the young person's arousal and affect, and the supervision and self-care that the role requires.
- **The five ways of noticing** — The clinician's attentional practice of noticing the young person's state before noticing their content.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Trauma-informed practice is a stance rather than a therapy, and its most consequential feature is that it begins with what has happened to a person rather than with what is wrong with them — a difference that changes decisions about waiting lists, physical environments, consent, staff behaviour, and documentation. The chapter traced the three developments that made the principle possible: the neurobiological and epidemiological research, the recognition that the adult-derived trauma treatments had been misapplied to children, and the political recognition that the systems treating trauma had frequently caused it.

The four core principles and the six-point framework were stated, with the observation that the fourth, collaboration and mutuality, is the one most often confused with its opposite, because much of what is described as trauma-informed is done to the young person rather than with them. The developmental context was developed: the plasticity and the vulnerability of the developing nervous system, the "child is not a small adult" principle, the distinction between single-incident and developmental trauma, and the cumulative and mediated nature of the developmental impact.

The threat response system was described with the three states and their signs, and the clinically decisive point was made: the client's state determines what intervention is possible, and a great many clinical failures with this population are failures of state rather than of technique. The three clinical rules were stated for the dorsal vagal, the sympathetic, and the ventral vagal states, and the window of tolerance was described with the observation that the preparatory work of Chapter 40 is a substantial part of the treatment rather than a preliminary to it.

The three-phase model was described with its structure, its explicit criteria for movement in both directions, and its four criticisms, including the criticism that the phases can become a way of avoiding the difficult work and that the model presupposes a safety that cannot always be established.

The precautions were organised into the ethical floor, the clinical precautions, and the organisational precautions, and the organisational section is unusual in a chapter of this book in treating the principle as a property of the organisation rather than of the practitioner. The developmental modifications were described, with the direct contact with the young person and the parent's role as the two that are frequently got wrong, and the specific working with young people who have been sexually abused was treated in its own subsection with its own rules.

The case of Samuel demonstrated the principle in its pure form, and the practitioner never conducted a trauma-processing intervention. The interventions were the direct contact without the implicated adult as intermediary, the removal of the "non-compliant" label from the school record, the school re-engagement, the explicit safety assessment, the twelve-minute orienting first meeting, the grounding work, the unhurried reception of the disclosure when it came, and the work with the mother — which was the decisive intervention, and which was turned by a single question about what the mother thought had been happening on Sundays.

The evidence was presented with its structure: good evidence for the individual components, thin evidence for the phase model as a package, more limited evidence for the stabilisation skills, largely indirect evidence for the service-level reforms, and strong epidemiological evidence for the principles. The limitations were named.

The strengths and limitations were set out, with the individualisation of a structural problem identified as the most serious, and the trauma identity as the error that is less visible than the diagnostic identity and the same in kind.

The cultural analysis addressed the culturally constituted meaning of the event, the young person for whom the event was not traumatic, the collective and historical injuries that an individualised model does not reach, and the culturally specific healing practices that may be more available and more effective. The ethical requirements covered the consent, the young person as decision-maker, the prohibition of compulsory disclosure, the limits of confidentiality stated in advance, the avoidance of retraumatisation by the systems, the avoidance of the trauma identity, cultural and historical humility, and the obligations of the professional to the staff.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 38.1 — The State Audit (90 minutes, field-based)

In a placement, observe a young person in three different settings and record, for each, their state: dorsal vagal, sympathetic, or ventral vagal. Record the physical evidence. Then record what the adults in each setting did in response. Most students find that the adults respond to the dorsal vagal state with the interventions that are appropriate to the sympathetic state, and the exercise is a more effective argument for the three-state model than any reading.

### Activity 38.2 — The Phase Placement (75 minutes, individual)

Take a case from placement and place the young person in one of the three phases, with the specific evidence for the placement. Then write the criteria: what would have to be true for them to move forward, and what would have to be true for them to move back. Then write the specific intervention that is indicated *now* and only now. The final step is the discipline of the model and it is the step that prevents the phases becoming a way of avoiding the work.

### Activity 38.3 — The Consent Conversation (90 minutes, pairs)

One student plays a young person who has disclosed and who is frightened of what will happen next; the other plays the practitioner. The practitioner must explain, in plain language, what will happen, what will be shared, with whom, and what the young person's choices are. Then reverse roles. The exercise is difficult and the difficulty is the point: most practitioners over-explain and under-inform, and the young person usually understands the first attempt better than the practitioner expects.

### Activity 38.4 — The Structural Audit (60 minutes, individual)

Take a case with a trauma history and write two accounts. The first is the trauma account: what happened, and what it produced. The second is the structural account: the housing, the income, the school, the family's circumstances, the community, the availability of a safe adult. Then write what proportion of the young person's current difficulty the two accounts explain, and write the interventions that follow from each. Then write the priority. The exercise is the most important one in the chapter and the one most often skipped in practice.

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the principles.** Read the National Child Traumatic Stress Network's conceptual paper and the SAMHSA framework, and read the National Child Traumatic Stress Network's practitioner guide. These are short and they are the definitional documents.

**For the science.** Read van der Kolk (2014) for the neurobiological account, Porges (2011) for the polyvagal framework, and Siegel (2010) for the attachment and regulatory account. The three together are the theoretical basis of the practice.

**For the clinical model.** Read the Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy manual and the EMDR manual, and read the child and family traumatic stress intervention manual. These are the specific protocols and they are more useful than the general accounts.

**For the developmental adaptation.** Read the developmental trauma literature and the work on the child-is-not-a-small-adult principle, and read the child advocacy centre model, which is a service-level application.

**For the critique.** Read the critical literature on the individualisation of structural problems, on the expansion of the definition of trauma, and on the trauma identity. The field's own self-criticism is essential and it is developing.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is the neurobiology of threat, the three-phase model, and the ethical floor rules of trauma work. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work. The mechanism to keep in view is how threat, arousal, avoidance, meaning, and relational safety shape what a young person can feel, remember, and learn. The characteristic professional risk is moving into traumatic material before stabilisation, confusing calm with shutdown, or making disclosure the price of care. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in stabilisation sessions, trauma-informed schools and services, caregiver work, and specialist treatment pathways, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Jonas is fifteen and is seen in an integrated school and health team for conflict with parents about autonomy. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Jonas's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Jonas explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Jonas chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Jonas has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Jonas can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
