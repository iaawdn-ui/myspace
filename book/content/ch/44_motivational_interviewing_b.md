
## 7. Working with Adolescent Ambivalence

Adolescents are developing autonomy under conditions of continued dependence. Adults control money, transport, school access, health care, privacy, and legal status. An invitation to “choose” may therefore be experienced sceptically. The counsellor should be explicit about what is negotiable and what is not.

Safety requirements are not preferences to be evoked away. If a young person is at serious risk, the practitioner acts protectively, explains why, and preserves as much choice as possible inside the safety frame: who is told first, where a conversation happens, which trusted adult is present, and what the young person wants recorded.

Mandated clients require honesty. A school may require attendance at a programme; a court may require treatment; a parent may require counselling. MI distinguishes the mandated frame from the young person's goals within it. “You didn't choose to be referred. Given that you're here, what—if anything—would make this time useful to you?” This respects reality without pretending coercion is consent.

## 8. Key Techniques

### 8.1 Importance and confidence rulers

“On a scale from 0 to 10, how important is improving attendance?” Then ask why the number is not lower, which evokes strengths and reasons, and what would move it one point higher. Confidence rulers follow the same pattern. Avoid asking first why the number is not higher; that invites barriers and defence.

### 8.2 Looking back and looking forward

Looking back recalls life before the problem or previous successes. Looking forward imagines a preferred future and its practical details. These are not fantasies; they generate discrepancy between values and current trajectory.

### 8.3 Exploring values

Sport, loyalty, freedom, competence, family respect, creativity, faith-neutral personal commitments, independence, and being trusted often matter more than abstract health warnings. The counsellor listens for values without exploiting them manipulatively.

### 8.4 Double-sided reflections

“You want your mum off your back, and you also hate lying because it makes you feel trapped.” A double-sided reflection holds both sides and often ends with change talk so the young person can respond to it.

### 8.5 Emphasising autonomy

“It really is your decision” is powerful only when true within the stated frame. Used falsely, it becomes coercion with softer language.

## 9. Case Illustration: “I'm Not Bothered About School”

**Presentation.** Malik, fifteen, attends school two days a week. Staff describe him as capable but unmotivated. His mother wants the counsellor to “make him understand” that examinations determine his future.

**Engaging.** The counsellor begins: “You didn't choose this referral. What would make these meetings not a waste of your time?” Malik says, “If you don't lecture me.” The counsellor reflects, “You've had enough adults explaining your future at you.” He nods.

**Focusing.** Options are mapped: sleep, lessons, teachers, friends, coursework, home conflict. Malik chooses “mornings” as the least annoying focus.

**Evoking.** Importance of attending more is rated 4. Asked why not 1, Malik says he wants an apprenticeship and knows attendance is checked. Confidence is 3 because waking feels “impossible” and first lesson is with a teacher who publicly returns his work. Change talk emerges around competence and freedom: a reference from school would help him earn money and leave a neighbourhood conflict behind.

Sustain talk is explored respectfully. Staying home protects him from embarrassment and lets him help his grandmother. The counsellor reflects both sides rather than characterising home as avoidance.

**Information exchange.** With permission, the counsellor explains that the apprenticeship provider asks for attendance patterns but that late improvement still matters. Malik asks whether arriving for second lesson “counts for anything”; together they check school policy rather than speculate.

**Planning.** Malik chooses a two-week experiment: attend Tuesday to Friday from second lesson, sleep routine on school nights, phone charging downstairs on two nights he selects, and a private arrangement with the teacher to receive written feedback before any public comment. His mother agrees not to deliver morning lectures, replacing them with one agreed prompt and transport money placed by the door.

**Review.** Attendance rises to 70 per cent. More importantly, Malik describes himself as “testing whether I can do it”, not obeying the counsellor. When a setback follows a classroom humiliation, the process returns to evoking rather than blame.

## 10. Evidence and Research Support

Meta-analyses support MI for substance use and several health behaviours, with generally small to moderate effects that vary by outcome, fidelity, population, and comparison condition. Evidence with adolescents is promising for alcohol and substance use, treatment engagement, health behaviour, and some school applications, but heterogeneous.

MI is often brief and can enhance other treatments. It is not, by itself, sufficient treatment for severe depression, trauma, psychosis, eating disorders, or entrenched conduct problems. Effects decay when practitioners drift into persuasion, when fidelity is low, or when the environment makes chosen change practically impossible.

Training requires practice and feedback. Reading about empathy does not produce reflective listening. Audio review, coding, supervision, and coaching are associated with better skill retention than one-off workshops.

## 11. Strengths, Limitations, and Ethics

Strengths include respect for autonomy, usefulness at treatment entry, compatibility with other models, and attention to language as a mechanism of change. MI helps practitioners notice their own contribution to resistance.

Limitations include potential overfocus on individual choice where structural barriers dominate. A young person cannot choose safe housing into existence, make an inaccessible curriculum accessible, or remove discriminatory discipline by motivation alone. MI must therefore be paired with advocacy and practical support.

Ethically, practitioners must not use MI techniques to manufacture consent, conceal institutional goals, or evade safeguarding. Affirmations must be genuine. Reflections must not twist meaning. Summaries must not erase sustain talk or dissent. The young person should know the counsellor's role, reporting obligations, and any non-negotiable requirements.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Ambivalence** — Simultaneous reasons for and against change.
- **Change talk** — Client language favouring change.
- **Sustain talk** — Client language favouring the status quo or opposing change.
- **Fixing reflex** — The practitioner's premature urge to solve and persuade.
- **Elicit–provide–elicit** — A respectful sequence for exchanging information.
- **Agenda mapping** — Collaborative identification and prioritisation of possible change topics.
- **Epistemic humility in MI** — Recognition that the client is the primary expert on their values and life.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Motivational interviewing treats resistance less as a client trait than as a signal about the conversation. By partnering with adolescents, reflecting ambivalence, evoking their own reasons and values, and supporting feasible plans, the counsellor makes change speakable without making it compulsory. Its discipline is ethical as well as technical: influence must remain transparent, autonomy real, and safeguarding honest.

4. Why ask why an importance rating is not lower before asking what would raise it?
5. How should MI be adapted for a mandated adolescent client?
6. When is advice-giving compatible with MI?

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For foundations.** Miller and Rollnick's fourth edition is the most current comprehensive statement.

**For adolescents.** Naar and Suarez provide developmental and family adaptations, including work with caregivers.

**For schools.** Rollnick and colleagues show how brief MI conversations can be used without turning teachers into therapists.

**For evidence.** Read meta-analyses critically, noting fidelity measurement and whether MI was stand-alone or an engagement prelude.
