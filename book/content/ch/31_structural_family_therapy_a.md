# Structural Family Therapy

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of the structural model within the family therapy movement and state the clinical problems that produced it.
- Define the five key structural concepts: structure, subsystems, boundaries, hierarchy, and family projection process.
- Explain the concept of the family structure and distinguish it from the family interaction pattern.
- Describe the structure of a family in terms of subsystems, boundaries, and hierarchy, and construct a structural map of a real family.
- Explain the concept of boundaries and their three types, and describe the boundary problems that appear in clinical work with adolescents.
- Describe the principles of structural family therapy, including the use of the structural map, the enactment, the challenge, the restructuring, and the maintenance of change.
- Apply the structural model to a case involving an adolescent and a family, and evaluate the limits of the model.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of structural family work with young people.

## Chapter Overview

The structural model was built on a clinical observation that is now so familiar it is easy to miss: **the family is a structure, and the symptoms are structural in origin.**

Salvador Minuchin and his colleagues at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic came to this through work with families of severe, entrenched, and chronic difficulty — families in which the identified patient's symptoms had been present for years, in which previous treatment had not helped, and in which the family appeared to be organised in a way that produced the symptom and resisted any change that did not alter the organisation. The structural therapists' claim was that a symptom in such a family is not an expression of intrapsychic conflict and not a learned behaviour, but a **consequence of the family's structure** — of who is in which subsystem, of where the boundaries are drawn, and of who is in charge of whom.

The model's central intervention is a **restructuring** of the family's organisation, and its central clinical skill is the **enactment**: the invitation to the family to do, in the consulting room, the thing they typically do outside it, so that the practitioner can see the structure in operation rather than hear about it. The enactment was the single most important contribution of the structural school to the technique of family therapy, and it has been adopted, in one form or another, by almost every subsequent school.

The model is a good fit for the work of this book. Adolescents present with structural problems in a way that younger children do not, because the adolescent's developmental task is a reorganisation of the family system, and the structural therapists' concepts of hierarchy, of boundary renegotiation, and of the move from the child subsystem to the adult subsystem are, in effect, descriptions of that task. The problem-solving therapy and the boundary work of the model map directly onto the developmental work of adolescence.

The model's limitations are also worth stating at the outset. The structural model is the least culturally responsive of the family therapy theories, because it assumes a nuclear family, a parental authority structure, and a set of developmental expectations that are not universal. In Chapter 48, the cultural analysis of the family therapies is taken up in full.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic

Minuchin trained as a paediatrician and as a psychiatrist, and his clinical work was at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic, where he led a training programme in family therapy that was the most rigorous in the field for two decades. The clinic's caseload was distinctive: children with severe behavioural and emotional problems, families in which the difficulty had usually been present for years, and families who had been through the child guidance and the individual therapy literature without benefit.

The clinical pressure of that caseload produced the structural theory. If a child's symptom has been present for years, has resisted individual treatment, and is embedded in a family that has developed an elaborate and effective organisation around it, then the organisation is the clinical fact, and the work must be directed at the organisation.

### 1.2 The two problems the model solved

**The problem of the "chronically ill" family.** Minuchin described a family in which a member has been designated as the sick one, and in which the family has organised itself around that designation: the routes of communication are narrowed, the parental roles are reduced, the siblings are conscripted into caretaking or into alignment with the identified patient, and the family has become a stable system whose stability depends on the continuation of the symptom. A treatment that does not restructure the system will produce a change in the symptom followed by its return, because the system has been designed to reproduce it.

**The problem of the "temporarily ill" family.** A second pattern, in which the identified patient is in a position that is transiently untenable — a child who has been assigned a role he cannot sustain, a family in which a parent has been left in charge of both the practical and the emotional management of the children — and in which the symptom will resolve when the structure is corrected.

### 1.3 The relationship to the other schools

The structural model emerged at the same time as the strategic and the Bowenian models, and the three share a premise — that the individual is not the unit of treatment — while differing in what they take to be the primary thing to change. The structural school changes the **organisation**. The strategic school changes the **sequence of interactions and the beliefs about the problem**. The Bowenian school changes the **emotional process and the differentiation**.

The distinctions are worth keeping clear because each generates a different clinical conversation. A structural conversation is about who talks to whom, who decides, and who belongs with whom. A strategic conversation is about the sequence in which things happen and about the attribution of responsibility. A Bowenian conversation is about intensity, differentiation, and the multigenerational pattern.

### 1.4 The development of the model

Minuchin's original formulation was developed with, and refined alongside, his colleagues — particularly Berry, Nagel, and others at the Philadelphia clinic — and it was given its definitive statement in Minuchin's 1974 book *Families and Family Therapy* and its structural-developmental statement in Minuchin's 1978 paper on the structural family treatment of the delinquent adolescent.

Two later developments matter for this book. The first is the **integration of the structural model with the attachment and developmental research** of the 1980s and 1990s, which supplied the developmental criteria for the tasks of the subsystems and made the model more usable with young people. The second is the **development of the structural model with families in poverty and in structurally disadvantaged circumstances**, which is the direction in which the model's assumptions have been most challenged and most usefully revised.

## 2. The Five Key Concepts

### 2.1 Structure

**Family structure** is the organisation of the family, characterised by two features.

**The structure is defined by the subsystems, the boundaries, and the hierarchy**, which are described below.

**The structure is defined by the family's pattern of interaction**, which is the observable and reportable behaviour of the family members: who talks to whom, who is included in what, who defers to whom, who initiates and who responds.

The distinction between the structure and the interaction pattern is important and is a common source of confusion. The interaction pattern is what the family does; the structure is the organisation that the pattern reflects and maintains. A family in which the father speaks to the mother and never to the daughter has an interaction pattern; the structure is the arrangement in which the father is the sole conduit of information and authority to the daughter, and in which the daughter is a member of the child subsystem that is functionally excluded from the adult subsystem.

### 2.2 Subsystems

A **subsystem** is a group of two or more people in the family who are more closely involved with each other than with the others, and which therefore develops its own rules, its own functions, and its own boundaries. Structural therapists have conventionally identified three in the nuclear family, and the terminology is worth using carefully.

**The individual subsystem** is the person.

**The sibling subsystem** is the group of siblings, and it is the unit in which the peer relations of the family are organised. In families with adolescents, the sibling subsystem is often the site of the most active boundary negotiation, and it may be the alliance in which the adolescent's separation is first attempted.

**The parent subsystem** is the marital or spousal unit, and in a family with adolescents this is the unit whose relationship with the child subsystem determines most of what happens.

Two subsystems may be **discrete** — clearly bounded from each other, with no crossover — or **nurturing** — clearly bounded in some respects but with permeability in others, which allows the flow of affection, support, and information while preserving the distinctness of the units.

### 2.3 Boundaries

**Boundaries** are the invisible lines of influence and responsibility that separate the subsystems and the individuals within them. They are the structural model's most important clinical concept, and the one that maps most directly onto the clinical problems of adolescence.

Three kinds of boundary appear in the clinical description.

**Diffuse boundaries** are indistinct, permeable, and non-defining. A person with a diffuse boundary has difficulty differentiating what belongs to them from what belongs to others; the other person's thoughts, feelings, and actions are experienced as their own; and the person is over-involved in others' lives and is unable to act independently. In the family context, diffuse boundaries appear as a parent who cannot allow a child to have an experience that is not shared with her, or a young person who cannot say what he thinks without checking what his mother thinks.

**Clear boundaries** are defined, appropriate to the developmental stage, and permit the flow of affection and support while maintaining the distinctness of the subsystems. A clear boundary between a parent and an adolescent allows the parent to care for the adolescent and to be involved in his life while also allowing him to be his own person, to have private thoughts, and to have relationships the parent does not enter.

**Rigid boundaries** are impenetrable and restrictive, and are typically the result of a history of premature autonomy, of trauma, or of a family in which closeness itself has been experienced as dangerous. A rigid boundary between a parent and an adolescent produces a young person who is emotionally isolated, self-sufficient, and unable to ask for help, and the young person's difficulty in asking for help is frequently mistaken for strength.

### 2.4 Hierarchy

**Hierarchy** is the clear line of authority across the subsystems. The structural model's position is that the parents are in charge of the children, and that the children are not in charge of the parents, and that a family in which the hierarchy is inverted produces symptoms in the children.

The three forms of hierarchy disorder are worth naming.

**Parentification** occurs when a child takes a parental function, either by taking over a practical role — the cooking, the shopping, the care of the siblings, the management of the household — or by taking over the emotional role, by becoming the confidant and the comforter of a parent. Parentification is the structural description of the "over-responsible child", and it is the single most common structural problem in the clinical literature on adolescents.

**Parentification may also run the other way**, in which the parent becomes the child — the parent who confides in the adolescent, who asks the adolescent for advice, who relies on the adolescent for emotional support, or who becomes so focused on the adolescent's needs that the adolescent becomes the caretaker of the parent's emotional life. Minuchin described this as the parent and the child becoming "inverted" in the hierarchy.

**Sibling inversion** occurs when a sibling takes a parental function over another sibling, and it is very common in families with a disabled or distressed child, and it creates a bond that is difficult to dissolve later.

### 2.5 The family projection process

The structural model adopted the family projection process from Bowen, and it uses it in a specifically structural way: the projection of the parents' own difficulties onto a child, and the child's structural role in the family being shaped by that projection.

The clinical significance in the structural frame is that the projected child is usually also the structurally positioned child. A mother who is unable to tolerate her own anxiety projects it onto a child who is placed in a role that requires her to manage it, and the child's symptoms are the medium of the projection.

## 3. Structural Diagnosis

### 3.1 The procedure

The structural diagnosis proceeds in four stages, and it is done with the family present.

**Stage 1: the presenting problem.** The referral question is recorded, and the practitioner asks the family to describe the problem in their own terms. The question that is asked is not "why does she do that?" but "when does it happen, and what happens just before, and what happens just after?"

**Stage 2: the interaction pattern.** The family is asked to describe a recent ordinary occasion in detail, and the practitioner draws the sequence of who did what to whom. This is the "sequence" work, and it is the technique the strategic school also uses; the structural school asks the same question and uses the answer differently.

**Stage 3: the structural map.** The family is drawn in the form of the standard structural diagram, with squares for males and circles for females, with the subsystems delineated, and with the boundaries marked. The drawing is done in the session, with the family's corrections, and the corrections are as informative as the drawing.

**Stage 4: the diagnosis.** The practitioner names the structure: who is in which subsystem, where the boundaries are diffuse, clear, or rigid, where the hierarchy is inverted, and what the family projection process is doing.

### 3.2 The structural map as a clinical tool

The map is used in three ways, and the student should be able to distinguish them.

**As a diagnostic instrument.** The map makes visible the structure that the family cannot see, and the moment of seeing is often a moment of considerable emotional impact.

**As a communication device.** The map can be handed to a school, to a doctor, and to a new practitioner, and it communicates the structure in a form that a non-specialist can use. It is the most portable document in the family therapy field.

**As a therapeutic instrument.** The map is drawn with the family, and the drawing is itself the beginning of the change, because a family that has drawn its own structure has begun to see it.

### 3.3 The distinction between the structural and the interactional formulation

A student should be able to produce both formulations for the same case, because they are different products and they point to different interventions.

The **interactional formulation** describes the sequence: who does what, in what order, with what consequence. It is the basis of the strategic work in Chapter 32 and of the contingency management in Chapter 28.

The **structural formulation** describes the organisation: who is in which subsystem, where the boundaries are, and where the hierarchy is inverted. It is the basis of the structural work.

For the same case, the interactional formulation might be: "the mother withdraws when the daughter asks for anything, and the daughter escalates until she is answered." The structural formulation might be: "the mother and the daughter are in a diffuse boundary, with the daughter functioning as the mother's confidant and the mother functioning as the daughter's emotional regulator; the parental subsystem has collapsed because the father is absent and the grandmother has assumed the parental function with a rigid boundary towards the father."

The structural formulation is the one that predicts what will happen if the practitioner works only on the interaction.

## 4. The Principles of Structural Family Therapy

### 4.1 The first principle: the structure causes the symptom

The first principle is the organising claim of the model: the symptom is a consequence of the family structure, and the symptom will persist as long as the structure persists and will resolve when the structure changes. The clinical implication is that a treatment directed at the symptom alone will fail, and the treatment must be directed at the structure.

### 4.2 The second principle: the family is the unit of treatment

The family is the unit because the structure is the thing that must change, and the structure is not located in any individual. The structural therapists are not neutral about this, and the practice of seeing the family together is not a convenience: a practitioner who sees only the identified patient will be inducted into the family structure as it is, and will be positioned in it, and will be unable to work with it.

### 4.3 The third principle: the intervention is the restructuring

The aim of the intervention is to change the structure: to move a young person from the child subsystem to a position in which they are treated as an adult where the developmental stage requires it, to redraw the boundaries, to restore the hierarchy, and to alter the alliances.

### 4.4 Enactment

The **enactment** is the signature technique of the model and one of the great technical contributions of the family therapy field. It is the invitation to the family to perform, in the consulting room, the interaction that they typically perform outside it, so that the practitioner can observe the structure directly.

**The procedure.** The practitioner asks a family member to describe a recent instance of the difficulty, selects a segment of it, and asks the participants to "go through it" — to enact the exchange as it happened, with the roles they normally occupy and with the words they normally use. The practitioner observes.

**What the enactment reveals.** The practitioner sees the sequence, the alliances, the covert rules, the double binds, the invitations and the refusals. What the family can describe and what the family can *do* are different, and the difference is usually the clinical material.

**The interruption.** After a short time — often less than a minute — the practitioner interrupts, and the interruption is the intervention. The practitioner does not comment on the content; the intervention is behavioural and structural. The classic interventions are: the practitioner asks the young person to do what they have been unable to do; the practitioner asks the parent to stop doing what they have been doing; the practitioner comments on what the family has just demonstrated; or the practitioner enacts a small piece of the interaction themselves in order to make the pattern visible.

**The risk.** The enactment is a challenge, and it can be experienced as a humiliation, particularly if the practitioner interrupts at the moment of the young person's greatest distress, or in a way that sides with the parent against the young person. The structural therapists were not always careful about this, and the modern practice is to time the intervention with care and to attend to the young person's subsequent state.

### 4.5 Challenge and restructuring

The **challenge** is the general term for the interventions that the practitioner makes to change the family's organisation, and the structural therapists' challenge is characteristically direct: naming the process, naming the double bind, and refusing the invitation to be triangulated.

Some of the specific challenges are worth naming.

**The refusal of the triangulation.** When the young person is enlisted as the parent's ally or as the parent's rescuer, the practitioner declines the role and says so. This is the intervention that addresses parentification directly, and it is often experienced by the parent as a betrayal, and it frequently produces an immediate deterioration in the therapeutic relationship with the parent that is temporary and is the sign that the challenge has landed.

**The naming of the double bind.** A double bind is a situation in which a person receives two incompatible messages, and the restriction on the communication of the message is itself part of the bind. A parent who says "you are an adult now, you make your own decisions" while also saying "if you don't do what I say, you don't respect me" has constructed a double bind, and a young person placed in a double bind is placed in a position from which any response is a symptom. The intervention is to name the bind to the parents, not to the young person.

**The boundary renegotiation.** The most important restructuring with an adolescent is the movement of the boundary between the parent-child subsystem and the adult subsystem. In most clinical work with adolescents, the parents are maintaining a pre-adolescent structure over an adolescent child, and the symptom is a report of the mismatch.

### 4.6 The maintenance of change

The structural therapists were unusually aware that the change achieved in a session decays, and the model has a good account of why: the change is a behavioural change in the family's organisation, and the old organisation is stable because it is familiar, because it is costly to change, and because the other members of the family have not been helped to accommodate the change. A young person who has been given more autonomy and a parent who has not adjusted to the new structure will find the change reversed within a fortnight, and the practitioner should expect it and plan for it.

Three technical requirements follow. The change must be **consensual** — every member of the family must have some stake in it, and a restructuring imposed from outside will be dismantled. It must be **explicit** — the new rules are stated aloud, in the session, and by the family, so that the new structure is a shared project rather than a secret. And it must be **maintained by the family's own mechanisms**, which in practice means the practitioner works with the parent on the parent's difficulties with the new structure, and the parent's difficulties are the material.
