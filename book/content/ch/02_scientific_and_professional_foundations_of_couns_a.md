# Scientific and Professional Foundations of Counseling

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the social, medical, and educational conditions that gave rise to the counseling movement in the twentieth century.
- Identify the four intellectual traditions inherited from psychology and explain what each contributes to the counseling knowledge base.
- Distinguish the scientific, the professional, the ethical, and the practical dimensions of the counseling enterprise, and explain how they depend on one another.
- Describe the principal research methods used to evaluate counseling interventions and the questions each is able to answer.
- Explain what professional accreditation and licensure do and do not accomplish, and why the distinction between them matters ethically.
- Locate counseling within the broader landscape of mental health service provision and describe the domains in which counselors practise.
- Discuss the critique that counseling is a "semi-professional" occupation and evaluate its force.

## Chapter Overview

Counseling did not begin as a science. It began as a set of social responses to a set of social conditions: mass urbanisation, the large-scale entry of women into the industrial workforce, the recognition that many people who had been confined in asylums and hospitals for want of other resources could be treated in the community, the expansion of public education, and two devastating wars that left behind large numbers of people who were injured, bereaved, and confused about what they were for. The profession that emerged from these conditions was created by people who wanted to do something useful and who needed to be able to justify what they were doing to institutions that funded them.

That origin explains several persistent features of the profession, and it explains them better than an account that begins with Rogers or with the publication of a textbook. It explains why counseling has always been unusually concerned with the demonstration of effectiveness. It explains why the profession has always been internally divided between a scientific and a humanistic wing. It explains why the training of counselors has always included supervised practice rather than only academic study, and why the professional codes have always placed unusual weight on competence and relationship. And it explains why the profession's central theoretical question is not "what is true?" but "what is defensible in the presence of a person who needs help now?"

This chapter traces that history in outline, identifies the four intellectual traditions the profession inherited, describes the architecture of the professional enterprise as it now stands, and examines the research methods by which the profession holds itself accountable. It closes with a discussion of the status of the occupation and of the critique that has been levelled at it.

## 1. From Asylum to Profession: The Social Origins

### 1.1 The nineteenth-century background

The counseling movement has antecedents that run through the nineteenth century, and a student who knows only the 1940s will misunderstand the profession's character.

The first antecedent is the **asylum reform movement**. Throughout the early nineteenth century, the institutional response to mental illness in Britain and the United States was custodial: large, overcrowded asylums whose purpose was detention and whose economies depended on the cheapness of the conditions in which the patients were kept. The asylum reform movement, associated with the physician John Conolly in Britain and with Dorothea Dix in the United States, argued instead for **moral treatment** — the provision of a clean, orderly, humane environment on the supposition that many patients were suffering from conditions responsive to care, kindness, routine, and purposeful activity. Moral treatment did not cure much, and it was destroyed by the overcrowding that followed the collapse of its institutional financing, but the claim it embedded in the professional imagination was decisive: *the conditions in which a person is treated matter, and the treatment of the mind is a specialised human enterprise rather than a custodial routine*.

The second antecedent is the growth of **public education** and, with it, the professionalisation of teaching. The nineteenth century saw the creation of graded school systems, the establishment of teacher training institutions, and the introduction of the idea that the systematic education of children required special knowledge and special preparation. The school guidance movement that began in the United States in the 1890s — combining the work of the developmental psychologist James, the educational statistician Engel, and, decisively, the work of **Leta Stetter Hollingworth** — located the beginning of professional school counseling in the scientific study of individual differences and in the conviction that the school could be a setting in which individual development was systematically supported. The school counselor was thus the first of the counseling roles to be defined, and it remains the role with the clearest institutional base.

The third antecedent is the **vocational guidance movement**, which arose in the first decade of the twentieth century and which was, in effect, the first organised attempt to apply psychology to the practical problems of human lives. Frank Parsons' work in Boston is conventionally taken as the beginning of vocational guidance, and Parsons' threefold formulation of matching, on the basis of the study of the individual, the occupations that the individual might fit, is the direct ancestor of the person-environment fit tradition that still organises career counseling.

The fourth antecedent is the **child guidance movement**, which began in the United States in the 1880s with the establishment of the first psychological clinics and which, by the 1930s, had produced the influential child guidance clinics. These clinics brought together a child, a family, and a set of professionals, and they developed the practice of working with a child's difficulties in the context of the family and the school rather than in the clinic alone. The contemporary practice of family assessment and school consultation descends directly from them.

### 1.2 The two world wars

The First World War produced the first large-scale demonstration that the systematic study of personality could be put to practical use. The military authorities required rapid assessment of recruits, and the development of the Army Alpha and Army Beta tests in 1917 represented a decisive advance in applied psychometrics. It also produced the first mass psychological casualty, the phenomenon subsequently called shell shock and now understood as combat-related post-traumatic stress. The observation that a proportion of soldiers could not return to civilian life, and that these men were not malingering but permanently altered, established both the clinical reality of trauma and the institutional necessity of specialised provision.

The Second World War was more consequential still, and its consequences were structural rather than symbolic. Three developments in particular shaped the post-war counseling profession.

The first was the **mass expansion of clinical psychology and psychiatry**, driven by the needs of returning servicemen and by the Veterans Administration hospitals, the largest psychological care system ever created. The evaluation and treatment programmes of the 1940s created a demand for trained practitioners, and the training programmes that met that demand produced the first generation of academically credentialed counselors and psychologists.

The second was the emergence of the **child guidance clinic as the centre of professional practice**, and with it the disciplinary formation of clinical child psychology. The influential Chicago and Boston clinics of the 1930s and 1940s, and the publications that emerged from them, established the model of a child clinician working with a family and with a school.

The third was the **rise of the mental health movement as a public policy concern**, and in particular the establishment in the United States of the National Institute of Mental Health in 1949, with its mandate for research, training, and service development. The creation of a federal research-and-training infrastructure is what made it possible for a profession to assert that it was scientific.

### 1.3 The four conditions of professional formation

Drawing these threads together, counseling became a profession in the middle of the twentieth century because four conditions were met.

| Condition | What it provided | Institution that embodied it |
|---|---|---|
| A distinct area of practice | A domain of work distinguishable from psychiatry, psychology, and social work | The school counselor, the rehabilitation counsellor, the marriage counsellor |
| A body of knowledge | A recognised literature and a shared curriculum | University departments and professional accreditation |
| A method of training | A standard programme combining academic study with supervised practice | Graduate programmes and practicum training |
| A professional identity and code | A shared set of commitments, standards, and ethical obligations | Professional associations and codes of ethics |

Each of these is necessary; none is sufficient alone. The profession's characteristic tensions — between the humanistic and scientific wings, between the school and clinical settings, between the academic and the vocational routes to qualification — are products of the fact that the four conditions were met at different times, in different institutions, for different populations.

## 2. The Four Intellectual Traditions

Counseling inherited four distinct intellectual traditions from psychology, and its internal structure reflects their coexistence rather than the triumph of any one of them.

![The four research traditions that constitute professional counseling.](fig03_research_tradition.png)

### 2.1 The experimental tradition

The experimental tradition begins with the founding of the laboratory and reaches its twentieth-century culmination in behaviourism. Its contribution to counseling is the **methodological** contribution: the experimental design, the operational definition, the single-case experimental method, the randomised controlled trial, and the general insistence that claims about what helps must be testable. The behaviourist wing of counseling is the direct inheritor of this tradition, and the entire enterprise of treatment manuals, efficacy studies, and fidelity monitoring in the 1980s and 1990s was a product of the experimental tradition applied to a clinical field.

### 2.2 The interpretive tradition

The interpretive tradition descends from the hermeneutic and phenomenological philosophies and entered psychology through the psychodynamic movement, the humanistic movement, and the later qualitative research tradition. Its contribution to counseling is the **understanding of meaning**: that behaviour has intelligibility, that symptoms have communicative and adaptive functions, and that the practitioner's task is first to comprehend and only then to change. The tradition's methodological contribution, qualitative interviewing and the analysis of meaning, entered counseling research more slowly but is now indispensable to the study of process, of the alliance, and of the client's own account of what has helped.

### 2.3 The humanistic and developmental tradition

The humanistic and developmental tradition, given its decisive expression in Abraham Maslow's work on motivation, in the research on self-actualisation, and in Kurt Lewin's field theory and group dynamics, contributed the concept of the **person as a self-actualising, socially embedded agent**. It supplied the concept of the fully functioning person, the person as the expert on his or her own life, the concepts of congruence and unconditional positive regard, and the group work tradition. It also contributed the *person-environment fit* framework of vocational psychology.

### 2.4 The systems and sociocultural tradition

The systems tradition, from general systems theory, cybernetics, and ecological psychology, contributed the concepts of **wholeness, circularity, feedback, and context**. It supplied the family systems and ecological frameworks that occupy Part V and Part VII of this book, and it is the intellectual source of the contemporary emphasis on context, on the interaction of person and environment, and on multilevel intervention.

### 2.5 The persistence of the division

The coexistence of these traditions explains the two great splits in the history of counseling: the split between the humanistic and the behavioural wings that dominated the 1950s and 1960s, and the split between the "training model" and the "clinical model" within graduate preparation. Neither split was a failure of goodwill. It reflected genuine and, within limits, irreconcilable differences about the nature of the person — whether the person is an active chooser who can be trusted, or a system of responses to contingencies that must be rearranged. Chapter 3 examines that question directly, because the question of what a person is remains the foundation of everything else.

## 3. The Architecture of the Professional Enterprise

Counseling today is a large and heterogeneous enterprise. It is worth describing its parts precisely, because students of the profession often meet several of them in a single week and need to know the differences.

![Three pillars of professional counseling identity.](fig04_professional_identity.png)

### 3.1 The domains of practice

**School counseling.** The school counselor works within an educational institution, with a caseload that typically includes individual students, small groups, whole-class programmes, consultation with teachers, and liaison with families and community agencies. The distinctive constraints of the role are institutional: the school day, the school calendar, the school building, the collective responsibility of the staff, and the fact that the client is usually not the person who sought help. Chapter 56 treats school practice in detail.

**Clinical and counseling practice in community mental health.** The community clinician works in a health centre, a hospital outpatient department, a private practice, a youth centre, or an agency, and typically sees a more selected population with more severe difficulties. The distinctive constraints are the reimbursement framework, the necessity of diagnosis for service eligibility, and the requirement of outcome reporting.

**Substance use and mental health services.** The substance use field, which is in some respects the most theoretically developed part of the profession, operates under a specific regulatory framework, and the treatment of co-occurring substance use and mental health difficulties is the field's characteristic technical problem. Chapter 14 in the curriculum map addresses it.

**Marriage, couple, and family therapy.** Family practice treats the relationship as the unit of intervention, and its distinctive technical problem is that the client who is referred is frequently not the person who is most distressed. Chapter 33 addresses work with parents and adolescents.

**Career and vocational counseling.** Vocational practice is organised around person-environment fit and is strongly shaped by the labour market and by the institutions of education and training. It retains the clearest link to the movement's nineteenth-century origins.

**Rehabilitation, disability, and developmental services.** Practising with people experiencing disability requires a theoretical position that does not locate disability in the individual, and this requirement has produced some of the most theoretically sophisticated work in the profession.

### 3.2 Settings and the shape of the work

Whatever the domain, the conditions of work are common across settings and worth naming, because they shape what is professionally possible. A counselor works within a setting that has its own goals, its own accountability structures, and its own informal culture. A counselor works within a legal and regulatory framework that determines what may be done, with whom, and with what record. A counselor works within an organisational structure that determines the pace of the work, the caseload, the referral pathways, and the extent to which collaboration is possible. A counselor works with a client who is usually in a setting the client did not choose. These conditions are not distractions from the counselling; they are the field in which the counselling occurs, and the professional competence that Chapter 57 assesses includes the capacity to work competently within them.

## 4. The Research Foundation

The professional claim to scientific status rests on a body of method, and a graduate student should be able to name and describe the principal methods, because the reading of the research literature in Part VII of this book requires it.

### 4.1 The randomised controlled trial

The randomised controlled trial assigns participants to conditions, ideally with allocation concealed and with assessors blind to condition, and compares outcomes. It is the strongest design for causal inference and the standard against which other designs are judged. Its limits in counseling research are severe: the sample sizes required for adequate power are large, so trials are usually conducted with adults rather than with young people; the interventions tested are usually manualised, which changes what is being tested; and the trials are frequently conducted in research clinics that do not resemble the services in which counselling is ordinarily delivered.

### 4.2 The randomised controlled trial in routine services

The most methodologically important innovation in the field has been the randomised controlled trial conducted within routine services with the outcome data of ordinary clients. This design, often described as a pragmatic or cohort-randomised design, has produced a very different and more sobering picture of counselling effectiveness than the efficacy literature. In large trials in which routine service providers delivered the treatments under routine conditions, average outcomes were considerably less favourable than in the efficacy literature, and the variability between providers was substantial. The implication is not that counselling does not work. It is that its effectiveness depends on conditions that the efficacy literature had abstracted away: the skill of the practitioner, the quality of the relationship, the fit between the intervention and the person, and the quality of the service system in which the work is done. Chapter 7 discusses this literature in detail, and Chapter 53 draws out its implications for measurement-based practice.

### 4.3 Single-case experimental designs

The single-case experimental design tracks an individual's outcome over time, with repeated measurement and, typically, the systematic manipulation of one variable. Its central weakness is generalisation to other individuals, but its strength is that it can be conducted within a routine service, that it provides immediate feedback to the practitioner, and that it can capture the individual young person whose case is at issue. Behavioural approaches have historically been the most willing to use these designs, and they remain a legitimate and underused option in school and agency settings.

### 4.4 Meta-analysis and systematic review

Meta-analysis pools the results of multiple studies, weighting each by its precision, and estimates an overall effect with a confidence interval. Systematic review differs in that it is a protocol-governed search and appraisal of a defined literature with a pre-specified inclusion criterion. Both are essential to the appraisal of the evidence, and both have to be read critically: the quality of a meta-analysis depends heavily on the quality of the primary studies, the homogeneity of the interventions, and the risk of publication bias in the source literature, which is substantial in psychotherapy research and is estimated in many reviews to run into the tens of percent.

### 4.5 Qualitative and mixed-methods research

Qualitative research asks what the intervention means, how it is experienced, and what happens in practice that does not appear in outcome data. It is the method by which the process of therapy has been understood, and it is indispensable to the study of the alliance, of client experience, and of the reasons why a manualised treatment is delivered differently in practice than in a trial. Mixed-methods research integrates the two, and its quality depends on the integration being specified in advance rather than improvised at the writing stage.

### 4.6 Practice-based evidence

Practice-based evidence is generated by routine outcome monitoring in real services, aggregated across caseloads, and analysed with quasi-experimental methods. It is the only body of evidence that describes what happens in ordinary services with ordinary clients, and it is the most direct challenge to the assumption that a treatment proven in a trial will work in a service. Chapter 53 develops the instruments.

## 5. Regulation, Accreditation, and Licensure

Three distinct mechanisms govern entry to and practice of the profession, and they are frequently confused.

**Accreditation** is a quality assurance mechanism applied to educational programmes rather than to individuals. In the United States, the Council for Accreditation of Counseling and Related Educational Programs accredits degree programmes against published standards that specify faculty qualifications, student-to-faculty ratios, curriculum content, field experience, and assessment practices. Accreditation is a statement about the programme, and it is a precondition of many forms of state licensure.

**Licensure** is a state-level legal requirement that an individual must hold a licence to practise. Its design varies considerably by jurisdiction, and the differences are not merely technical. In some states the licensure track is a two-year master's degree with a supervised practicum and a national examination; in others it is a doctoral degree with additional supervised hours. A student considering practice in a specific jurisdiction should establish the requirements early, because the implications for training and for the sequencing of coursework are substantial.

**Certification** and **registration** are alternatives used in some jurisdictions in place of licensure, with the usual consequence that the title of the professional is not legally protected.

The ethical significance of the distinction is that licensure is a public protection mechanism, and it is the mechanism through which the state's interest in the competence of those who treat its citizens is expressed. It is also the mechanism through which the profession has historically accepted a measure of state oversight in exchange for the protection of its own scope of practice. The profession's complicated relationship with licensure — the recurring disputes over the independent practice of counselling, the movement to create a distinct licensure for licensed professional counsellors, the "licensure compact" initiatives — is best understood as a struggle over the distribution of power between the state, the profession, and the other mental health occupations.

### 5.1 What the codes require

The ethical codes of the major professional bodies require a great deal, and a graduate student should read them closely, because they constitute the legal and moral floor of practice. Among their provisions are: the requirement of competence and of the maintenance of competence through continuing education; the requirement of informed consent, including, for minors, the obtaining of parental or guardian permission and the young person's assent; the requirement of confidentiality and the specification of its limits, including the duty to disclose when a client is a danger to self or others and the child-protection reporting duties that apply in most jurisdictions; the requirement of informed consent and of the limits of the confidentiality of group and family work; the requirement that the practitioner not discriminate on the basis of race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, age, disability, or socioeconomic status; the requirement of appropriate boundaries and of the avoidance of dual relationships, particularly in work with minors; the requirement of competence in the use of assessment instruments and of the obligation to use instruments for their intended purposes; the requirement to use the most current and relevant evidence in practice; and the requirement to attempt to resolve ethical issues through an orderly process, beginning with consultation and supervision and escalating to regulatory bodies where necessary.

These requirements are the framework within which every theoretical decision in this book must be made.

## 6. The Critique of the Semi-Profession

Counseling has attracted a recurring and not wholly unfair criticism that it is a **semi-professional** occupation: a group with pretensions to professional status that lacks some of the hallmarks of a profession. The classic criteria of a profession are a distinctive body of knowledge, entry controlled by a formal educational process, autonomy in the conduct of practice, a code of ethics with enforcement, a commitment to a lifetime of continuing education, a social organisation with a formal structure, and the acceptance of a distinctive social responsibility.

Measured against these criteria, counseling does reasonably well on the body of knowledge, on entry, on ethics, and on continuing education, where many jurisdictions now require it. It does less well on autonomy, because much of counselling is delivered in settings in which the organisation determines what is done, to whom, and for how long. It does least well on the social organisation, because the profession has historically been divided among several competing bodies with different requirements, and on the question of a distinctive social responsibility, because the profession's answer to "what are you for?" has historically been ambiguous.

The most useful response to this critique is neither to dismiss it nor to accept it wholesale. Two of its claims require sustained response in the present circumstances. The first is the autonomy critique, which is the substance of the argument for the integration of the profession and for the recognition of counselling as a distinct mental health discipline rather than as a specialism of psychology or social work. The second is the equity critique, which is the substance of the argument that the profession's commitments to justice require attention to its own internal arrangements — who is admitted, who is trained, who is paid, and who is treated.
