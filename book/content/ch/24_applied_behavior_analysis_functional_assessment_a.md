# Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the origin and the defining commitments of applied behaviour analysis as a discipline and as a practice.
- Distinguish descriptive functional assessment from experimental functional analysis, and state the difference in the strength of the claims each can support.
- Construct an operational definition of a behaviour, and explain why the definition determines the quality of the whole assessment.
- Apply the ABC model to a real behaviour and generate a functional hypothesis from the data.
- Describe the four elements of a functional analysis and the design options for each.
- Explain the distinction between the four functional classes and the three setting, or "mood", demands of behaviour.
- State the difference between descriptive, hypothesis-testing, and experimental functional assessment, and the circumstances in which each is appropriate.
- Plan a functional assessment for a young person, including safety provisions where the behaviour is dangerous.
- Critically evaluate the strengths and limitations of the functional assessment approach, including its ethical dimensions with minors and people who do not use speech.

## Chapter Overview

The clinical question "why does this young person do that?" sounds like an invitation to speculate. In the behavioural tradition it is an invitation to design an experiment.

Applied behaviour analysis — the applied wing of the behavioural framework introduced in Chapter 23 — takes a view of behaviour that is at once narrower and more demanding than any other theory in this book. It is narrower because it confines itself to the behavioural environment of the individual, and it makes no claim about the interior. It is more demanding because it requires the clinician to state, in operational terms, what the behaviour is; to gather data; to generate a specific and testable hypothesis about what the behaviour accomplishes; and then to test that hypothesis against the data. A clinician who cannot state a testable hypothesis has not finished the assessment, and the discipline is unusually clear about this.

The method's power lies in the fact that the hypothesis, once formed, can be checked. The method's liability lies in the fact that a hypothesis that has been checked and found false is more deflating than a guess that was never examined, and the field has a documented history of treating a failed prediction as a challenge to the person rather than as information about the behaviour.

The history of the discipline is inseparable from its history with children. For three decades, the field's flagship intervention was the behavioural treatment of autism, and the controversy that surrounded it — the controversy about aversives, about the training of compliance, about the rights of participants, and about the quality of the research — has shaped every subsequent ethical standard in the discipline and remains the sharpest test of its integrity. A student who reads the applied literature without this history will misread its confidence.

This chapter presents the method in its technical form, because the method is genuinely demanding and it cannot be learned from a description. It then turns to the ethical and cultural questions that the method raises with unusual sharpness, and it concludes with the practice of functional assessment with young people in the settings in which they are actually found.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The founding of applied behaviour analysis

The discipline named itself in 1968, at a conference held in Washington, D.C., and its founding statement was Donald Baer's paper "Some Dimensions of Applied Behavior Analysis." The definition that was agreed is still the shortest accurate description of the field:

> Applied behavior analysis is a natural science concerned with the analysis of behavior. It is a basic science with a fundamental characteristic of being a science that is primarily concerned with the analysis of the functional relations between behavior and environment; that is, with the systematic and controlled manipulation of environmental variables and the resulting changes in behavior; and it is a technology for the development of behavior change technologies that are grounded in the science and that are themselves derived from it.

Three phrases in that definition are worth holding on to, because they mark the field's commitments. "A natural science" claims a place in the scientific family, with the standards of replication, measurement, and falsification that this implies. "Functional relations" commits the field to the analysis of relations rather than to the description of events. And "a technology" commits it to the production of tools that work, with a requirement that the tools be derived from the science rather than assembled from intuition.

The final clause — that the technology must be derived from the science — is the field's most distinctive and most frequently violated standard, and it is the reason the discipline has a well-earned reputation for treating a treatment that has not been validated as an inadequate treatment rather than as an adequate one.

### 1.2 The 1968 criteria for applied research

The same conference produced a set of criteria by which applied research could be assessed, and the list remains one of the most useful instruments in the discipline for evaluating a study — and, by extension, a treatment.

The criteria require that the problem be a behaviour that is important to the individual or to society; that the behaviour have a measurable effect on the environment; that there be a systematic manipulation of some aspect of the environment; that there be a clear relation between the manipulation and the behaviour change; and that the behaviour change be behaviourally defined. The criteria are demanding in a particular way: a study that manipulates nothing is not applied research, and a study that manipulates something and reports a change without establishing the relation is not applied research either.

The 1968 criteria were later revised in 1993 in response to the recognition that the original list was not sensitive to issues of generalisation, of the social validity of the behaviour, and of the participant's quality of life, and the revised criteria add a requirement that the intervention should improve the participant's quality of life, that it should be generalisable to the settings in which the behaviour occurs, and that it should be acceptable to the participants. The revisions were, in effect, an attempt to answer the ethical criticisms of the preceding two decades.

### 1.3 The Unitarian Position

The 1970s saw a sustained and unusually rigorous theoretical argument within the discipline over the "Unitarian Position" — the claim that a single set of principles about behaviour, expressed at different levels of description, is sufficient to analyse all behaviour, including complex human behaviour.

The position was developed most fully by the "Part I" and "Part II" papers of 1973 and 1974, which argued for a three-level analysis: a level of the functional relations between behaviour and the environment, a level of the relations among the controlling stimuli (the "functional relations" between the environment and the environmental behavior), and a level of the individual's history and private events. The argument was deliberately narrow: it did not claim that behaviour is simple, and it did not claim that complex problems have simple solutions. It claimed that the *analysis* is uniform, and that the apparent differences between the simple and the complex are differences in the complexity of the environmental control, not differences in kind.

The Unitarian Position is significant in this book because it is the most sustained attempt in the discipline to reconcile behaviourism with a richer psychology. It is also the most technical, and a student is not expected to master it. What the student should take from it is the insight that the difference between a simple and a complex case is often a difference in the number and subtlety of the controlling variables, and that the number of variables can be managed if they are specified one at a time.

### 1.4 The controversy with autism

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, a number of applied behaviour analysis programmes delivered what were called behavioural programmes for autistic children, in which a variety of techniques — some of them aversive, some of them involving food or drink as reinforcers, and some of them involving the deprivation of sleep — were used to shape behaviour.

The controversy that followed is the most consequential episode in the history of the discipline, and it is the reason that the field's ethical language is what it is today. Four of the criticisms were substantiated.

**The research was weak.** Studies in this period frequently used designs in which there was no experimental control, in which the outcome was defined in terms that could not be independently verified, and in which the participants were not selected against a control condition. When internal critiques of the early research were published in the 1970s and 1980s, the effect on the field's confidence was severe.

**Some of the techniques were aversive.** The use of contingent aversive stimuli in some programmes was documented, and while most practitioners in the field used the least restrictive procedures available, the incidents were sufficient to shape the field's subsequent ethical standard.

**Informed consent was not obtained in the modern sense.** Parents were asked to consent to treatment, which is not the same as a young person's assent to a specific procedure, and the field later developed distinct and detailed requirements for both.

**The behaviour targeted was sometimes trivial.** Some programmes targeted such behaviours as the lack of a stereotypic movement, on the grounds that any behaviour that interfered with learning should be changed, and the field later became more careful about the difference between a behaviour that is a disorder and a behaviour that is merely unusual.

The criticisms were not simply an attack from outside the discipline. Many of the sharpest came from behaviour analysts, and the discipline's response was to reform: to require experimental control in applied studies, to develop alternatives to aversive procedures, to formalise assent and withdrawal procedures, to develop the least restrictive procedure standard as a requirement rather than a preference, and eventually to make the participant's quality of life a criterion of success alongside the behaviour change.

The history is not presented here as a matter of settled blame. It is presented because a student who intends to work with young people in behavioural settings will be asked about it, and because the honest answer — that the field made serious errors, that the errors were substantive, and that the discipline's current standards are a direct product of them — is the only answer that is both accurate and professionally usable.

### 1.5 The expansion into other settings

From the late 1970s the discipline expanded rapidly into other applications: classroom behaviour, self-injury, aggression, pica, disruptive behaviour in residential settings, and the teaching of skills to people with intellectual disabilities. The expansion brought the discipline into contact with school systems, hospitals, and courts, and it brought it into contact with the legal and ethical questions that follow from that contact.

The applied behaviour analysis approach to **treatment fidelity** — the systematic measurement of whether the intervention was delivered as intended — is another contribution of the period and is now standard practice in the field, and is a model of professional self-discipline that the wider profession has largely adopted.

## 2. The Method: Four Elements

Every functional assessment in the discipline contains four elements, whatever its setting and whatever its sophistication. A student who cannot name the four has not understood the method.

**The operational definition.** A precise, observable, measurable specification of the behaviour, including the conditions under which it occurs, the conditions under which it does not, and the way it is counted. The definition determines the quality of everything that follows, and no amount of subsequent care compensates for a poor one.

**The observation and recording system.** A structured method of gathering data, with a defined recording unit, a defined recording schedule, and defined rules for recording. The system must be practical enough to be used consistently by the people who will use it, because a measurement system that is not used is not a measurement system.

**The functional hypothesis.** A specific, testable statement about what the behaviour accomplishes, derived from the data and from the interview and observation information, and expressed in a form that can be refuted.

**The test.** The procedure by which the hypothesis is compared with the data, which is either an experimental functional analysis, a hypothesis-testing observational design, or — in the most constrained circumstances — a reasoned judgement that the available information does not permit a test.

### 2.1 Operational definitions

An operational definition specifies the behaviour in terms that any trained observer would agree upon, and it specifies the conditions under which it is counted. Three components are usually required.

**The topography**, or the physical and observable form of the behaviour: what the person does, in terms that can be seen and counted.

**The context**, or the conditions under which the behaviour occurs and is counted: the setting, the time, the activity, and the people present.

**The intensity**, or the measure by which variations in the behaviour are graded, and a stated threshold for what counts.

A worked example clarifies the point. "Daniel is aggressive" is not an operational definition and cannot be used for any purpose. "Daniel hits a peer with an open hand, at a rate of one or more occurrences per five minutes, during unstructured break times in the main corridor, with intensity recorded on a three-point scale from 1 (a single light push) to 3 (a blow causing a peer to fall or requiring adult intervention)" is an operational definition. It can be counted by two observers, it can be compared across weeks, and it can be used to test a hypothesis.

The severity thresholds in the example also matter for a reason that is not merely technical: behaviour that is dangerous requires additional provisions in the plan, and a definition that does not distinguish severity will fail to trigger them.

### 2.2 The ABC model in practice

The Antecedent–Behaviour–Consequence model, introduced in Chapter 23, is the standard observation sheet. Two variants are in common use and they answer different questions.

**The ABC narrative** records the full sequence of events leading up to and following each occurrence of the behaviour, in prose, with the antecedents described at whatever level of detail is available. It is the most informative instrument and the most laborious.

**The ABC tally** records, for each occurrence, the coded antecedent and the coded consequence, in a compact tabular form suitable for a large number of observations. It is less informative per occurrence and much more informative in aggregate.

The components of the antecedent record are typically: the activity in progress, the people present, the location, the time, and any recent change. The components of the consequence record are typically: the adult response, the peer response, the effect on the environment, and any delay before the response.

### 2.3 Generating hypotheses

A **functional hypothesis** is a statement of the form: *This behaviour occurs in order to produce [consequence], and it occurs more often in [setting] and under [antecedent conditions].* Three elements must be present for the hypothesis to be testable: the consequence, the setting, and the antecedent conditions.

The four classic functional classes are the most common hypothesis categories, and they are stated in terms of the consequence produced.

**Attention** — the behaviour is maintained by the delivery of attention, usually social and usually from an adult.

**Escape or avoidance** — the behaviour is maintained by the removal or postponement of a demand or an aversive stimulus.

**Access to tangibles** — the behaviour is maintained by the obtaining of a physical item or activity.

**Automatic reinforcement** — the behaviour produces its own consequence, as in the sensory consequences of repetitive behaviour or of self-injury.

The fifth class, which is often distinguished but is sometimes added to the four, is **social negative reinforcement**, in which the behaviour removes or postpones social interaction, for example ending a conversation.

### 2.4 The three setting demands

Beyond the four functional classes, a second set of hypotheses concerns the *setting demands* that the behaviour accommodates. These are derived from the work of Iwata and colleagues on self-injury and are important because they explain a great deal of behaviour that the four classes do not.

**Automatic** — the behaviour is maintained by its own sensory consequences and is unrelated to the social environment.

**Attention** — the behaviour occurs in the presence of others and is directed at obtaining their attention.

**Escape/avoidance** — the behaviour occurs in response to demands, and terminates or postpones them.

**Attention and escape/avoidance** — the behaviour serves both functions, and it is common in clinical settings where the behaviour is both attention-seeking and a response to a demand.

The value of the setting demands is that they are more directly observable than the functional classes. A practitioner who watches a young person for an hour and can state under what circumstances the behaviour occurs and what happens immediately afterwards has a good foundation for hypothesis testing, even if the interpretation of the underlying function remains tentative.

## 3. Descriptive Functional Assessment

### 3.1 The components

A **descriptive functional assessment** is a structured attempt to identify the antecedents and consequences of a behaviour using indirect and direct observation, interviews, and record review, without manipulating the environment.

Its components are typically:

- **Structured and unstructured interviews** with the young person, the parents, the teachers, and where appropriate the other professionals involved. The interview is not merely a matter of asking what the behaviour is for; in a well-conducted assessment the interviewer is trying to elicit the specific antecedents and consequences, and asking questions of the form "What happens right before?" and "What happens right after?" rather than "Why does he do that?"
- **Direct observation** in the natural setting, using a structured recording system.
- **Record review**, which is frequently the most productive component and is consistently under-used. School behaviour logs, attendance records, exclusion records, incident reports, and nursing notes frequently contain the pattern before anyone has looked for it. The case illustration in Chapter 23, in which eleven exclusions all fell within a single forty-minute period, is an example of what a record review produces.
- **The ABC analysis**, which organises the observations into the antecedent–behaviour–consequence sequence.

### 3.2 The strengths and the weaknesses

The descriptive assessment is non-invasive, does not require the cooperation of the environment, can be completed quickly, and is suitable for situations in which experimental manipulation would be impractical or unsafe. It is also subject to a severe limitation: **the data are observational, and the observer's presence may change the behaviour**, and more importantly, the descriptive data can support a hypothesis but cannot establish it, because the correlations that the data contain are not themselves the causal relations.

A practitioner who has constructed a plausible hypothesis from descriptive data and then proceeds to design a treatment from that hypothesis without testing it is making a common and consequential error. The hypothesis may be wrong, and if it is wrong, the treatment will be ineffective at best and harmful at worst, because the treatment will be built on contingencies that are not maintaining the behaviour.

The discipline's rule is straightforward: a hypothesis derived from descriptive data is a hypothesis until tested.

### 3.3 The information-gathering interview

The interview deserves separate treatment, because it is where the useful data most often is and where the most frequent errors occur.

The most common error is asking "why" questions. "Why does she hit her teacher?" invites a story, and the story will be a plausible account composed of the interviewee's theories of behaviour and the child's intent, and it will bear little relation to the maintaining contingencies. The productive questions are the concrete ones: what does the child do immediately before? What do the adults do immediately after? How long before an adult arrives? What happens in the ten minutes after? What did the other children do? What was different today?

The second common error is accepting a single informant's account as the account. Parents, teachers, and the young person each observe the behaviour in different settings, with different access, and with different stakes. Disagreement between informants is information: it usually means the behaviour is setting-specific, and the setting-specific pattern is usually where the function is.

The third error is the framing of the question in deficit terms. If the interviewer asks "What is causing this behaviour?", the answer will be a cause. If the interviewer asks "What is happening in the room just before this behaviour happens, and what happens immediately after?", the answer will be data.

## 4. Hypothesis-Testing Observational Designs

Between the descriptive assessment and the full experimental analysis stands a family of designs that test a specific prediction by analysing patterns that already exist in the natural data, without manipulating the environment. They are less rigorous than a functional analysis and considerably more rigorous than a descriptive assessment.

### 4.1 The prediction method

The **prediction method** is the simplest of the designs. The practitioner identifies a specific prediction that follows from the hypothesis — for example, "the behaviour will occur more often in the absence of the teacher than in her presence" — and then examines existing records to test it. The limitation is that the data were not collected for the purpose and may not contain the necessary information.

### 4.2 The ABC correlation method

The **ABC correlation method** analyses the recorded antecedents and consequences to determine whether the behaviour is reliably associated with particular consequences. It is the most useful of the family and the most commonly used. Its logic is direct: if a behaviour is maintained by attention, then it should be followed by attention more often than it is not, and the proportion of occasions followed by attention should exceed the base rate of attention delivery in the setting.

The base-rate comparison is what gives the method its power and is frequently omitted in informal use. A behaviour that is followed by attention on 80 per cent of occasions looks striking, but if the adults in the setting deliver attention to almost every child almost all the time, then 80 per cent is below the base rate and the behaviour may be maintained by something else entirely.

### 4.3 The structured ABC method

The **structured ABC method** goes further by manipulating the recording procedure itself: the recorder is instructed to look for occasions on which the behaviour occurs, and the antecedents and consequences are recorded in a more detailed and more reliable way than the ordinary ABC record permits. It is a substantial improvement on the standard record and it is well suited to the situation in which a hypothesis is already reasonably specific and needs firmer data.

## 5. Experimental Functional Analysis

### 5.1 The logic

A **functional analysis** is a single-case experimental design in which the consequences that follow the behaviour are systematically manipulated across conditions, and the behaviour is measured in each condition. The logic is that if a particular consequence is what maintains the behaviour, then removing that consequence should reduce the behaviour and restoring it should increase the behaviour again.

This is the method that converts a hypothesis into a finding, and it is the method's central contribution. It is also the most demanding, and it requires the full cooperation of the setting.

### 5.2 The standard conditions

A **traditional functional analysis** includes a series of conditions, typically:

- **A play condition**, in which the behaviour should not occur and the environment is arranged to be conducive to it. Behaviour that occurs at high rates in this condition suggests that the behaviour is maintained by its own consequences — that it is *automatically* maintained.
- **An alone condition**, in which the young person is left alone briefly with an observer present. This is a control for the possibility that the behaviour is cued by the presence of others. It is also a condition of ethical concern, because it involves leaving a young person alone, and it requires the safeguards described in Section 9.
- **A demand condition**, in which demands are made and the opportunity to escape is contingent on the behaviour. Behaviour that increases under demands is consistent with an escape function.
- **A play condition with attention delivered** contingent on the behaviour. Behaviour that increases when attention is delivered is consistent with an attention function.
- **A control condition**, in which the consequence of interest is delivered non-contingently, at random intervals. This is a crucial control, because it distinguishes the *contingent* delivery of a consequence from the mere presence of the consequence, which is what identifies the behaviour as attention-seeking rather than as behaviour that persists in the presence of a stimulus.

The conditions are typically run in an alternating or rotating design, sometimes with a reversal to demonstrate that the effect follows the condition rather than the passage of time.

### 5.3 The practical and ethical difficulties

The traditional functional analysis is often not feasible with a young person, for a set of reasons that are practical rather than theoretical.

**Time.** The design may require multiple sessions across days or weeks, and a young person's pattern of severe behaviour is often unstable over such a period, which compromises the interpretation.

**Safety.** Some behaviours are dangerous, and some of the conditions — particularly the alone condition, and the play-with-attention condition when the behaviour is aggression — carry a risk that requires safeguards.

**Setting cooperation.** The analysis requires that staff, teachers, and parents follow the contingency arrangements precisely, and in a real school or clinic, adherence to a protocol that requires five different staff members to behave in specified ways in specified conditions is a substantial and often unrealistic demand.

**The young person's own behaviour.** A young person who has learned what the conditions mean will behave differently across them, and the very behaviours that are most in need of assessment are the ones most likely to be affected by the assessment.

**The absence of a natural setting.** Some of the conditions cannot be arranged in the settings in which the behaviour actually occurs.

### 5.4 Brief and practical functional analyses

The literature contains a series of modified designs developed in response to these difficulties, and a student should know them because they are what is actually used.

The **brief functional analysis** reduces each condition to a short block, typically five minutes, and reduces the number of conditions, often to three or four. It is the workhorse of contemporary clinical practice and it can be completed in a single session in most cases.

The **practical functional analysis** is a specific and widely used protocol, developed for use in naturalistic settings with typically fewer and briefer conditions, and designed so that the analysis can be conducted by caregivers or by school staff rather than only by a specialist.

The **synthesis of multiple indirect and direct evidence** approach, sometimes called the **indirect plus direct** method, is a common strategy in which a hypothesis is first narrowed using interviews and record review, and then tested with a brief direct analysis. The indirect evidence is used to select and design the direct conditions, which is a legitimate use of indirect data — provided the test is still experimental.

### 5.5 What the functional analysis does not tell you

A functional analysis establishes which consequence maintains a behaviour in a particular setting and under particular conditions. It does not establish why the behaviour developed, what the young person means by it, or what should be done about it. A functional analysis is an answer to a narrow question — *what does this behaviour do?* — and a treatment plan that extends from it is making a step that requires a judgement of a different kind.

This is a point on which the behavioural and the humanistic traditions can be usefully joined, and it is worth putting to students directly: the functional analysis is a genuinely valuable and rigorous tool, and it is capable of establishing things that no other method in this book can establish, and it says nothing whatever about the meaning the behaviour serves, and both of these are simply true.

## 6. Functional Assessment with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 Setting-specificity

The most important developmental observation is that the function of a behaviour in a young person is frequently **setting-specific**. A young person may be calm in the counselling room and dysregulated in the classroom; cooperative with one teacher and oppositional with another. A behaviour that is maintained by escape in one setting and by attention in another cannot be addressed by a single plan, and a practitioner who assesses only one setting will produce a plan that works in the assessed setting and not in the others.

The practical implication is that the assessment should be conducted in the setting where the behaviour occurs, by someone with access to it, and that the referral question should be reformulated accordingly. A referral that says "he is aggressive" should be replaced by "he is aggressive in the corridor at break, and here is the log showing when."

### 6.2 The developmental context of the consequences

The consequences available to a young person differ in important ways from those available to an adult, and the assessment must take account of them.

**The contingency density is lower.** Young people's environments are more structured, with more adult-determined consequences, and often fewer opportunities for the behaviour to produce an effect. A behaviour that would be reinforced by a glance in an adult setting may be entirely unreinforced in a classroom with six other pupils and a teacher who is trying to teach.

**The escape contingencies are typically more powerful.** School demands are aversive for many young people, and the escape behaviour is often strongly reinforced because the relief of the demand is immediate and large.

**The social consequences are peer-dominated.** For most adolescents, the social consequences of a behaviour outweigh the adult ones by a considerable margin, and a functional assessment that examines only adult responses will miss the functional relevance of peer approval entirely.

**The developmental appropriateness of the consequence changes the analysis.** A consequence that would be strongly reinforcing at fifteen is nearly irrelevant at six, and the value ordering that the young person reports should be treated as a clinical datum rather than as a set of preferences to be indulged.

### 6.3 The involvement of the family and the school

Applied behaviour analysis in a school or clinic is never a solo activity. The assessment happens in a system, the data are gathered by people in that system, and the plan requires their continued adherence. Two practical consequences follow.

First, the assessment should be designed so that the people who will implement the plan are involved in designing it. A plan designed in an office and delivered to a classroom is a plan with a low probability of being implemented as written, and the low fidelity will be misread as the failure of the intervention.

Second, the data should be collected by the people who will implement the plan, or at least by a person who works alongside them, because the fidelity of the data collection determines the validity of the whole process.

### 6.4 Provisional plans and the treatment integrity question

Where a full functional analysis is not feasible, the discipline permits a **provisional treatment** based on a descriptive assessment, on the explicit condition that the hypothesis is treated as provisional and is revisited. The practice of provisionally implementing the most likely hypothesis while continuing to collect data is a reasonable clinical compromise, and it is only reasonable when the data collection continues.

The risk that must be named is the one that runs through the whole discipline: a provisional hypothesis that is never tested becomes a settled assumption, and the behaviour that does not respond is attributed to the young person's characteristics rather than to the hypothesis. The clinical safeguard is a pre-agreed review point at which the question "has the hypothesis been tested?" is asked explicitly.
