## 8. How Culture Modifies Each Major Theory

Culture does not simply add a variable to a theory; it modifies the theory's premises. The following summary of the points at which culture enters each tradition is offered as a practical check.

### 8.1 Cognitive theory

**The premise at risk.** Cognitive theory assumes that the person interprets experience and acts on the interpretation, and that a maladaptive appraisal is a misapplication of a rational capacity. The cultural difficulty is that the appraisal is formed within a social world that the practitioner does not share, and the "irrational" appraisal may be an entirely rational response to a world that is not as safe, predictable, or just as the practitioner assumes.

**Specific modifications.** The cognitive therapist must establish what the young person's appraisals are of *specific situations*, and must not import the practitioner's own situational appraisals. The distinction between a thought and a belief must be made in the young person's own conceptual system rather than in the therapist's. Behavioural experiments must be designed in the young person's actual world — a test of the prediction "people will treat me fairly" requires a situation in which the young person is actually likely to be treated unfairly if the belief is correct, and this may be uncomfortable to arrange. The cognitive work on discrimination must be directed at the *appraisal of the discrimination*, and the practitioner must be careful not to require the young person to appraise their own appraisal of their own racism, which is a demand that many young people experience as a demand to accept the discrimination.

**The most common error.** The cognitive therapist who treats a young person's distrust of authority as a cognitive distortion to be corrected. Sometimes it is. Often it is accurate.

### 8.2 Behavioural theory

**The premise at risk.** Behavioural theory assumes that behaviour is learned and maintained by contingencies, and that the contingencies are the appropriate target of intervention. The cultural difficulty is that the contingencies are structured by social arrangements — the distribution of authority, the discipline practices of the school, the policing of the neighbourhood — and that an intervention which modifies the young person's behaviour without modifying the structure will produce compliance without change, or change at a high cost.

**Specific modifications.** The functional analysis must include the institutional contingencies, and not only the family's. A young person's non-attendance may be maintained by a school response that the school has not recognised as a contingency. The token economy and the incentive systems described in Chapter 28 raise distinct ethical questions in a school with a racialised discipline policy, because the differential application of reinforcement reproduces the differential application of discipline. And the behavioural practitioner must be alert to the possibility that the "problem behaviour" is a form of resistance to a structure that the young person has every reason to resist.

### 8.3 Humanistic and existential theory

**The premise at risk.** Humanistic theory assumes the self-actualising tendency, and it assumes that the conditions of growth are the conditions of relationship. The cultural difficulty is that the humanistic framework's account of the person is a distinctly Western and individualistic one: it presupposes a bounded, self-directed, self-expressing individual whose authentic self can be discovered by looking inward. In many cultural contexts the self is constituted relationally, and the "authentic" self that the young person should be guided towards may be a self that is more thoroughly embedded in a network than the framework allows.

**Specific modifications.** The concept of congruence must be examined: whose definition of authenticity is in use? A young person who is deeply committed to a family's expectations and also privately distressed by them is not "incongruent" in any simple sense; the situation is a conflict between a relational self and a personal one, and the conflict is a developmental and cultural problem rather than a symptom. The humanistic practitioner's own cultural location must be examined, since the "unconditional positive regard" that the framework offers is a form of regard that the practitioner extends on the basis of a particular understanding of persons.

### 8.4 Systemic and family theory

**The premise at risk.** Systemic theory assumes that the family is the primary system, and that the practitioner can enter it and work with its patterns. The cultural difficulty is that "family" is not one thing, that the boundaries of a family are culturally variable, and that the practitioner who imposes a nuclear-family model on a system that is structured differently will misdescribe it.

**Specific modifications.** The genogram and the structural map must be drawn around the family's own actual structure, which may include a grandparent who functions as a parent, an aunt who functions as a parent, a family business, a religious community, or a transnational set of relationships. The practice of including the parents in the work with a young person, which the evidence supports in many circumstances, must be examined for its fit: in a context where the family is a source of safety, excluding the family is a serious error; in a context where the family is a source of danger, including it may be dangerous. The genogram should also record the family's own history of migration, displacement, and loss, which is frequently the actual location of the difficulty.

### 8.5 Constructivist and narrative theory

**The premise at risk.** Constructivist theory assumes that the meaning a person makes is the appropriate object of work, and that the practitioner and the young person can negotiate a shared account. The cultural difficulty is that the practitioner holds institutional and professional power within the negotiation, and that the young person's account may be constrained by a discourse that positions them as deficient — a discourse that the practitioner, by virtue of professional training, is also inside.

**Specific modifications.** The narrative practitioner must be alert to the fact that the "dominant story" may be the dominant story of the practitioner's own culture, and that the young person's account is sometimes the only account that has not been suppressed. The practice of externalisation — separating the young person from the problem — is culturally sensitive, because the discourse that the practitioner externalises is the discourse that the young person is inside, and the young person may experience the externalisation as a subtle form of accusation.

## 9. Culture and the Therapeutic Relationship

The alliance literature reviewed in Chapter 5 is largely conducted with Western samples and largely operationalised in Western terms. The cross-cultural literature makes several adjustments that are directly relevant.

**The dyadic assumption.** The "therapeutic alliance" presupposes a two-person relationship. In many cultural contexts the significant relationship is not dyadic but triadic or collective, and the young person may expect that the family or the community is a party to the work. A practitioner who insists on a strictly dyadic frame may be excluding the very relationships that sustain the young person.

**The confidentiality expectation.** Confidentiality is one of the clearest cultural variants in the alliance literature. Many young people from collectivist or community-oriented backgrounds expect that what they say will be shared with the family, and may regard a strict confidentiality norm as evidence that the practitioner does not understand the family. A practitioner who imposes strict confidentiality without discussion may inadvertently communicate distrust of the family. The solution is to make the confidentiality arrangement explicit and to discuss it, which is the practice the cultural formulation interview supports.

**The idealisation of the practitioner.** In cultures with high respect for authority and for expertise, the young person may present an idealised and uncritical view of the practitioner, and the alliance may be strong precisely because the young person is not treating the practitioner as a person they can be honest with. The relationship is then not a working alliance but an idealisation, and the working material has to be found in a different way. This is a configuration that the psychodynamic and relational literatures describe and that the cross-cultural literature confirms.

**Verbosity and disclosure norms.** The norms of self-disclosure differ across cultures and across families, and a young person whose family values restraint will produce a sparse narrative that will be misread as a lack of engagement or a limited capacity for insight.

## 10. Culture and Assessment

Assessment with a young person from a minority background is where cultural failure is most likely and most consequential, and four specific problems recur.

**Diagnostic misreading.** The standard diagnostic criteria contain cultural assumptions about the phenomenology of conditions, and the effect of the cultural misreading is to inflate the diagnostic rate in minority populations, with consequences that follow from the diagnosis for treatment, for medication, for institutional referral, and for the young person's own self-understanding. The studies of psychosis in African and Caribbean populations, and of ADHD across cultural groups, are the best-documented examples.

**The family as the problem.** Where the family's cultural practice is unfamiliar to the practitioner — a practice involving a parent with a physical disability, a family that has recently migrated, a grandmother who is the primary carer — the difficulty can be misread as a family dysfunction. Chapter 33 addresses the specific dynamics.

**The neglect of the social determinants.** Standard assessments ask about the young person's internal state, their symptoms, their history, and their functioning, and they do not systematically ask about the conditions in which the young person lives. A young person who is not sleeping because the family has one room for four people and no heating, who is not attending because the journey is unsafe after a fight at school last year, and who is not eating because the family's income halved when the father lost his work, has a presenting picture that will be misread if the assessment does not ask.

**The instrument problem.** The standard measures were normed on Western, white, and often adult samples, and their use with a young person from a different background can produce a misleading score. This does not mean the instruments should not be used; it means that the score should be treated as one source of information, with the cultural meaning of the item examined before the number is interpreted.

## 11. Culture, the School, and the Family

Two institutions dominate the lives of young people and both are culturally positioned in ways that a culturally responsive practitioner must understand.

**The school.** The school is the institution with which a young person from a minority background is most likely to have a conflict, and the reasons are structural: the differential application of discipline, the differential treatment of the same behaviour by different teachers, the under-representation of minority staff, the linguistic mismatch between the young person's home language and the language of instruction, and the tendency to interpret cultural difference as behavioural deficit. A school referral, in other words, is itself a culturally meaningful event, and the practitioner should ask what has happened at school before interpreting the referral as evidence of the young person's disturbance.

**The family.** The family is the young person's primary system and, for many young people from minority backgrounds, the primary source of both stress and resilience. The cultural-responsiveness literature is clear that the family should be engaged where it is safe to do so and where engagement is not required as a condition of the young person's care, and the discipline here is real: a practitioner who insists on parental involvement as a precondition of treatment will exclude precisely those young people for whom the parents are part of the problem.

The practice of a culturally responsive family assessment is set out in Appendix A and developed in Chapter 33.

## 12. Case Illustration: The Fifth Formatter

**The referral.** A fifteen-year-old boy, Idriss, is referred by his school for "non-compliance, low mood, and possible ADHD." The referral states that "he doesn't listen, doesn't do the work, has detention every week, doesn't want to be in school, and his mum says he's the same at home." The school has previously considered a behaviour plan and has discussed, at a parents' evening, whether a diagnosis of conduct disorder might be appropriate.

**The background, obtained with consent.** Idriss is a recent arrival, having come to the area eight months ago, having lived with his grandmother and two aunts in another city while his mother worked. His mother, Fatou, is now working two jobs, one of which involves a night shift, and is in the process of applying for housing; she has been on the waiting list for eleven months. Idriss speaks a second language at home and has been at this school for eight months. He describes the school as "fine" and gives no more detail. He has not been physically aggressive, but he has refused to comply with three separate staff on at least six occasions each, and has been in detention "loads of times." His grades are poor in two subjects and average in the rest.

**The cultural formulation interview.** The practitioner asks the identity question first. Idriss names his family, his country, his language, and his football team, and does not name a country as a central identity. The explanatory models question is the pivotal one, and Idriss's answer is: "My mum says I don't try because I've decided I don't want to be here. I think she says that because she's tired. I think it's because the teachers talk like I can't understand them and I do understand them. I think they don't know I came from another school where you could ask questions."

**The formulation.** The practitioner now has a competing account, and the important work is to hold both without discarding the school's. The school's account — that Idriss is non-compliant and possibly has a conduct disorder — is not an unreasonable reading of a referral of this kind, and it is what a service that does not ask the explanatory models question would write. But the information now available supports a different formulation, and it is one that the referral did not contain.

The formulation is that Idriss's non-compliance is **a response to an experience of being treated as incapable of understanding**, in a school where his accent, his questions, and his previous educational history mark him as a foreign body, and where the school has responded to his non-compliance with detention, which he experiences as confirmation. The attendance data are consistent with this: Idriss's absences cluster in the two subjects in which he is weakest, and in those subjects the teachers are those who report that he "doesn't try." The low mood is consistent as well, though the practitioner notes that the mood data are derived from a rating scale that has not been validated in his second language, and treats them with corresponding caution.

The ADHD question is left open. The practitioner is explicit that there is no information supporting it — no information on early development, no information on the pattern across settings, no information from the previous school — and that the school has generated the referral partly because a diagnostic label would make the school unaccountable for a young person it does not know how to teach.

**What the practitioner does.** Three things, in this order.

First, she asks Idriss's mother to come to the next session, and asks Idriss whether that is all right, and does not tell him what she wants to discuss. She is careful, because she knows that the mother's account will be challenged and that Idriss will know it. She also says to the mother, before the session, that her task is to understand what is hard for her and what she is managing, and that the school's account of the problem as a difficulty with Idriss is one of several, and that she would like to hear hers.

Second, she contacts the two subject teachers separately, with consent, and asks a functional question: what specifically does Idriss do when he does not comply, and what happens immediately before and after? She also asks them what happens with a student who asks a question. The answer is instructive, and she does not share it with the school's senior leadership, because the practitioner is not the school's agent and the teachers are not her clients.

Third, she does not make a diagnosis, and she does not accept the school's framing of a possible conduct disorder. She writes to the school, with consent, stating what she is doing, what she is not able to offer, and when she will report back. The letter is careful, non-confrontational, and specific.

**What happens.** Fatou's account is that Idriss is exhausted, that the school is fifteen miles from home and that the bus takes an hour, that she is working nights and that he is effectively a young person living alone, and that he was thriving at his previous school. The assessment of the extended family reveals that his grandmother, who is 71 and has diabetes, has been the person getting him up and fed, and that his mother has not been able to be home on weekday mornings since the second job started. The functional data from the teachers show that Idriss's non-compliance occurs almost exclusively in two classes, and that both teachers' responses to his questions are curtailed.

The plan that follows is not a treatment plan in the first instance. It is a set of practical arrangements — the bus, the timetable, the grandmother's capacity, the mother's shift pattern, the housing application — and the counselling work begins with the relationship and with Idriss's account of his own competence, which is the thing the school has been inadvertently attacking for eight months.

**The teaching point.** Nothing in the case required a new technique. It required the explanatory models question. A culturally unresponsive service would have received the school's referral, applied a deficit model, considered a diagnosis, and recommended a behaviour plan, and would have found the young man more difficult in a year. The difference between the two outcomes is a difference of formulation, and the formulation was available at the first session in a question that costs nothing to ask.

## 13. Evidence and Research Support

**Well supported.** That discrimination and minority stress are associated with adverse mental health outcomes in adolescents, with dose-dependent effects and a substantial mediating role for appraisal. That a strong, positive ethnic identity is associated with better mental health, academic engagement, and resilience. That the presence of a stable and supportive adult is one of the strongest protective factors across problems and populations. That cultural identity development follows a developmental sequence and is associated with a range of outcomes. That family and community connectedness is protective across cultures.

**Supported with qualifications.** That culturally adapted interventions produce better outcomes than their unadapted originals; the direction of the finding is generally positive and the effect is usually modest, and the studies are few and generally not of high quality. That the practitioner–client cultural match improves outcomes; the finding is inconsistent, and the stronger version of the claim — that match is a condition of effective work — is not supported by the evidence.

**Weakly supported or contested.** That bicultural stress is a distinct, measurable phenomenon with distinctive sequelae; the concept is theoretically useful and empirically under-specified. That acculturation gap is a cause of family conflict in immigrant families; the concept has been substantially revised in the light of criticisms of its assumptions about family structure and about the direction of cultural change, and the empirical findings are weak.

**Absent.** Robust evidence on the differential effectiveness of approaches for young people of different cultural groups; evidence on culturally adapted interventions with specific populations; evidence on the long-term effects of cultural identity development.

**A note on the strength of these findings.** The research on culture and mental health is, in general, of better quality than the research on psychotherapy effectiveness, and it is a considerable irony that a field which has spent three decades calling for evidence-based practice has produced a body of culturally sensitive research that is stronger than much of its own treatment research. A student should be able to say this without defensiveness: the evidence for the importance of culture is better than the evidence for the interventions the field delivers.

## 14. Strengths and Limitations of the Culturally Responsive Framework

**Strengths.** It identifies a genuine and consequential failure in the mainstream tradition: that theories and practitioners have taken the dominant group's norms as universal, and that this failure has produced measurable harm. It provides a set of practical instruments — the cultural formulation interview, the levels of analysis, the models of identity development, the minority stress framework — that can be used in real cases. It redirects attention to the social conditions of the young person's life, which is where the largest effects are. And it makes the practitioner's own assumptions an object of study, which is ethically necessary and practically useful.

**Limitations.** The evidence base for culturally adapted interventions is thinner than the claims. The concept of culture remains under-theorised in relation to the individual-level variables the clinical tradition measures, so the framework describes the social context well and the interaction between the social context and the individual poorly. The emphasis on the cultural "other" can, if not handled carefully, re-centre the dominant group's practices as the norm, which is precisely the problem the framework was developed to address. And the framework's emphasis on the practitioner's self-examination, while correct, can become a preoccupation that crowds out the practical work.

## 15. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

The ethical requirements of culturally responsive practice are specified in the codes of the profession, and the requirements are more specific than they first appear.

**Non-discrimination.** The codes prohibit discrimination on the basis of race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, age, disability, and socioeconomic status. The prohibition extends to the assessment process, and a practitioner who applies a standard instrument without examining its cultural assumptions and without examining whether the resulting score is meaningful in the young person's context has discriminated in effect.

**Cultural humility and self-assessment.** The codes require the practitioner to examine their own assumptions, biases, and power. The requirement is ongoing and it is specific: the practitioner should be able to name, in writing, the populations they assume they understand well, the populations they assume they do not, and the kinds of young person whose case they are most likely to misformulate.

**Communication.** The codes require that the practitioner communicate in a manner that the client can understand, and that requirement has a cultural dimension that is easy to miss. Communication includes the interpretation of silence, of directness, of eye contact, of the appropriate register of address, of the use of humour, and of the appropriate degree of directness in discussing difficult matters. A practitioner who is comfortable with directness will misunderstand a young person whose cultural norm is indirectness, and will reach the wrong conclusion about the young person's insight, their engagement, or their honesty.

**Power.** The profession's codes increasingly require attention to the ways in which the counselor–client relationship is shaped by social power, and the third-generation literature insists that this is not optional. A practitioner working with a young person whose family has no secure housing, whose parents work two jobs, and who is at risk of school exclusion is working with a young person whose access to the treatment itself is a function of the family's economic position. A service that requires weekly attendance during a period of family crisis is requiring a resource the young person may not have. Chapter 48 treats this in detail.

## 16. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Cultural responsiveness** — The requirement that an intervention be examined for the assumptions it carries about the person and about what constitutes a problem, and adapted where those assumptions do not hold for the young person.
- **Cultural humility** — A stance of continuous openness to the possibility that the practitioner's framework is partial and that the people they work with hold knowledge they have not been taught.
- **Cultural competence** — The first-generation construct in which the practitioner acquires knowledge of a cultural group in order to work more effectively with its members; now generally regarded as an inadequate model.
- **Anthropocentrism** — The assumption, identified in the first-generation multicultural literature, that mainstream theory and practice take the norms of the dominant culture as universal.
- **Minority stress** — The theory that the experience of chronic discrimination, and of the threat of discrimination, constitutes a chronic stressor with predictable mental health consequences.
- **Cultural taxation** — The additional burden borne by members of marginalised groups who are also responsible for representing their group to the dominant culture.
- **Microaggression** — A commonplace, low-level, often inadvertent instance of discriminatory intent or effect, which accumulates over time.
- **Ethnic identity** — A sense of belonging to and identification with an ethnic group, with an associated exploration of that group's history, values, and meaning; a protective factor when strong and positive.
- **Cultural identity development** — The developmental progression through which a young person examines, accepts, and integrates their cultural identity; modelled as pre-identity, exploration, and achievement.
- **Acculturation** — The process of cultural change following contact between cultures, involving shifts in behaviour, identity, and social organisation.
- **Biculturalism** — The state of holding and participating in two cultural systems, which is not a stage of acculturation but a configuration in its own right.
- **Acculturation gap** — The construct describing the divergence between the acculturation of parents and of children in an immigrant family; the empirical basis for the construct has been substantially revised.
- **Intersectionality** — The principle that the systems of power operating through race, gender, class, disability, sexuality, and other categories intersect to produce experiences that cannot be understood by examining each category separately.
- **Cultural formulation interview** — A structured set of questions eliciting the young person's and family's explanatory models and the cultural meaning of the presenting problem; reproduced in Appendix A.
- **Explanatory models** — The young person's and family's own accounts of the cause of the problem, what has helped, and what is expected from treatment.
- **The cultural lens** — The five-level analytic frame — individual, interactional, family, community, societal — used to formulate a case culturally.
- **The social-context question** — The diagnostic question generated by the cultural lens: if the difficulty were entirely a product of the social context, what would we see?
- **Relational self** — A conception of the self as constituted through relationships rather than as a bounded individual, which is characteristic of many cultural contexts and is not accommodated by the individual-level clinical framework.
:::

## 17. Chapter Summary

The mainstream counseling tradition has taken the norms, values, and categories of the dominant culture as universal, and the multicultural literature has named this anthropocentrism and developed three successive responses: cultural competence, cultural awareness and humility, and cultural responsiveness with a social justice commitment. The third generation is the contemporary position, and it makes two claims: that no intervention is culturally neutral, and that many presentations of adolescent distress are produced by systemic conditions rather than by individual pathology.

The vocabulary of the field — culture, ethnicity, race, social identity, acculturation, biculturalism, intersectionality — has been refined, and the evidence base on culture and adolescent mental health is stronger than the profession's ambivalence suggests: discrimination and minority stress are reliably associated with adverse outcomes, a strong and positive ethnic identity is reliably protective, and the presence of a stable and supportive adult is one of the strongest protective factors known. Where the evidence is thin — on the superiority of cultural adaptation, on differential effectiveness across cultural groups, and on long-term outcomes — the field's confidence outruns its data.

The cultural lens provides a practical instrument: a five-level analysis of the individual, the interaction, the family, the community, and the societal context, together with the social-context question that tests whether the young person's symptoms are explicable without individual pathology at all. The cultural formulation interview operationalises the approach and functions simultaneously as a clinical, a quality, and an ethical instrument.

The chapter's case, of a fifteen-year-old whose school referred him for non-compliance, was not resolved by a new technique. It was resolved by the explanatory models question, and by the practitioner's willingness to let the young person's account compete with the referral's.

## 19. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 8.1 — The Cultural Formulation Interview Practicum (2 hours, triads)

One student plays a fourteen-year-old who has recently migrated and is failing at school; one plays the parent, who interprets the school as hostile; one conducts the interview using the questions in Appendix A. The interviewer is required to ask the explanatory models question and to ask the social-context question in the form given in Section 4.1. The observers produce a one-page formulation at all five levels, plus a list of three questions the interviewer should have asked. Rotate the roles. The parent role is the hardest and the most instructive.

### Activity 8.2 — The Assumption Audit in a Clinical Case (90 minutes, individual)

Take a case you have read in this book — Nadia, Emeka, Priya, Jordan, or Idriss — and write the five-level cultural formulation. Then write, in a separate column, the formulation you would have given from the standard clinical framework, with no cultural lens applied. Mark every place where the two differ, and specify what difference the cultural lens would make to the plan. The exercise is best done in writing because the differences are usually invisible in discussion.

### Activity 8.3 — The Practitioner's Own Map (2 hours, individual, confidential)

Draw your own five-level map: your family of origin, your cultural and linguistic background, the communities you belong to and do not belong to, the faith or secular commitments you hold, the professional training you received and the assumptions it installed, and the populations for which you predict you will misformulate. Then write the three assumptions you are most likely to impose on a young person. Keep this document; it is the raw material for the reflective journal in the course assessment and for the work in Chapter 48.
:::

## 20. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory and the history.** Read the multicultural counselling theory chapters in the standard survey texts, and then read the critical literature on the cultural competence model's limits. Note how quickly the field's language has changed in thirty years, and consider what each change was responding to.

**For the empirical base.** Read the primary studies on minority stress and adolescent mental health, the studies on ethnic identity and resilience, and the protective-factor literature on the presence of a supportive adult. The last of these is the most directly useful to a practitioner, and it is the least often cited.

**For the instruments.** Use the cultural formulation interview in practice, and read the manual that accompanies it. Then read the clinical literature on cultural formulation in specific populations, which is where the instrument's limitations appear.

**For the ethical work.** Read the professional codes' sections on non-discrimination and on cultural responsiveness, and read the third-generation and critical literature on power in the counseling relationship. This is the strand of the course in which the student's own conclusions are most likely to require revision, and it is best done with a peer group rather than alone.
