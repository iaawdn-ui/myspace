
## 7. Working with Reactions to Ending

Reactions may include relief, pride, indifference, grief, anger, anxiety, idealisation, devaluation, symptom return, or sudden requests for personal contact. Parents may feel gratitude, fear of losing support, or pressure to declare success. Practitioners may feel sadness, guilt, rescue urges, or relief; these require supervision.

Useful interventions include normalising mixed feelings, revisiting previous endings, allowing dissatisfaction to be spoken, and distinguishing ending counselling from ending care. Avoid interpreting every reaction as pathology. Sometimes anger is a proportionate response to an abrupt institutional decision.

Where deterioration occurs near ending, assess genuinely rather than assuming it is only separation protest. Extend or refer when risk warrants. At the same time, do not let every fluctuation indefinitely postpone a planned ending; build confidence in the wider support plan.

## 8. Practitioner Departure and Transfer

When a practitioner leaves, tell the young person as early as policy and certainty permit. Explain what will happen to records, who will receive a summary, whether a joint handover is possible, and what choices exist. Do not promise personal contact outside service systems.

A transfer summary should be concise and relevant: reason for referral, formulation, risk and safeguarding, interventions and response, current goals, medication or medical contacts where relevant, family and school arrangements, and what helps. Avoid sending entire process notes unless legally and clinically justified.

Joint handover sessions can help but may also overload. The young person should consent where possible and know what will be discussed.

## 9. Non-Attendance and Unplanned Endings

Services need humane non-attendance procedures. A missed appointment may indicate improvement, fear, transport problems, family conflict, forgetting, shame, or a confidentiality breach. Follow local policy, safeguarding duties, and the agreed contact method. Messages should not reveal sensitive information to whoever controls the phone.

Document attempts, close episodes transparently, and tell referrers what has occurred. Where risk is high, non-attendance may require escalation rather than administrative closure.

Practitioners should also examine service contributions to dropout: waiting times, inaccessible locations, culturally unsafe practice, poor alliance, inconvenient scheduling, or failure to involve the young person in goals.

## 10. Case Illustration: Ending Without Disappearing

**Presentation.** Tobi, seventeen, has completed twenty sessions for panic and school avoidance. Attendance and functioning have improved. The counsellor is leaving the school service in five weeks.

**Process.** The counsellor tells Tobi immediately rather than waiting until the final session. Tobi initially says, “Fine, I was going to stop anyway,” then misses the next appointment. A brief agreed text is sent without clinical detail. At the following session, Tobi acknowledges anger: adults “always go once things are working”.

The ending phase reviews the original panic diary, the mastery ladder, and the teacher arrangements that now belong to school rather than counselling. Tobi writes a future-self plan: early signs, first three steps, trusted adult, tutor check-in, and when to seek health care. A joint meeting with the school's pastoral lead confirms continuation of the entry and break plan. Tobi chooses not to transfer to another counsellor but knows the re-referral route.

At the final session, Tobi says the ending is “annoying but normal”. The phrase is clinically meaningful: separation has been survived without disappearance, punishment, or pretence that it does not matter.

## 11. Evaluation of Services and Practitioner Learning

Termination data should be examined by reason: planned completion, transfer, dropout, service capacity, moving, risk escalation, or dissatisfaction. Services should ask whether endings differ by ethnicity, disability, language, gender, care status, or deprivation. High completion rates may reflect selection rather than quality.

Client feedback at ending should include whether goals mattered, whether the young person felt respected, what was unhelpful, and what should change. Practitioner reflection can identify recurring difficulties with dependency, conflict, boredom, rescue, or avoidance of endings.

Evaluation is not only discharge measurement. It asks whether gains persist, whether referrals were accepted, whether school and family systems maintained changes, and whether re-referral reflects relapse, appropriate help seeking, or failure of step-down support.

## 12. Ethical Considerations

Ethical endings require honesty about time, competence, funding, and availability. Do not prolong treatment for practitioner income, training needs, research, or attachment. Do not terminate abruptly to punish non-compliance except where safety or professional boundaries require immediate action and policy supports it.

Post-termination contact should follow organisational and professional rules. Social media contact, personal messaging, gifts, and dual relationships require particular care with minors and former minor clients. Gratitude can be acknowledged warmly without creating private channels.

Closing records should distinguish outcome at ending from speculation about the future. Referrals should not exaggerate or minimise risk to secure acceptance. The young person should know what is being sent, subject to safeguarding exceptions.

## 13. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Termination** — The planned or unplanned conclusion of a counselling relationship.
- **Booster session** — A time-limited follow-up session to review maintenance and problem-solve setbacks.
- **Treatment dropout** — Client-initiated ending before agreed completion, whose meaning requires inquiry rather than assumption.
- **Step-down care** — Movement to a lower-intensity or more community-based form of support.
- **Handover** — Planned transfer of relevant information and responsibility between practitioners or services.
- **Relapse prevention** — Identification of warning signs, high-risk contexts, coping responses, and help-seeking routes.
:::

## 14. Chapter Summary

Ethical endings are honest, prepared, evaluative, and connective. They return authorship to the young person, transfer useful knowledge to the systems that remain, and leave a clear route back to help. A good ending does not pretend the relationship did not matter; it demonstrates that mattering does not require permanence.

3. What outcomes besides symptom scores should inform ending?
4. How might deterioration near ending be understood without dismissing real risk?
5. Write a transfer summary structure that protects privacy.
6. What makes a booster session ethical or unethical?
7. How can services detect inequitable patterns in endings?
