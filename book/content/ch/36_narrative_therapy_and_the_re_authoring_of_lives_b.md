
## 5. Narrative Work with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The developmental fit

Narrative therapy is used extensively with young people, and the reasons are developmental as well as practical.

**Adolescence is the period of the identity narrative.** The central developmental task of the period is the construction of a coherent account of who the young person is and who they are becoming, and the accounts available in the surrounding discourse — from the family, from the school, from the peers, from the media — are in competition with the one the young person is building.

**The dominant story is often assigned from outside.** A young person who is in trouble, who is unwell, or who is in a family that is under strain is the recipient of a story that is spoken about them rather than by them, and the developmental risk is that they will adopt it.

**The adolescent's capacity for narrative is fully developed.** The ability to hold a complex account of one's own life, to see that there are different ways of telling the same events, and to recognise that the account is a construction rather than a fact, is a late adolescent capacity, and the practice is appropriate to it.

**The group's format is developmentally suited**, because adolescents are highly attentive to the questions asked by their peers and highly sensitive to the possibility of being told what to do.

### 5.2 The initial conversation

The narrative first session is distinctive, and a student should know what it involves.

**The practitioner tells the young person what the practice does.** The explanation is direct: "I am not going to ask you why you do the thing you do. I am going to ask you what the thing does to you, and what you do to it, and I am going to help you write down the parts of your life that the thing has not managed to reach. And at the end we are going to write a new version of your story that includes those parts." The young people who stay with the practice are frequently the ones who have never been told that the problem is not their fault.

**The practitioner asks the young person to describe the problem in their own words**, and does not supply a label. "What do the adults at school call it? What do you call it?" The name the young person chooses is usually more vivid and more externalising than the diagnostic one, and the use of the young person's own name is the beginning of the externalisation.

**The practitioner locates the problem's network.** "Where does this thing show up in your life? What does it do to your sleep? To your friends? To what you do at the weekend?" and the answers are written down, and the writing is the map of the problem's influence.

**The practitioner looks for the unique outcomes from the first session**, and the first session should end with at least one, however small, and the therapist should not leave the session without one, because the young person's experience of the session will be determined by whether the practice delivered what it promised.

### 5.3 Working with the school and the family

**With the school.** The letter from the problem is the most useful document in a school setting, and the practitioner should explain to the young person how it works before it is written, so that the young person can decide whether they want it shared. A letter from the young person that says "The Panic makes me do the breathing and it tells me everyone is watching and it makes me go to the toilet and it makes me get sent out of class" gives the teachers something to work with that is not the young person.

**With the family.** The narrative approach is unusually well suited to family work, because the practice of the family is to find the dominant stories that circulate in the family and to examine them, and the re-authoring is a collective act. The practitioner can map the family's dominant stories — "we're the family where nothing works", "she's the one who was always the difficult one", "we don't talk about that" — and the unique outcomes can be located in the family's own history.

**The systemic re-authoring question.** "Who else would have to change their story to make room for this?" is the question that connects the individual work to the family and to the school, and it is the question that a practitioner who is working systemically should always ask.

### 5.4 The work with the unique outcomes

The process of developing a unique outcome requires patience, and a student should know the common obstacles.

**The young person cannot find one.** This is common in the early sessions, and the usual reason is that the question is too big. The remedy is to reduce the time scale: not "a time when the anxiety didn't happen" but "a moment in the last week when you felt more like yourself", and the smaller the frame the more likely the young person is to find something.

**The young person finds only negative unique outcomes.** A young person who says "the only time it was better was when I was on drugs" has found a genuine unique outcome, and the practitioner's task is to find the *components* of that experience. "What did you like about being on drugs? What did it give you that you wanted?" and the answer — the relief, the numbness, the confidence, the not-caring — is then located in other parts of the young person's life where the same thing can be had differently. This is the technique that the literature calls the **re-authoring of the unique outcome** and it is one of the most useful and least discussed.

**The young person produces a unique outcome that is too small.** "I was okay for about ten minutes" is a valid unique outcome and it is frequently dismissed by the practitioner, and it should not be, because the ten minutes can be examined for the factors present in it and the factors can be extended.

**The young person resists the re-authoring.** Some young people are invested in the dominant story, because it is a story in which they are the protagonist and it has a certain dignity, and the practitioner should note that the dominant story usually protects the young person from certain kinds of responsibility and from certain kinds of hope, and the examination should be gentle.

### 5.5 The limits with young people

**Not every young person can or will work this way.** The practice requires a capacity for reflection and a tolerance for ambiguity, and a young person in acute crisis, or with a very limited cognitive capacity, or with a very short attention span, may not be able to.

**The re-authoring can become a demand.** A young person who has been told that they must find their unique outcomes and who cannot, has been given another task they will fail, in a practice that promised them that they were the author.

**The problem is sometimes real and does not need externalising.** A young person who is being abused needs the abuse stopped, and the externalisation of "the abuse" into a separate agent, however therapeutically elegant, can be heard as a dilution of the reality.

## 6. Case Illustration: The Young Woman Who Had Become the Problem

**Presentation.** Keziah is sixteen and has been excluded from two schools in the past year. The referral is from her current school, which is worried about her attendance and about a fight in the corridor. Keziah's mother attends the first appointment and says, before the practitioner has said anything, "It's the same as it was with her father."

**The first individual session.** The practitioner does not begin with the history. The practitioner begins with the question: "What do the people at school call it? And what do you call it?" Keziah says: "They call me a behaviour problem. I call it the Black Hole." The practitioner asks what the Black Hole does, and Keziah says: "It eats everything. It ate my friends. It ate my year. It comes in at nine and it's there all day and it eats everything I try to do." The practitioner writes this down, and Keziah watches the words being written, and the practitioner says: "Can we keep going? What else does it eat?"

Keziah says: "It ate my music. It ate going to see my aunt. It ate my grades, but honestly the grades were gone before the Black Hole." The practitioner asks whether there is anything it has not eaten, and Keziah thinks and says: "It hasn't eaten my swimming."

**The unique outcome.** The practitioner develops the swimming. "Tell me about the swimming." Keziah talks for twenty minutes without interruption. She swims four times a week, before school, at a pool her aunt drives her to, and the times are the best parts of her week. The practitioner asks what is different about the swimming, and the answers are precise: she is in the water and nobody can talk to her; she is doing a thing she is good at; she is with a coach who tells her what to do and does not have a mood about it; and her phone is in her bag.

**The mother's account.** In the joint session, the mother says that Keziah has always been "a handful" and that she was exactly like her father, and the practitioner asks the mother, gently, to say more about the father. The mother says that he was "a lovely man until he drank" and that he left when Keziah was three, and that she has spent thirteen years being careful that Keziah does not become him. The practitioner does not pursue this, but the practitioner does ask one question: "What did Keziah look like at three?" and the mother says: "She was the funniest child. Absolutely." And she stops, because she has heard it.

**The dominant story.** The practitioner names it explicitly in the session with Keziah: "So the story is that you are going to end up like your dad, and everything you touch turns into the Black Hole, and there is nothing you can do about it. And you have been living inside that story since you were three." Keziah says: "That's my mum's story. That's not mine." The practitioner asks: "So what is your story?" and Keziah says: "My story is that I am the person who is in trouble." And then she says, after a pause: "And that other people decide what that means."

**The re-authoring.** The re-authoring is done with a document. The practitioner asks Keziah to write a letter to the Black Hole, and suggests the first line: "I know what you want." Keziah writes, and the letter is three paragraphs long, and the third paragraph is: "You are not allowed in the pool. You are not allowed at my aunt's. If you want to be fed, feed me at breakfast, not at nine o'clock at night." The practitioner reads it and asks: "Are you sure this is what you want to send?" and Keziah says: "Yes."

**The systemic work.** The letter is shared with the mother, with the form tutor, and with the swimming coach. The form tutor's response is the most striking thing in the case: the teacher, who had been receiving the letter as an account of a difficult pupil, writes back a note saying that the Black Hole "sounds exhausting" and that the pupil is "not the problem, then". The mother writes back to Keziah a note that is in the third person — she says that she has realised she has been writing Keziah's story for her — and it is the first time the two have spoken in six months.

**The outcome.** At three months Keziah is in one school, is attending four days a week, is still swimming four times a week, and is in contact with her mother. The fight in the corridor has not recurred. The school has not made any formal change to her plan, which the practitioner regards as a missed opportunity, and the practitioner has a further four sessions and uses them to work with the mother and with the school on the question of the expectations.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the model's distinctive operations. The problem was named by the young person, and the naming was an externalisation; the unique outcome was located with precision and its components were identified; the dominant story was named, and the naming revealed that it was a story written *about* Keziah rather than by her, and the young person's own correction — "and that other people decide what that means" — was the pivot of the case. The re-authoring was done in the young person's own written words, and the document was the mechanism of the change. And the systemic question — who else has to change their story — was answered productively, because the teacher and the mother each had a story about Keziah that they had never examined, and the letter from the problem gave them the material.

## 7. Evidence and Research Support

### 7.1 The status of the evidence

The evidence for narrative therapy is good in a specific and unusual way, and a student should be able to describe it accurately.

**The outcome research is the strongest in the family therapy field.** The studies that have used standardised outcome measures, and the practice-based research that has used single-case designs, have produced consistent findings of substantial improvement, and the effect sizes reported in the reviews are among the largest in the family therapy literature.

**The research on the distinctive outcomes is the most interesting contribution.** The studies of the *distinctive outcomes* — the specific changes in the young person's own language and in their own account that the research identifies as the markers of a successful narrative therapy — have identified a remarkably consistent set: the young person begins to use the problem's name less and their own attributes more; the unique outcomes become more detailed and more numerous; the young person begins to attribute change to their own actions; and the language of the dominant story shifts.

**The research on the group format is good**, and the studies of the storytelling group have found substantial effects and good retention.

**The evidence with young people is substantial**, across schools, community mental health, and family settings.

**The evidence base has been criticised on methodological grounds**, and the criticisms are worth knowing: the trials are frequently small, the fidelity of the delivery is difficult to measure and is rarely reported, and the practitioners who conduct them are often the developers of the model.

### 7.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The measures of the practice's distinctive mechanism are retrospective and interpretive**, and the identification of a "distinctive outcome" depends substantially on the researcher's judgement.

**The trials are few and small**, and the confidence intervals are wide.

**The generalisability to other cultures and other languages is uncertain**, given the practice's roots in a specific intellectual context.

**The evidence does not address the complex presentations**, and the practice with young people with severe mental health needs, with neurodevelopmental conditions, or with acute risk is largely untested.

## 8. Strengths and Limitations of Narrative Therapy

### 8.1 Strengths

**It is a theory of identity that is developmentally apt.** The account of the young person as an author of their own account is a better fit for the adolescent than most of the theories in this book.

**It locates the problem outside the person**, and it does so in a way that a young person can feel.

**It produces artefacts.** The letters and the documents are tangible, they belong to the young person, and they often outlast the therapy.

**It is compatible with systemic and family work**, and the re-authoring of a family story is a natural extension of the individual practice.

**The group format is efficient and powerful**, and it is unusual in producing a real sense of a collective practice.

**It gives the practitioner something to do when nothing is working.** The externalising questions work in almost any circumstance, and they work when the young person will not talk about the problem.

**It is explicitly attentive to power**, and it has produced some of the clearest ethical writing in the therapy field.

### 8.2 Limitations

**It can be used to individualise a structural problem.** This is the most serious criticism and it is discussed at length in Section 10.

**It requires a capacity for reflection that some young people do not have**, and the practice has relatively little to offer the young person in acute crisis.

**It is difficult to learn well.** The externalising questions are easy to learn and easy to misuse, and the practice's discipline lies in the quality of the listening, which is not teachable by a manual.

**Its conceptual foundations can be opaque in practice**, and the philosophical apparatus can obscure what the practitioner is actually doing.

**The measures of the practice are weak**, and the field has invested more in the articulation of its theory than in the evaluation of its outcomes.

## 9. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 The politics and the practice

Narrative therapy is the most explicitly political of the approaches in this book, and the practitioner should be able to state both the value of that and the danger of it.

**The value.** The practice's attention to how the dominant discourse constitutes identities is a genuine contribution, and it is what allows a young person who has been labelled to be understood as a person rather than as a label. The observation that a young person's difficulties are frequently produced by the responses of others to them — the school's avoidance, the family's worry, the referral — is clinically important, and the practice gives the practitioner a way of working with it.

**The danger.** The practice's language of the "dominant story" can be turned into an account in which the young person's difficulties are the product of other people's narratives, and in which the young person is thus not responsible for anything. A young person who has been sexually assaulted, or who has developed a serious mental illness, is not a person whose account has been imposed. The practice's own literature has been critical of this tendency in recent years, and the correction is that the story is not the reality, and the events are not the story.

### 9.2 Culture, identity, and the dominant story

**The dominant story is often a cultural story.** A young person who is a member of an ethnic minority in a majority population, who is an immigrant, who is a young person in a family with a particular religious practice, may be living inside a dominant cultural story about people like them, and the practice's externalising questions may be the most direct way of working with that.

**The practice's origins are in a particular intellectual tradition**, and a practitioner who uses the externalising questions should know something about the structuralist and post-structuralist theory and about the feminist critique of the professional claim to know, because the questions make claims and the claims should be able to be defended.

**Naming the problem in a particular way can be culturally loaded.** A name given to a problem in one tradition may carry a specific theological or moral meaning in another, and the practitioner should ask.

**The re-authoring can overwrite the family's or the community's account.** A young person who is invited to author a new account of their life may be implicitly invited to author a *Western individualist* account, and the young person who has a strongly communal sense of self may find the practice strange or offensive.

### 9.3 Ethical requirements

**The consent to externalise is not automatic.** A young person must consent to the practice, and the consent should be informed, and the young person should be told that the practitioner will be asking about the problem in a particular way.

**The confidentiality of the documents.** A letter from the problem contains material the young person may not want a parent or a teacher to read, and the practitioner must establish what may be shared before the document is written rather than after.

**The power of the practitioner over the young person's account.** The practitioner is the one with the questions, and the young person may come to believe that the practitioner has a view about the right account. The discipline is to be genuinely curious, and to notice the times when the practitioner's preferred direction is being imposed.

**Not every problem is a story.** A young person with a medical condition, with a neurodevelopmental condition, or with a substance dependence requires treatment, and the re-authoring is not a substitute for it.

**The practitioner's own position.** A narrative practitioner is not a neutral observer of a story, and the practitioner who is part of a dominant discourse is likely to reproduce it inadvertently, particularly in the questions they think to ask. This should be examined in supervision.

**The social conditions.** The practice's own framework insists that the problem is produced in a social field, and the practitioner who externalises a young person's difficulties while ignoring the conditions in which the young person lives has done the practice's work in name and its opposite in substance. A young person whose problem is a school that is unsafe, or a family that is exhausted by two shifts of work, or a housing situation that is impossible, needs those things changed, and a re-authored story is not a substitute.

## 10. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Narrative therapy** — The practice developed by White and Epston in which the young person's identity is understood as constituted by the stories they live by, and in which the work is the re-authoring of those stories.
- **The person as the author of their life** — The foundational claim that the account of a life is a draft, revisable, and made by the person whose life it is.
- **Externalisation** — The practice of separating the person from the problem so that the problem becomes an object that can be seen and described, and the person becomes the subject of their own life.
- **The grammar of externalisation** — The shift from "the problem makes you do" to "you do", in which the problem is given the attributes of an agent and the person the attributes of a subject.
- **Naming the problem** — The practice of giving the problem a name, usually the young person's own, and of writing that name down.
- **The problem's influence** — The network of effects the problem has on the young person's life: what it prevents, what it makes them do, and what it costs them.
- **The young person's influence** — The network of effects the young person has on their own life and on others, and the map that is usually thinner than the first and always more useful.
- **Unique outcome** — An event, moment, feeling, thought, or piece of learning that does not fit the dominant story and that opens a space for a different one.
- **The five criteria of a unique outcome** — The young person's own, a specific event rather than a generalisation, a departure from the dominant story, sufficiently detailed to be re-told, and linked to a capacity the young person already has.
- **Dominant story** — The account of the self that the young person lives by, with its subject position, its plot, and its moral.
- **Problem-saturated story** — The older term for the dominant story, in which the young person is the cause of the problem.
- **Re-authoring** — The process of expanding the account of the young person's life so that it includes the unique outcomes, without requiring the negative account to have been false.
- **The difference between a reframe and a re-authoring** — A reframe replaces a negative account with a positive one; a re-authoring expands the account and makes room for the exceptions.
- **The letter from the problem** — A letter written in the problem's voice describing what it has done and what it wants, usually written by the young person.
- **The letter to the problem** — A letter written by the young person to the problem, in which the young person sets the limits on what it may do.
- **The letter to the young person** — A letter from the practitioner recording what they have noticed and what they value.
- **The take-away letter** — A letter the young person writes to themselves, containing the unique outcomes and the re-authored account.
- **The therapeutic group** — The group in which the re-authoring is done collectively, with the listening restricted to the questions "what was different?" and "how come?".
- **The two permitted questions** — "What was different?" and "How come?", and the exclusion of advice, interpretation, and suggestions.
- **The re-authoring of the unique outcome** — The technique of locating the components of a negative unique outcome and finding them in other areas of the young person's life.
- **The systemic re-authoring question** — "Who else would have to change their story to make room for this?", which connects the individual work to the family and the school.
- **The fabula and the syuzhet** — The raw material of lived events and the organising shape given to them, from narrative theory.
- **The narrative frame** — The structure that selects certain events and excludes others, and the reason that the events excluded from a young person's story are the clinical target.
- **The excised text** — The parts of the story that the frame excludes, and which the practice seeks to recover.
- **Post-structuralist narrative analysis** — The intellectual tradition that treats meaning as made in discourse rather than found in events.
- **Feminist epistemology** — The critique of the professional claim to know the truth about a client, particularly a woman.
- **The problem is not the person** — The reframe that narrative practice offers, and its two corollaries in the externalising work.
- **Meaning is made, not found** — The proposition that the meaning of an experience lies in the account given of it and that the account can be different.
- **The document in the school setting** — The letter from the problem used to convert the teacher's response from a reaction to the young person into a response to the problem.
- **The dominant cultural story** — The account available in a society about a category of person, which the practice's externalising questions can address directly.
:::

## 11. Chapter Summary

Narrative therapy emerged in the late 1980s from a combination of literary theory, feminist epistemology, post-colonial thought, and social work practice research, and its central claim is that a person's identity is constituted by the stories they live by and tell about themselves, and that the stories are not the person. The claim is practical rather than abstract: the story through which a young person has organised a genuinely difficult life is now producing the outcomes.

The intellectual sources were described and the development of the movement in Australia and New Zealand was traced, including the sharp break with the family therapy tradition and the prior question the practitioners asked: by what criteria is this family understood to be a family at all.

The core concepts were developed in full. The person as the author of their life was described with its three corollaries — that identity is not fixed, that the problem is not the person, and that meaning is made rather than found. The externalisation was described with its technical vocabulary: the naming of the problem, the writing of the problem's influence, the writing of the problem's influence on the young person's influence on others, the identification of the unique outcomes, and the authoring of a new story. The problem's influence and the young person's influence were described as the two maps, with the observation that the second is almost always thinner and that the discovery of the young person's existing influence is the beginning of a different account.

The unique outcome was described with its five criteria, and the dominant story with its subject position, plot, and moral. The re-authoring was described and distinguished from the positive reframe: a reframe replaces, a re-authoring expands, and the expansion does not require the negative account to have been false.

The techniques were described in detail: the externalising questions and the rules for them; the four documents and their specific uses, in the therapy room, in the school, and in the letter to the young person; and the therapeutic group with its two permitted questions, which are the mechanism of the practice.

The developmental fit with young people was developed through the identity narrative, the externally assigned dominant story, the adolescent's capacity for narrative, and the group's suitability. The initial conversation was described, and the specific modifications for the school and the family were set out, including the systemic re-authoring question. The four obstacles to developing a unique outcome were described with their remedies, including the re-authoring of a negative unique outcome, which is one of the most useful and least discussed techniques in the model.

The case of Keziah demonstrated the model's distinctive operations. The problem was named by the young person and the naming was the externalisation; the unique outcome was located with precision and its components identified; the dominant story was named and the young person's own correction — that other people decide what that means — was the pivot; the re-authoring was done in her own written words; and the letter from the problem gave the teacher and the mother the material to examine the stories they had been telling about her.

The evidence was presented with its unusual structure: the outcome research is among the strongest in the family therapy field, the research on the distinctive outcomes is the most interesting contribution and has identified a consistent set of markers, and the criticism of the methodological quality is legitimate. The strengths and limitations were set out, and the principal limitation — the individualisation of a structural problem — was identified.

The cultural and ethical analysis was substantial. The politics of the practice was examined from both sides: its value in allowing a young person who has been labelled to be understood as a person, and the danger of an account in which the young person is not responsible for anything. The cultural questions were addressed: the dominant story is often a cultural story, the practice's intellectual origins carry commitments that should be known, the naming of a problem can be culturally loaded, and the re-authoring can overwrite a communal account of the self. The ethical requirements closed the chapter: the consent to externalise, the confidentiality of the documents, the power of the practitioner over the young person's account, the recognition that not every problem is a story, the practitioner's own position, and the social conditions, which the practice's own framework insists upon and which a re-authored story cannot substitute for.

## 13. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 36.1 — The Externalising Question Set (60 minutes, individual)

Write a full set of externalising questions for a specific problem you have encountered, then rewrite each one so that it could only be answered by the problem and not by the person. Then write the variant of each question that would inadvertently re-internalise the problem, and mark it. The exercise teaches the grammar of the practice, and the second half of it teaches the discrimination that distinguishes a practitioner who has internalised the discipline from one who has only learned the questions.

### Activity 36.2 — Finding a Unique Outcome (90 minutes, field-based)

Take a young person from placement and find one unique outcome: a specific event, in the last month, that does not fit the dominant story. Write it out in the young person's words, in the detail in which they described it. Then identify the components — who was present, what was happening, what the young person was doing, what they were wearing, where they were, what the mood of the setting was. Then write the small step that transfers one component into a context where the problem usually operates. The exercise is the core technique of the chapter, and the component analysis is the part that practitioners most often skip and that makes the difference between an outcome and a wish.

### Activity 36.3 — The Therapeutic Group (90 minutes, small group)

In a group of four or five, one member tells a short story of a difficulty, and the others are restricted to the two permitted questions — "what was different?" and "how come?" — for five minutes. Then the storyteller reports what they noticed. The group then processes what it was like to be restricted in this way, and what it was like to receive only those questions. Most groups find the restriction surprisingly hard and the questions surprisingly powerful, and both findings are the point.

### Activity 36.4 — The Dominant Story Audit (75 minutes, individual)

Take a young person from placement and write their dominant story in three sentences, in the third person, as it would be told by the school, by the family, and by the young person. Then write the "who else would have to change their story to make room for this?" answer for each. Then write the practical step for the two people whose stories are most clearly shaping the young person's outcomes. The audit is the exercise that connects the narrative work to the systemic and consultative work of Chapters 33 and 56, and it is the most useful single exercise in the chapter.
:::

## 14. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory.** Read White and Epston (1990) and then White (2007), and read Bruner (1990) for the account of narrative as a mode of human reality. Ricoeur (1984) is the philosophical source and is worth reading in the first part only.

**For the practice.** Read White and Stone (2008) and Epston and White (1995). Then read the group format literature, which is the most immediately usable part of the practice.

**For the intellectual sources.** Read the feminist epistemology literature on the professional claim to know, and read the post-colonial and social constructionist literature on discourse and identity. A student who knows these sources will use the externalising questions with more care and with far less mystification.

**For the evidence.** Read the outcome studies and the distinctive outcomes research, and read the critical appraisals alongside them.

**For the critique.** Read the critiques of narrative practice for its treatment of structural problems and for its individualistic framing, and read the feminist and post-colonial critiques from within the field. The field's own self-criticism is the most useful preparation for working with it responsibly.
