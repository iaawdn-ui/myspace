# Schema Therapy: Modes, Styles, and Early Maladaptive Schemas

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the development of schema therapy, including Young's departure from the cognitive tradition and the relationship to the Beckian research tradition.
- Explain what an early maladaptive schema is, and describe the five domains and the eighteen schemas.
- Explain the three coping styles — surrender, avoidance, and overcompensation — and their relationship to the schemas.
- Describe the mode model, including the child modes, the dysfunctional coping modes, the dysfunctional parent modes, and the Healthy Adult mode.
- Construct a schema and mode map for a case.
- Describe the principal techniques of schema therapy and state their indications and cautions.
- Apply schema thinking to work with children and adolescents, and specify the developmental modifications.
- Evaluate the evidence for schema therapy and for the specific components.

## Chapter Overview

Schema therapy was developed by Jeffrey Young and his colleagues in the 1980s and 1990s, initially as an extension of Beck's cognitive therapy for patients with what Young termed "characterological" problems — pervasive, characterological, and typically developmental difficulties that did not respond to the standard cognitive techniques. Beck himself was for many years an opponent of the new direction, on the ground that the schema model's constructs lacked empirical specificity; the dispute between the two traditions is one of the most instructive in the history of the field, and it has been substantially resolved by subsequent research.

The theory's central claims are that a young person's difficulties are maintained not only by current beliefs but by **early maladaptive schemas** — patterns of experience and affect, concerning the self and others, formed in childhood, and enduring across the lifespan; that the young person responds to these schemas with one of three **coping styles** — surrender, avoidance, or overcompensation — each of which is an attempt to cope with the schema and each of which maintains it; and that the young person's experience is organised in **modes** — distinct psychological states, some of them childlike, some of them "parent-like," and one of them, the Healthy Adult, from which the therapy is conducted.

The theory is of considerable relevance to work with young people, and for a reason that is not obvious at first: it is one of the very few theories in the field that takes seriously the developmental origins of a young person's difficulties, and it does so in a way that is compatible with a developmental practice rather than in opposition to one. A schema is by definition formed early, and a young person with a schema of abandonment is a young person whose developmental task of trust has been obstructed. This gives the therapist a language for the developmental work that is unusually precise.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 Beck's research programme

The single most important development in the psychotherapy research literature in the 1980s was Aaron Beck's programme of psychotherapeutic research, funded by the National Institute of Mental Health, which demonstrated that specific treatments could produce specific, measurable improvements in specific disorders, and which established the methodology — the manualised treatment, the controlled trial, the components analysis, the prediction study — that transformed the profession's claim to scientific status.

The programme produced two of the field's most robust findings, which are also among its most striking. The first is the finding that a substantial proportion of patients with personality disorders, chronic depression, and complex presentations do not respond to standard cognitive therapy. The second is the finding that the *personality* of the patient — its structure, its rigidity, its depth — predicts the outcome of treatment at least as well as the symptom, and that the treatments that work for these patients are those that address the characterological level rather than the symptom level.

### 1.2 Young's departure

Jeffrey Young was a member of Beck's research team, and the second finding above is what made his work possible. Young's observation, developed through the clinical application of Beck's cognitive therapy with difficult patients, was that the standard techniques worked on the surface and left the underlying structure untouched, and that the patients who did not respond had a characteristic profile: they were rigid, they had interpersonal difficulties that the cognitive techniques did not address, they were prone to a recurrence of their difficulties under stress, and their beliefs were deep, unconditional, and global rather than situational.

Young's proposal was that what the standard cognitive model described as "core beliefs" were better understood as **early maladaptive schemas** — patterns of experience and affect, concerning the self and others, formed during childhood and adolescence, and enduring throughout the life span, and functioning as the basis for a wide range of later difficulties. The theory's claim is that the schemas are not merely beliefs but are deeply held **patterns of feeling and thinking about the self and others**, with their own developmental history.

### 1.3 The dispute with Beck

Beck was a long-standing opponent of schema therapy, and the reasons are instructive. Beck's objection was that the schema constructs were not specified with the precision necessary for empirical test, that the term "schema" was used in a way that implied a theoretical construct without an operational definition, and that the theory was in danger of becoming unfalsifiable.

The dispute was eventually resolved empirically, in a series of studies in the 1990s and 2000s that demonstrated the construct validity of the schema constructs, the reliability of the self-report and clinician-rated measures of the constructs, and their predictive validity for treatment outcome. The resolution did not produce a final synthesis of the two traditions, and the two remain distinct in practice, but the outcome of the dispute was a substantial increase in the field's respect for the characterological level.

### 1.4 The expansion of the theory

The theory has been extended in several directions since the 1990s: to the treatment of personality disorders, with the development of the schema-focused treatments for borderline and avoidant personality disorder; to group and to couple therapy; to the developmental and childhood applications, which are the subject of Section 7; and to the emotion-focused and somatic traditions, with which the theory has become increasingly integrated.

## 2. Early Maladaptive Schemas

### 2.1 The definition

An **early maladaptive schema** is a pattern of experience and affect, concerning the self and others, that is formed in childhood, is deeply felt, and is enduring across the lifespan. The definition has five components, each of which matters.

**"Pattern"** — the schema is a generalisation, not a specific memory. A young person with a schema of abandonment does not have a specific memory of abandonment that is being retrieved; they have a general expectation, built from many instances, that people will leave.

**"Experience and affect"** — the schema includes the emotional quality of the original experience, not only its cognitive content. The schema of abandonment is not only the belief "people will leave"; it is also the feeling of the small child's fear and grief.

**"Concerning the self and others"** — the schema is always about a relation between the self and others.

**"Formed in childhood"** — the developmental origin is essential to the construct, and a schema formed at four and a schema formed at fourteen are different in their implications.

**"Enduring"** — the schema persists across the lifespan and across the life circumstances, which is why it is not adequately addressed by working on current beliefs.

### 2.2 The five domains and the eighteen schemas

The schemas are organised into five domains.

**Disconnection and rejection** — the sense of not belonging, of being fundamentally unacceptable or unwanted. The schemas: Abandonment/Instability; Mistrust/Abuse; Emotional Deprivation; Defectiveness/Shame; Social Isolation/Distrust.

**Impaired Autonomy and Performance** — the sense of being impeded in the ability to act independently, to express one's needs, and to function. The schemas: Dependent/Submissive; Vulnerability to Danger or Illness; Unrelenting Standards; Self-Sacrifice; Approval-Seeking/Recognition.

**Impaired Limits** — the sense of difficulty in setting limits, in protecting oneself, and in tolerating a range of emotions and impulses. The schemas: Entitlement/Grandiosity; Self-Control/Discipline.

**Other-Directedness** — the sense of surrendering control of one's life to others in order to avoid conflict, blame, or abandonment. The schemas: Subjugation/Self-Sacrifice; Self-Approval/Recognition.

**Overvigilance and Inhibition** — the sense of being unable to relax, to express spontaneous emotion, or to meet the world with spontaneous interest. The schemas: Emotional Deprivation; Pessimism/Negativity; Unrelenting Standards; Approval-Seeking/Recognition.

The full list of eighteen schemas with their descriptions and their typical childhood antecedents is reproduced in Appendix C.

### 2.3 The developmental antecedents

Each schema is associated with a set of **typical childhood antecedents** — the environmental conditions in which the schema is likely to have formed, and the unmet emotional needs that it reflects. The schema of Abandonment/Instability, for example, has antecedents including death or loss of a parent, chronic illness in a parent, parental unavailability, and a parent who was inconsistent, and it reflects the unmet need for a secure base and for stability.

The developmental antecedents are the most clinically important part of the schema construct for work with young people, because they tell the practitioner what to look for in the developmental history and they indicate what the young person's unmet developmental need is. A young person with a schema of Defectiveness/Shame and a schema of Unrelenting Standards has a developmental history of criticism and conditional approval, and the unmet need is unconditional acceptance. This is a precise and usable formulation, and it maps directly onto the developmental material in Chapter 9.

## 3. Coping Styles

### 3.1 The three styles

A young person confronted with a schema responds in one of three **coping styles**, and the style is the mechanism by which the schema is maintained.

**Surrender.** The young person allows the schema to be enacted, and behaves in ways that are consistent with it and that confirm it. A young person with a schema of Abandonment who is in a relationship with an unreliable partner will tolerate the unreliability, will not protest, will minimise the harm, and will — when abandoned — experience it as a confirmation rather than as a surprise. Surrender is the mode of a young person who has learned that protesting does not work.

**Avoidance.** The young person avoids situations in which the schema is likely to be activated. A young person with a schema of Defectiveness/Shame will avoid the swimming lesson, the oral presentation, the birthday party, or any situation in which they might be exposed. Avoidance is the mode of a young person who has learned that being seen is dangerous.

**Overcompensation.** The young person does the opposite of the schema, in an exaggerated and rigid way, in order to prevent it from being activated. A young person with a schema of Rejection will become hyper-competent, hyper-controlling, and compulsively successful, and will experience the vulnerability of the schema as intolerable, and the overcompensation will bring its own costs, including the failure to form relationships in which the vulnerability could be tolerated.

### 3.2 The maintenance cycle

The three styles maintain each other in what the theory calls the **schema coping cycle**, and a practitioner should be able to describe it.

The schema is activated. The young person responds with the characteristic style. The style produces a short-term benefit — the anxiety is reduced, the rejection is avoided, the vulnerability is not felt, or the achievement is achieved. The style then produces consequences in the longer term: the interpersonal patterns that confirm the schema, the narrowing of the young person's world, the depletion of their resources, and the reinforcement of the belief that the schema is true.

The clinical implication is that the coping style is where the work is done, and that the schema itself is not the target of intervention in the early phase. A practitioner who attacks the schema directly will be resisted, because the coping style is what is protecting the young person from the schema.

## 4. Modes

### 4.1 The mode model

**Modes** are the distinct psychological states in which a person finds themselves, each with its own characteristic feelings, thoughts, and behaviours, and each organised around a particular need or aim. The mode model is a more immediately usable clinical tool than the schema construct, and it is the level at which most therapists actually work.

**Child modes** are the young, vulnerable, feeling states that are frozen in the child's experience of the original event.

**Vulnerable child modes** are the modes in which the young person feels small, sad, scared, ashamed, or helpless — the emotional core of the schemas, and the mode in which the young person's unmet needs are felt.

**Angry child modes** are the modes in which the young person feels anger, resentment, or defiance, usually in response to a perceived violation of their needs. The angry modes are the emotional complement of the vulnerable modes and are frequently the mode in which a young person presents to services.

**Punitive parent modes** are the internalised representations of the critical, blaming, and demanding authority that the young person has absorbed, and they are the "should" voice of the young person's own inner world. The punitive parent modes are the source of the "I must be perfect" experience and of the harsh self-criticism that is so common in young people with high achievement and with self-harm.

**Demanding parent modes** are the internalised standards that drive the young person to perform, to achieve, and to never be satisfied. Their function is to protect against vulnerability, and their cost is the relentless standards schema and the burnout that follows.

**The healthy adult mode** is the mode from which the therapy is conducted, and it is the mode in which the young person can reflect, plan, and act. The therapeutic task is to strengthen it.

### 4.2 The healthy adult and the child's needs

The theory's account of the therapeutic task is that the healthy adult mode is developed in the context of a relationship with a therapist who provides, in a limited and carefully bounded way, the **reparenting** that the young person did not receive in childhood. The therapist supplies the experience of being met with empathy, with unconditional regard, and with the experience of a limit that is set kindly and consistently, and the young person internalises the experience of the therapist's mode.

This is one of the most contested claims in the theory, and the student should know the history: it derives from the object relations tradition, and the developmental research on attachment and on the internal working models provides a substantial empirical rationale for the claim that early relationships are formative. What the theory adds is the clinical specification of how the formative experience is provided in a bounded and ethically appropriate way, and the limits on the role are set out in Section 10.

## 5. Formulation: The Schema and Mode Map

The schema and mode map is the formulation instrument, and it is one of the more useful in the field.

**The schema map** specifies: the young person's early maladaptive schemas in order of priority; the coping style in relation to each; the specific childhood antecedents; the unmet emotional needs; the typical triggers in the present; the characteristic thoughts, feelings, and behaviours; and the short-term and long-term consequences.

**The mode map** specifies: the child modes and the dysfunctional coping modes in relation to each; the typical triggers for each; the mode in which the young person presents; the mode in which the young person is most vulnerable; the internal dialogue between the modes; and the conditions under which each mode is entered.

The maps are used with the young person, and the practice of drawing them is itself an intervention: a young person who has been in a difficult pattern for years frequently has no idea that they are moving between states, and seeing the map is often the moment at which the pattern becomes tractable.

## 6. The Therapeutic Techniques

### 6.1 Limited reparenting and the empathic confrontation

**Limited reparenting** is the therapeutic relationship itself, and the specification is important: the therapist provides warmth, empathy, understanding, and validation, and the *unconditional positive regard* is the core, but it is provided within clear and consistent limits. The therapist does not permit the young person to abuse, manipulate, or overwhelm; the therapist does not do the young person's homework; the therapist does not function as a parent substitute beyond the boundaries of the relationship. The theory calls this "the perfect parent" model, and the ethical specification of it is set out in Section 10.

**Empathic confrontation** is the technique of bringing the young person's own conflicting positions into direct contact, in a supportive and non-blaming way. "You've said you want to be a doctor, and you've also said you want to be free to choose, and I don't think both of those can be true at the same time, and I wonder which one you want more." The confrontation is empathic in that it names the conflict without judging either side of it, and it is the technique that distinguishes schema therapy from the cognitive therapies in their handling of the deeper material.

### 6.2 Imagery rescripting

**Imagery rescripting** is the technique in which the young person is asked to imagine a scene from the original childhood experience, to identify the mode in which they are then, and to imagine a version of the scene in which the unmet need is met — by the child version of themselves, or by a "good enough" caregiver figure who supplies what was not supplied.

Imagery rescripting is one of the most used techniques in the whole field, and it is one of the most useful with young people, because the child version of the young person is a powerful and accessible image, and because the young person can be helped to see the caregiving they needed and did not receive, which is a developmental fact rather than a cognitive exercise.

The cautions are specific and important: the young person must be in a sufficiently stable state, the imagery must be approached at a pace the young person can tolerate, the work must be done with the mode explicitly identified, and the technique should not be used with a young person in acute crisis.

### 6.3 Chair work and dialogue work

**Chair work** places the young person in a dialogue with a part of themselves, usually one of the internal modes, seated in an empty chair. The young person speaks from one mode and then from the other, and the aim is to enable a resolution — typically, the young person learning to take the part's perspective and to recognise its need, and the mode then losing some of its grip.

**Dialogue work** is the more structured, more cognitively oriented version, in which the two "voices" are explicitly written out, examined for their evidence, and then restated in a more balanced form. It is more behavioural and more concrete than the chair work, and it is more appropriate with a young person who cannot sustain the affective intensity of the chair work.

### 6.4 The behavioural and cognitive elements

Schema therapy also includes the standard cognitive and behavioural techniques — the thought record, the behavioural experiment, the exposure, the activation of the healthy adult — and their use is differentiated by the mode in which the young person is. The clinical skill is to choose the technique that matches the mode: behavioural activation and problem-solving in the healthy adult mode; the empathic confrontation in the vulnerable child mode; the dialogue work with a demanding parent mode.
