
## 5. The Practice of Parent–Adolescent Work

### 5.1 The first session: the parents alone

The first session is with the parents, and it is the session in which the work is determined, and it has a specific structure.

**The referral and the actual request.** The practitioner asks what the parents hope will be different, and then asks the question that reframes the session: "And what do you want for [the young person's name] in three years' time? Not what you want them to stop doing — what you want their life to look like." The two questions usually produce different answers, and the second is the one the work is built on.

**The developmental timeline.** The practitioner asks the parents to describe their child's development, from the earliest years they can remember, and the narrative does a great deal of the work. Parents who describe a child who was always difficult are describing a child they have never had a relationship with; parents who describe a child who was easy until a particular point are identifying the point at which the difficulty began, and the point is often the point at which the developmental task changed and the parental structure did not.

**The pattern.** The practitioner asks about a specific recent instance, and gets the sequence, using the material of Chapter 32. The sequence is the most useful single artefact of the assessment, because it is specific, and because it identifies the change points.

**The escalation.** The practitioner asks the parents to describe what they do when the young person refuses. The answer is the escalation, and most parents have not heard themselves describe it.

**The parents' own lives.** The practitioner asks what has been happening in the parents' own lives over the last year. A new job, an illness, a bereavement, a debt, a pregnancy, a separation, a new baby, a move, a caring responsibility for an older relative. The list of things that can precipitate a family crisis in this period is short, and the practitioner should have it in mind, and the answer is frequently that something has happened.

### 5.2 The five components of a parent-focused intervention

**Component one: the psychoeducation.** The parents are told what the developmental task is and what the developmental timetable looks like, and the specific points about the decline in disclosure, the arguing, and the detachment from parental control are explained. The explanation is not to make the parents feel guilty; it is to remove the interpretation that the young person's behaviour is a rejection of them, which is the interpretation that generates the escalation.

**Component two: the reconstruction of the sequence.** The parents and the practitioner map the escalation cycle together, on paper, in the session. The map is the therapeutic instrument, because the parents can see the cycle only once it is drawn. The practitioner should expect the moment of recognition, and it is often the most important moment in the whole course of work, and it is also the moment at which a parent becomes hopeful, because the cycle implies that it can be interrupted.

**Component three: the skills.** The parent training content is taught: positive reinforcement, attention to the desired behaviour, planned ignoring, time-out from reinforcement, consistency, and the "catch them being good" practice. The skills are taught by rehearsal, not by discussion, and the rehearsal is the reason parent training works better than information about parenting.

**Component four: the renegotiated structure.** The specific changes to the rules: the curfews, the phone, the privacy, the contribution to the household, the disclosure, the consequences. The changes should be written down, agreed, and dated, and they should include an explicit statement of what the young person gets as well as what is required. A set of rules that only restricts is a set of rules that will be breached.

**Component five: the relationship work.** The most important and the most neglected component. The parents and the young person need a relationship that is not the one in which the conflict occurs, and the work is on the *quality of the connection* rather than on the *management of the behaviour*: the specific daily contact, the specific appreciation, the specific time, the specific activity. A father who has a Saturday morning hour with his fifteen-year-old son and does something the son chooses has built something that the token of a good week of compliance cannot buy.

### 5.3 Sequencing: the parents first

The sequencing follows from the developmental reasoning and should be defended to the referrer if the referral expects to see the young person in the first week.

**Weeks 1–4: the parents.** The psychoeducation, the sequence, the skills, the renegotiated structure. The young person is seen briefly and individually in parallel, for the alliance and for the developmental work.

**Weeks 5–8: the parents and the young person together.** The renegotiated rules are made explicit, the young person is given a real role in the negotiation, and the practical arrangements are settled.

**Weeks 9 onward: the work with the young person**, which can now proceed because the environment has changed.

The strongest practical argument for this sequence is empirical: a young person who is asked to change while the adults who control the environment are not changing will conclude that the adults are not serious, and the conclusion will be correct, and the young person's cooperation will be withdrawn. A young person who is asked to change after the adults have changed will conclude that the change is real, and the conclusion will also be correct, and the young person's cooperation will be available.

### 5.4 When the parents will not work

Sometimes the parents will not or cannot do the work, and the practitioner needs a plan.

**The parents are the problem.** In a minority of cases the young person is the least disturbed member of the family, and the work is with the parents' own difficulties. The practitioner should be able to identify this situation, and the referral should be redirected rather than the work being forced.

**The parents cannot agree.** A common situation is that the two parents want different things. The work is then directed at the *disagreement*, and a common and effective intervention is to help the parents reach an agreement about the rules as a preliminary to anything else. Parents who cannot agree on a curfew cannot implement a curfew.

**The parents are overwhelmed.** A family with three jobs, no transport, and a young person with additional needs cannot undertake a skills programme. The response is to reduce the intervention to its minimum viable form: one specific rule, changed, consistently applied.

**The young person refuses to participate.** The work continues with the parents, and the young person's refusal is treated as information, and the practitioner maintains a separate channel with the young person.

### 5.5 Consultation with the school

Almost every case of adolescent conflict involves the school, and the school is usually where the young person's difficulties are most visible and where the parents' changes are least likely to be supported. The consultation work has four elements.

**The school's account of the behaviour.** The behaviour in the corridor, in the classroom, and in the dining hall may look entirely different from the behaviour at home, and the school is a different system with different contingencies.

**The alignment of the responses.** If the parents have made a new rule and the school has a different one, the young person will be confused and will be given the opportunity to play the two against each other. The alignment should be explicit and should be written down and shared.

**The information flow.** The school is a source of information about the young person, and the practitioner must decide what is shared with the school and what is not, and the young person must know, and the consent requirement is absolute. In most cases, the young person's grades, the school reports, and the practitioner's observation are shared with the parents and not with the school, unless the young person agrees.

**The teacher's needs.** The teacher is dealing with the behaviour in the classroom, and the teacher needs something concrete. A specific plan for the classroom — a specified response to the disruption, a specified alternative the young person can use, a specified signal for help — is worth more than a general reassurance.

### 5.6 Working with the young person directly

The work with the young person is not only a phase that comes later. Several interventions can be delivered directly.

**The developmental work.** The practitioner works with the young person on the specific tasks of the period: the separation from the parental system, the development of an individualised value system, the development of the peer world, the toleration of the inevitable arguments. The work is developmental and it is not reducible to a coping skill.

**The relationship with the young person as a buffer.** Even where the parents cannot change, the practitioner can be the adult in the young person's life who is reliable, non-intrusive, and available, and the young person's experience of being able to trust an adult is itself a protective factor and a developmental achievement.

**The work on the specific difficulty.** Where the young person's difficulty is a symptom — the anxiety, the depression, the eating, the substance use — the specific treatment is delivered in parallel, and Chapter 38 and Chapter 47 address the complex cases.

**The mediation.** Where the parents cannot work together, the practitioner can work with the young person and one parent on the relationship, and the work can produce a change in the dyad that the family system then accommodates.

## 6. Case Illustration: The Young Man Who Had Been Given Ultimatums

**Presentation.** Samuel is fifteen and lives with his mother and his younger sister. He is referred by the school, which is concerned about his behaviour in lessons. His mother attends the first appointment and is, in the practitioner's word, "furious and frightened in about equal measure". The referral describes a young man who is argumentative, who does not do his homework, who is "rude" to his mother, and who has recently been excluded twice.

**The first session with the mother.** The practitioner begins with the question that reframes the session: "What do you want for Samuel in three years' time?" The mother says, without hesitation: "I want him to go to university. I want him to have what I didn't have." The practitioner asks what she wants him to stop doing, and the mother says: "The arguing. The rudeness. The not doing his work."

The two answers are in different places and the difference is the whole case. The mother's stated goal is a future, and it is a future she has a strong investment in, and it is a future in which Samuel is the vehicle for the repair of her own history. The referral question is about the present, and it is about compliance.

**The sequence.** The practitioner asks the mother to describe a recent argument in detail. What emerges is a highly patterned exchange. Samuel comes in at nine; his mother asks him about his day; he says "fine"; she asks again; he says "I said fine"; she says "don't talk to me like that"; he says "what do you want me to say, mum"; she says "the truth"; he says "there's nothing to say"; she raises her voice and says "don't give me that attitude, I've had enough, you're going to sit at that table until you've done your work"; he goes up to his room; she calls up after five minutes; he does not answer; she says "fine, be like that"; and the evening ends with both of them certain that the other is the problem.

The practitioner maps it on paper, with the mother, and the mother's face changes when she sees it. The sequence is recognisable: the repeated question after "fine", the "don't talk to me like that", the demand that he produce an emotion he does not have, the ultimatum about the table, the five-minute silence, and the withdrawal. And then the practitioner asks the critical question: "What happens if he does sit at the table?" The mother says: "He doesn't. He just talks." The practitioner asks: "So what does he get by not sitting at the table?" The mother thinks and then says, with some surprise: "He gets to be upstairs."

**The formulation.** Samuel's refusal is maintained by escape. He is not in conflict with his mother; he is a young man who has discovered that a specific sequence of exchange delivers him to his room, and the room is where he wants to be, and it is the only thing in his day that is reliably his. The mother's ultimatums are the problem, and they are also the solution — they are the family's only route to the room, and they have taught Samuel that the negotiation is the way to get there.

**The intervention with the mother.** Four elements.

*Element one: the developmental frame.* The practitioner explains the mid-adolescent phase: the arguing, the testing, the increasing need for an individualised value system, the decline in disclosure, the detachment from parental control. The explanation is delivered with the developmental literature, and it is delivered as information rather than as correction, and the practitioner is careful to say that the arguing is necessary and is not going to stop.

*Element two: the restriction.* The practitioner gives the mother a specific instruction: "For the next two weeks, you are not going to ask him a second time about anything. If he says 'fine', that is the end of the conversation about his day. If he says nothing, that is also the end. The only exception is the question about the work, and the work question is going to be a different conversation, and I will show you how."

The reasoning is given explicitly: the repeated question is the hook, and the young man is not resisting the question, he is resisting the repetition, and the repetition is what gives him the opening.

*Element three: the work question.* The practitioner spends twenty minutes with the mother on how to ask about the homework, and the difference is technical but decisive. "How's the homework?" produces "fine" and then a confrontation. "What did you get through on the maths?" produces a description. The second question cannot be answered with "fine" and it does not require the young person to produce a feeling, and it opens a conversation about a specific thing.

*Element four: the negotiation with Samuel.* The practitioner meets Samuel alone and explains what is happening, in terms that do not disclose his mother's account of the sequence: "Your mum and I have been looking at what happens when she asks you about your day. You've probably noticed that when she asks twice, things get worse. She's going to try asking once and then leaving it. I wanted you to know it's happening so it doesn't feel like a trick." Samuel considers this and says: "She'll forget." The practitioner says: "Probably. And then you can tell her, and we'll do it again."

**The outcome.** The change is visible in three weeks. The arguments stop; what replaces them is silence, and the practitioner explains to the mother that silence is a good outcome here, because the silence is the absence of a fight rather than the presence of a wall. Samuel comes down of his own accord more often, and the work gets done more often, and the two of them have a relationship that is not warm but is not combative. The school reports an improvement in the lessons, though less dramatic than the mother hoped, and the practitioner is careful to say why: the school is a different system and the changes have to be made there too, and the consultation with the form tutor is what produces them.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the whole of the chapter. The referral was about compliance. The problem was the escape contingency that the mother's ultimatums had created. The work was with the mother, because the mother was the tractable target, and because the developmental argument was the thing that changed her behaviour. And the practitioner did the thing that is hardest in this work, which was to tell the mother that the arguing would not stop, and that the goal was a different relationship and not the elimination of the disagreement.

## 7. Evidence and Research Support

### 7.1 The status of the evidence

The evidence for parent-focused work with adolescents is the strongest in the field, and a student should be able to summarise it precisely.

**Parent training has a robust effect on oppositional behaviour and conduct disorder**, and the Cochrane reviews report substantial effects, maintained at follow-up, and the effects are larger for younger children than for adolescents.

**Parent training improves the parent's own symptoms of distress**, and the parental depression and the parental stress are among the better-established effects, which is one of the strongest arguments for the approach, because the parent's distress is what maintains the escalation.

**Parent training is effective in school-based formats**, and the evidence for the school-based delivery of parent training is good, and the school-based formats are more accessible to families who cannot attend clinic appointments, and the evidence for the telephone and the online formats has grown.

**Behavioural family therapy, which combines parent training with the teaching of problem-solving and communication skills, has a strong evidence base**, and the Cochrane reviews of behavioural family therapy for conduct problems report large effects.

**Family-based treatment of adolescent substance use has good support**, and the family-based approaches consistently outperform the individual approaches in this population.

**Parent-focused interventions improve the young person's functioning as well as the behaviour**, and this is one of the more important findings, because the changes in family functioning are what make the behaviour change durable.

**The effects are smaller for adolescents than for younger children**, and the pattern of the research is that the more entrenched the pattern and the older the young person, the smaller the effect.

### 7.2 The limitations of the evidence

**The studies are largely North American and European**, and the cultural specificity of the parenting constructs in them is significant.

**The samples are selected**, and the families who complete parent training are more engaged and less disadvantaged than the families who are referred.

**The measures are largely behavioural and parental-report**, and the young person's own account of the change is less often measured.

**The long-term follow-up is limited**, and the question of whether the effects are maintained into the adult years is largely unanswered.

**The studies rarely examine the mechanism**, and it remains difficult to specify which components of the parent training package are responsible for the effect.

## 8. Strengths and Limitations of Parent-Focused Work

### 8.1 Strengths

**It works.** This is the strongest empirical claim that can be made for any intervention with adolescents and their families, and it is unusual in this field.

**It addresses the adults who control the environment.** A young person cannot change a household; a parent can.

**It is deliverable in schools and in primary care**, and it is deliverable by non-specialists with supervision, and the evidence for the supervised delivery is good.

**It is short.** Parent training is typically between eight and sixteen sessions, and brevity is a clinical advantage with a population that does not stay.

**It improves the parents**, and the parents' improvement is a durable benefit even if the young person's symptoms recur.

**It does not require the young person to be motivated.** This is a significant advantage: a young person who is not engaged can still benefit from a change in the environment.

### 8.2 Limitations

**It can be used in ways that are not therapeutic.** The techniques of parent training can be used to produce compliance in a young person whose problem is not compliance, and the field has a genuine responsibility here.

**It can locate the problem in the parent when the problem is elsewhere**, and this is the principal risk of the approach.

**It says little about the young person's own experience**, and a practitioner working exclusively in the parent frame will produce a family that functions and a young person who is not seen.

**The effects on adolescents are smaller than on younger children**, and the hardest cases are the ones that most need it.

**It assumes a stable, cooperative, two-parent family**, and a substantial proportion of the families in contemporary services are neither stable nor cooperative nor two-parent.

## 9. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 Whose problem is being treated?

This is the central ethical question of the chapter, and it should be asked in every case.

When a young person is referred for conflict with their parents, the referral frames the young person as the problem. The ethical work is to establish whether the young person is the problem, the parents are, the family system is, or the circumstances are. Four different interventions follow, and a practitioner who does not make the distinction will deliver the wrong one.

**The young person is the problem.** The conflict is a symptom of the young person's difficulties, and the parents need support in managing a difficult developmental period, and the work with the young person is the primary work.

**The parents are the problem.** The young person is the least disturbed member of the family, and the work is with the parents, and the young person's needs are secondary to the parents' in this particular case.

**The family system is the problem.** The conflict is the family's; the young person is the site.

**The circumstances are the problem.** The conflict is a rational response to conditions — poverty, a disability, a housing crisis, a shift pattern, a family member's illness, a school that is unsafe. A parent who is working two jobs and is raising a disabled child alone and is being told by the school that her son is "difficult" is not a parent with a parenting problem.

A practitioner who does not ask the fourth question will systematically misdiagnose the families who most need help.

### 9.2 Cultural considerations

**The parenting norms of the practitioner are not the parenting norms of the family.** The practices that the parent training literature treats as effective — the daily attention, the specific praise, the consistent response, the clear rules, the negotiated decision-making — are specific to a particular cultural and historical context, and many families use other systems that work.

**Some of the most "effective" practices are the most damaging across cultures.** A parent who has been taught to withdraw attention as a response to a child's bid may, in a context where the child's bid is a call for connection in a context of scarcity, be withdrawing the very thing the child needs. The practitioner must know the family's context before implementing the ignoring techniques.

**Authoritative versus authoritarian.** The parenting literature's preferred style is authoritative — high in warmth and high in structure — and the style is a culturally specific form, and a family that is authoritative in its own terms may be described by an observer in the field's terms as authoritarian.

**The father's role.** The evidence base and the practice of parent training have been developed disproportionately with mothers, and the involvement of fathers is consistently lower than it should be, and a programme that does not include the father in a family where the father is present and available reproduces a structural problem.

**Language and the training materials.** The parent training materials are written in a particular register and a particular idiom, and a family whose first language is different will need the concepts translated and the materials adapted, and a practitioner who hands over a translated leaflet has not adapted the intervention.

### 9.3 Ethical requirements

**Informed consent from the parents and, where the young person is engaged, their informed participation.** A parent who consents to a skills programme under the impression that it will make their child comply has been misled.

**The young person's right to be heard.** Even where the work is with the parents, the young person's account must be sought, and it must be sought by someone the young person trusts.

**Confidentiality.** The boundaries between the individual work with the young person and the family work must be explicit and must be stated to everyone, and the practitioner must be able to state what will be shared with the parents and what will not.

**The prohibition on the use of the work to control.** A practitioner should decline a referral whose stated purpose is to obtain compliance, and should say why, and should offer the alternative.

**Proportionality.** The intervention should be proportionate to the difficulty, and the practitioner should resist the temptation to impose a full programme on a family with a small and specific difficulty.

**Equity of access.** Parent training delivered in a clinic to families who can attend is a service that will systematically under-reach. The school-based, the telephone, and the online formats exist because of this, and a practitioner in a service with a poor attendance record should be actively looking for them.

## 10. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Parent–adolescent conflict** — The friction between the parents and the adolescent in the second decade, which is a normal feature of the developmental renegotiation and becomes a clinical problem when it is escalating and the relationship is deteriorating.
- **The developmental task of the second decade** — The renegotiation of the relationship with the parents through which the young person becomes a person rather than a child.
- **The four parent accountabilities** — To provide a secure base that can be relinquished, to maintain the authority the adolescent needs, to provide a structure that is firm and flexible, and to maintain a relationship with the young person that is not the one they had as a child.
- **The parental task of mourning the child** — The work of relating to the young person the adolescent has become, which is routinely skipped and is experienced by the young person as rejection.
- **Escalation cycle** — The patterned sequence in which the parent's escalation produces the child's escalation and the parent's backing down then reinforces the child's.
- **The three features of the escalation cycle** — The escalation is a communication, the parent's escalation triggers the child's, and the parent's backing down is the reinforcement.
- **Parent training** — A structured programme of behavioural and communication skills for the parents, delivered in groups or individually, that has the strongest evidence base of any intervention with families.
- **Positive reinforcement and attention** — The parent skills of attending promptly and specifically to the desired behaviour, with a description of what the child did.
- **Planned ignoring** — The complete and temporary withdrawal of attention from a behaviour, taught with the expectation of an initial increase.
- **Time-out from reinforcement** — A specified removal of access to reinforcement contingent on a behaviour, taught with the rules that make it safe and effective.
- **Consistency between the adults** — The agreement of all caregivers who have significant contact with the young person, which is the most commonly neglected and most necessary element of the work.
- **The "catch them being good" practice** — The systematic noticing, describing, and responding to instances of the desired behaviour, with a record.
- **Mainstream communication pattern** — The characteristics of good parent–adolescent communication, and the profile of the families with better outcomes.
- **Conflict communication pattern** — The characteristics of poor parent–adolescent communication, and the profile of the families with worse outcomes.
- **The four phases of disclosure** — High control and high disclosure in early childhood, decreasing parental control and declining disclosure in early adolescence, peer orientation and the development of an individualised value system in middle adolescence, and the re-establishment of a more equal relationship in later adolescence.
- **Detachment from parental control** — The early adolescent developmental task, and the reason the decline in disclosure is normal rather than a symptom.
- **The individualised value system** — The middle adolescent developmental task, and the reason the arguments are part of the work rather than evidence of its failure.
- **The three common conflicts** — The autonomy conflict, the sexuality conflict, and the achievement conflict, each with a different clinical character.
- **The achievement conflict** — The conflict over the young person's school performance, which is frequently a conflict with the parent's projected self.
- **Reformulation of the request** — The clinical move of distinguishing the parents' request for the removal of a behaviour from their wish for the young person's life.
- **The reconstruction of the sequence** — The mapping of the escalation cycle on paper with the parents, which is the therapeutic instrument of the parent-focused work.
- **The renegotiated structure** — The specific, written, dated set of changes to the household rules, including what the young person receives as well as what is required.
- **The relationship work** — The component directed at the quality of the connection between the young person and the parents, as distinct from the management of the behaviour.
- **Sequencing the parents first** — The clinical sequence in which the parents' work precedes the family's joint work, and the empirical basis for it.
- **The minimum viable intervention** — The reduced form of parent work, consisting of one specific rule changed and applied consistently, for families who cannot undertake a full programme.
- **The parent's disconfirmation** — The repeated question, the demand for a feeling, and the ultimatum, and their role in producing the confrontation.
- **The "what did you get through on the maths" question** — A specific formulation that cannot be answered with "fine" and that opens a conversation about a particular thing.
- **Consultation alignment** — The requirement that the rules and responses in the school match those in the home, and the written record of the alignment.
- **The school's own contingencies** — The different reinforcement structure of the school setting, which requires its own functional analysis and its own plan.
- **Whose problem is being treated** — The ethical question of whether the young person, the parents, the family system, or the circumstances constitute the problem, and the four different interventions that follow.
- **The circumstances as the problem** — The category in which a young person's behaviour is a rational response to material conditions, and the failure to consider it that misdiagnoses the families most in need.
:::

## 11. Chapter Summary

This chapter brought the three preceding theories together around the central clinical situation of this part of the book, and its governing argument is that when a young person and their parents are in conflict, the parents are the more tractable target. The argument is developmental rather than moral: the adolescent's task is a renegotiation of the relationship with the parents, the young person cannot unilaterally leave the child subsystem, and the adults hold the door.

The developmental rationale was set out with the four parent accountabilities, and the clinical presentation was described as a structural mismatch — the parents operating a pre-adolescent structure over an adolescent child — with a simple clinical test: compare the demands with the developmental capacity of the specific young person. The three-part task of the practitioner was described, and the most important element was the clarification of what the parents actually want, which is almost never the removal of the behaviour.

The behavioural analysis of the escalation cycle was described in detail, with its three features, and the parent training evidence was summarised, including the breadth of the effects and the finding that the sessions with the parents are the sessions. The core skills were described, and a substantial caution was given: the model treats the parent's attention as a technical resource, and a practitioner who works exclusively in that frame will produce a compliant young person and a distressed relationship. The resolution proposed was that parent training works when it is used to increase the quality of the relationship rather than the compliance of the child, and that the techniques are the same.

The communication material was described with the mainstream and conflict patterns as an assessment instrument usable in a single family session, and the four phases of disclosure with the two clinical conclusions that follow: the decline in disclosure in early adolescence is normal and is not a symptom to be treated, and the arguments of the middle adolescent years are part of the developmental work rather than its failure. The three common conflicts were described, and the achievement conflict was identified as the one in which the family therapy work is most clearly indicated.

The practice section gave the structure of the first session with the parents, the five components of a parent-focused intervention, the case for sequencing the parents first with its empirical basis, the four reasons the parents might not be able to do the work, the four elements of school consultation, and the interventions that can be delivered directly to the young person.

The case of Samuel demonstrated the chapter. The referral was about compliance; the sequence revealed an escape-maintained refusal produced by the mother's own repeated questions; the restriction broke the cycle; the work question was re-specified so that it could not be answered with "fine"; and the practitioner told the mother explicitly that the arguing would not stop and that the goal was a different relationship rather than the elimination of the disagreement.

The evidence was summarised with its limits, and the strengths and limitations were set out with the principal risk identified: that the approach will locate the problem in the parent when the problem is elsewhere. The ethical section took up the question of whose problem is being treated, with four categories and the corresponding interventions, and made the point that a practitioner who does not ask the fourth question will systematically misdiagnose the families who most need help. The cultural analysis addressed the specificity of the parenting constructs, the possibility that the "most effective" practices are the most damaging across cultures, the authoritative and authoritarian distinction, the under-involvement of fathers, and the adaptation of the materials. The ethical requirements closed the chapter with the prohibition on the use of the work to obtain compliance and the requirement of equity of access.

## 13. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 33.1 — Mapping the Escalation Cycle (75 minutes, field-based)

Take a real case from placement, or a relationship you are closely involved in, and map the escalation cycle on paper, in the specific terms in which it occurs, with the actual words that are used. Then mark the point at which the cycle would break if one person did something different, and write what that something different would be. Then write the sentence that would introduce that change to the family without it sounding like an accusation. The last step is the one that matters, and most students discover that the change is easy to specify and hard to introduce.

### Activity 33.2 — The Mainstream and Conflict Audit (60 minutes, individual)

Using the mainstream and conflict pattern lists from Section 3.1, rate a family you know on each of the twenty characteristics. Then write the three characteristics on which the family most clearly deviates from the mainstream, and for each, write the specific practice that would move it. The point of the exercise is the discipline of moving from a general impression to a specific behaviour, and of resisting the temptation to work on the twenty characteristics rather than on the three.

### Activity 33.3 — The Three-Year Question (60 minutes, triadic)

One student plays a parent who has referred their fifteen-year-old for "rude and lazy"; one plays the counsellor; one observes. The counsellor's task is to ask the question "What do you want for your daughter in three years' time?" and to follow it wherever the parent goes, without rescuing the parent from their own answer. The observer's task is to note the moment at which the parent's account of the problem changes, and to write down the exact wording at that moment. Almost every group finds that the parent's answer to the three-year question is not about compliance at all.

### Activity 33.4 — The Consultation Map (60 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, take a school-based case and draw a map of the behaviours at home and at school, with the specific antecedents and the specific consequences in each setting. Then identify the three places where the settings are giving contradictory messages to the young person, and write the specific conversation needed with the school. Then identify what the practitioner must ask the young person before the school conversation happens. The third step is the ethical step, and the groups consistently discover that the third step has been omitted in most of the cases they have known.

## 14. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the developmental rationale.** Read Steinberg (2014) for the authoritative parenting account, Sroufe (1995) for the attachment foundation, and Smetana (1995) for the disclosure research. The disclosure research is the most clinically useful of the three and the most frequently unknown.

**For the intervention.** Read the Oregon model manual and Dishion and McDonough for the intervention science, and read a parent training curriculum in full. The curriculum is more useful than the research literature for practice.

**For the consultation.** Read the school-based family intervention literature, and read the consultation literature on aligning school and home. The alignment problem is the one that most often defeats an otherwise sound plan.

**For the ethics and culture.** Read the evidence on cross-cultural variation in parenting, read the literature on the use of parent training with families who do not share the practitioner's parenting norms, and read the ethical guidance on the use of behavioural techniques with families.

**For the harder cases.** Read the family-based treatment literature for adolescent substance use and for eating disorders, and read the multisystemic therapy literature. These are the cases in which the parent-focused work needs to be embedded in a larger system, and they are the ones that most often present.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is developmental and systemic consultation with families in conflict. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is family patterns, attachment, boundaries, communication, stories, and relational resources. The mechanism to keep in view is how interaction sequences and shared meanings distribute distress, protection, responsibility, and possibility across a system. The characteristic professional risk is blaming families, exposing a young person in unsafe joint sessions, or ignoring material conditions and individual needs. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in family meetings, school consultations, care networks, and individual sessions informed by systems thinking, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Leah is fifteen and is seen in a family support service for repeated classroom escalation. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Leah's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Leah explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on family patterns, attachment, boundaries, communication, stories, and relational resources.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Leah chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Leah has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Leah can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
