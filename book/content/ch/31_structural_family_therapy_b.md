
## 6. Structural Family Therapy with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The developmental fit

The structural model is unusually well matched to the developmental stage at which adolescents present, for four reasons that a student should be able to state.

**Adolescence is a structural task.** The adolescent's work is a reorganisation: the movement from the child subsystem to a position of increasing autonomy, the renegotiation of the parent-child boundary, the acquisition of a peer world that the parents do not control, and the eventual establishment of a life that is theirs. These are exactly the structural variables the model names.

**The mismatch is the pathology.** A family that maintains a pre-adolescent structure over an adolescent child produces a symptom. The symptom is the report of the mismatch, and it is a report in the language of behaviour because the young person does not have another channel.

**The parentification problem is developmentally salient.** A young person who is managing a parent's emotional life is being asked to do a developmental task in reverse, and the structural model has the clearest available language for it.

**The intervention is structural.** The restructuring of the boundary and of the hierarchy is precisely the intervention that the developmental task requires, and the model provides the technical means to do it.

### 6.2 The four structural tasks of adolescence

The structural-developmental formulation identifies four developmental tasks, and the assessment of an adolescent is organised around them. A student should be able to use them as a checklist and, more importantly, should be able to recognise a task that has been left incomplete.

**The first task: to differentiate from the family of origin while maintaining a relationship with it.** The failure of this task presents as either fusion — the young person cannot be apart — or cut-off — the young person cannot be together. A family that has not made room for the differentiation will find that the adolescent leaves entirely, or that the adolescent stays and cannot develop.

**The second task: to move from the child subsystem to a more adult position in the family.** This involves the renegotiation of the boundary, the assumption of adult responsibilities, and the participation in adult decision-making. The failure of this task presents as a young person who is treated as a child in the family's structure and who responds with the only available means.

**The third task: to develop peer relationships of increasing depth and mutuality.** The family structure must permit the peer world, and the parents' difficulty in permitting it is usually a difficulty with their own fear.

**The fourth task: to develop a vocational and financial direction that enables leaving home.** This is a task that is often overlooked in clinical work with adolescents and it is a common source of family conflict that is misread as a behavioural problem.

### 6.3 Boundary problems in clinical practice

Four boundary presentations recur, and each requires a different intervention.

**The diffuse boundary.** The parent and the adolescent are so close that the parent's feelings are the adolescent's feelings and the adolescent's are the parent's. The clinical presentations are: the adolescent who cannot make a decision without checking with the parent; the parent who cannot tolerate the adolescent's distress and must resolve it immediately; and the adolescent who reports a sense of being responsible for the parent's emotional state. The intervention is the establishment of a clear boundary, which is painful and which is usually resisted by the parent, because the parent experiences the boundary as a loss.

**The rigid boundary.** The parent and the adolescent are separated, and the separation is usually the residue of something: an earlier closeness that ended in conflict, a loss, a previous family breakdown, a history of the parent being hurt by the same behaviour. The clinical presentations are: the adolescent who is emotionally unavailable, who does not share anything, who has no visible distress and who cannot be reached; and the parent who says the adolescent is "fine" and is puzzled by the referral. The intervention is a gradual increase of contact, and the risk is that the increase of contact re-ignites whatever produced the rigidity.

**The two-boundary problem.** The adolescent is closely enmeshed with one parent and rigidly distant from the other. This is the classic triangulated adolescent presentation, and it is very common where a family has separated or where one parent is absent. The intervention is directed at the parental subsystem and not at the adolescent, and a practitioner who works with the adolescent alone will achieve very little.

**The boundary that oscillates.** The family alternates between intense closeness and complete distance, and the adolescent learns that the distance follows the closeness. The oscillation is usually maintained by the parent's own unresolved process, and the intervention is the work with the parent, and the adolescent's stability improves when the oscillation stops.

### 6.4 The clinical sequence

**Session 1: the individual.** An assessment with the young person, alone. The purpose is to hear the young person's own account of the family and to establish a relationship that is not mediated by the parents. This session is a structural intervention in itself, because it establishes the young person as a person with a perspective that is not the family's.

**Session 2: the family, and the map.** The family session in which the structure is drawn. The practitioner asks the family to describe a recent instance of the difficulty and to enact it. The drawing of the map follows.

**Session 3: the challenge.** The intervention session, in which the practitioner names the structure, refuses the triangulation, and makes the restructuring explicit. These sessions are frequently uncomfortable and frequently produce a temporary deterioration in the relationship with one or both parents, and the practitioner should be prepared for it and should have said in advance what will happen.

**Session 4 onwards: the maintenance.** The work with the parents on the new structure. The young person is seen individually and the family is seen together, and the balance depends on the case.

### 6.5 Work with the parents when the young person will not attend

A common situation is an adolescent who refuses to attend family sessions. The structural model has a clear answer, and it is more pragmatic than the Bowenian one.

The practitioner works with the parents alone, and the adolescent's absence is treated as a piece of structural information rather than as an obstacle. The parents are asked what the adolescent says about coming, and the answer is usually a statement about the family rather than about the therapy: "she says you'll take her side", "he says we'll all just criticise him", "she says you always talk about her when she's not there." Each of these is a description of a structural problem, and each of them can be worked with.

The practitioner also keeps a direct channel open with the young person, and the most useful practical step is often to shift the individual work to a different setting — a school, a text message, a walk — so that the young person is not in a position in which attendance means entering a structure they have named as unsafe.

## 7. Case Illustration: The Young Man Who Was Not Allowed to Grow Up

**Presentation.** Declan is sixteen and lives with his mother, his father, and a younger sister of eleven. He is referred by the school for "oppositional behaviour and possible ADHD". His mother attends the first appointment and describes a young man who is argumentative, who does not do his chores, who does not do his homework, who is in his room with his door shut, and who is "not the son I raised".

**The individual session.** Declan's own account is different. He describes a household in which he is the family's electrician, the family's driver, the family's shopper, the person who deals with the boiler, the person who looks after his sister when his mother works, and the person who is told to be "a man" by his father and not to be "a baby" by his mother. He says his mother rings him from work when the heating breaks. He says his father has never once asked him about school. He says that if he wants to go to a friend's house he has to have a reason and a time and a plan for getting back.

He also says something that reframes the referral: "You know what the problem is? There's no room for me to be fifteen. I'm either a little kid or I'm a man. There's nothing in between."

**The family session and the map.** The family is invited in and the practitioner asks each of them to describe a recent instance of the difficulty, and the family cannot agree. Declan's father describes an incident last week in which Declan did not come down for dinner. His mother describes an incident in which Declan did not answer the phone when she called from work. Declan describes a different incident entirely, in which his father told him he would be "kicked out" for coming home late.

The practitioner then asks the family to enact the most recent of these, and the mother begins to describe the phone call, and Declan's voice immediately changes, and he says, in a flat and sarcastic register, "I can't do anything right, can I?" — and the practitioner observes the father's immediate shift from the sofa to standing, and the mother's hand going to her mouth, and the whole family reorganising around a fifteen-year-old's sarcastic sentence.

The practitioner interrupts at that point and says: "What I see is that Declan said one sentence and the three of you changed. Declan, what did you want to happen when you said that?" Declan says: "I wanted him to sit down." The practitioner asks his father whether he sat down. He did not. Declan says: "That's what I mean."

**The structural map.** The map is drawn in the session, and it looks like this. There is a parental subsystem in which the mother and the father are joined by a boundary that is diffuse at the point of contact with Declan and rigid at the point of contact with each other. There is a child subsystem of Declan and his sister, in which the siblings are close. The critical feature is between the parents and Declan: the boundary is *clear* in the direction of responsibility and *diffuse* in the direction of emotional involvement, and the map shows this as a line that is solid on one side and dotted on the other.

The projection process is also visible. Declan's mother is a single parent of a nine-year-old sibling when Declan was born, and the grandmother was the primary carer for the first three years. The grandmother died when Declan was four, and the mother describes having been "in pieces" for a year. The father met the mother when Declan was five and has never, by his own account, known how to talk to him; he says "I've never been one of those dads who does the talking." The projection of the grandmother's caretaking onto the mother, and the mother's fear of the loss of another person, are being carried into Declan's role as the family's competent one.

**The formulation.** Declan is not oppositional. He is *parentified*, and the parental subsystem is *collapsed in its parental function*, with the grandmother's function having been taken over by Declan. The family's difficulty is that the adult roles were never re-established after the grandmother's death, and the structure has been maintained for twelve years by the most efficient possible arrangement: the oldest child does it.

The symptom — the argumentativeness, the sarcasm, the closed door, the refusal to do homework — is the report of a young person who is a functional adult in a family that will not let him be fifteen.

**The intervention.** Four elements, and the sequencing matters.

*Element one: the parental subsystem.* The practitioner works with the parents alone for the first four sessions, and the work is not about Declan at all. The first session establishes that they have not spoken to each other about anything other than the children and the bills since Declan was five. The second session addresses the father's account of his own history with his own father, which is a short, angry, and revealing account, and the third addresses the mother's, which is a long account of a loss and of a household that held itself together. The fourth session is a joint session in which the practitioner asks them to describe each other's difficulties, and asks them to say it to each other rather than to him. It is a difficult and unglamorous session and it is the essential one.

*Element two: the boundary renegotiation.* The restructuring is specific and it is written down. Declan will have his own room and his own door, which will be closed when he wishes, and the family will not comment on it. Declan will have one evening a week when he is responsible for a specified household task, and the other two evenings his mother and his sister will manage without him, and this is the hardest part of the plan for the mother. Declan will not be the emergency contact for the heating, the shopping, or the school, and the practitioner will say this to the family explicitly and will then say why: "You have been running this house with a twelve-year-old in a man's job and it has nearly worked. It cannot go on, and it is not fair to him, and I am not going to let the household fail when it happens, so we are going to plan for it."

The practitioner says the last part in the family session, in front of Declan, because the unspoken fear is that the family will collapse when Declan stops, and saying it aloud is what makes the restructuring possible.

*Element three: the father's role.* The father's role is specified and it is small. One specific task, done on a specific day: he will drive Declan and his sister to the football match on Sunday, and they will go to the cafe afterwards, and this is a routine, and routines do not require intimacy. The practitioner explains to the father that Declan does not want a relationship that is emotionally intense; he wants a relationship that is predictable and does not require him to perform.

*Element four: the individual work with Declan.* Declan is seen throughout. The work is about the anger, which is enormous and is entirely justified, and about the difference between the anger at his family and the sarcasm, which is a defence that costs him the room he is in.

**The outcome.** At four months, Declan is still argumentative and is markedly less sarcastic. The chores are done, mostly, and the household tasks have been transferred with two significant relapses, both of which occur when his mother is under pressure at work and both of which are workable. The father and the son have a Sunday routine and have had three arguments in it, which the father reports as "progress, she says". The mother reports that she is "exhausted but different".

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the structural model almost completely. The symptom was structural in origin. The intervention was a restructuring of the boundary, of the hierarchy, and of the parental subsystem, and the individual work with Declan was necessary and was not sufficient. The single most important moment in the whole case was the practitioner's refusal to allow the family to collapse silently, and the naming of the fear in front of the young person. A practitioner who had worked only on Declan's sarcasm, or only on his mother's anxiety, or only on the father-daughter relationship would have achieved a fraction of what was achieved.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The status of the evidence

The structural model has a modest evidence base, and the specific findings are as follows.

**There is no strong controlled evidence for structural family therapy as a whole.** The model's own tradition is more clinical than experimental, and the outcome literature on the specific model is limited.

**The evidence for family-based treatment of adolescent eating disorders is strong**, and the family-based approaches in this area share the structural premise that the parents must be restored to a central and authoritative position in relation to the young person's eating and that the parentification of the mother by the daughter must be interrupted. The family-based approach to anorexia nervosa and to bulimia nervosa is one of the clearest demonstrations in the field that a structural change in the family produces a change in the symptom.

**The evidence for behavioural family therapy for conduct problems is strong**, and the behavioural family therapy protocols are the most thoroughly validated family interventions for oppositional and conduct disorder. They are not structural family therapy, but they are family interventions with a clear structure: the parents are the agents of change, the child is the target, and the parent-child interaction is the medium.

**The evidence for multisystemic therapy is strong**, and the model is a systemic intervention with the same core premise.

**There is modest evidence for structural family therapy with child and adolescent conduct problems**, from a small number of controlled trials, and the results are more variable than those of the behavioural approaches.

### 8.2 What the evidence cannot settle

The question of whether structural family therapy is better than other family approaches is largely unanswerable, because the models are rarely compared and the therapists who practise them are rarely the therapists who evaluate them. The honest position is that the model provides a coherent account and a set of techniques, and that the specific interventions within it have variable support, and that the practitioner should be clear about which of their interventions are evidence-supported and which are technique.

### 8.3 Limitations of the evidence

**Small samples and high allegiance.** The family therapy literature has among the highest allegiance effects in the field, and the structural tradition has been particularly prone to a strong theoretical commitment.

**Proximal outcomes.** The family therapy literature overwhelmingly measures the resolution of the identified symptom, the family functioning, and the satisfaction of the referrer, and rarely measures the distal outcomes.

**The problem of the "response of the identified patient".** In family research, the young person's own account of improvement is often excluded in favour of the parents' account, on the grounds that the parents are more reliable. This is a serious methodological error in a field whose subject is the family, and it has been corrected in the more recent literature.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of the Structural Model

### 9.1 Strengths

**It identifies a cause that can be changed.** The model's claim that the structure causes the symptom is unusual in psychotherapy, and it is a claim that points directly at something that can be worked on.

**It gives a clear description of the pathology.** A practitioner who can say "the parental subsystem has collapsed and the child is functioning as a parent" has a description that is immediately useful, that the family can understand, and that predicts what will help.

**It has a technical repertoire.** The enactment, the challenge, the map, the boundary renegotiation, and the maintenance of change are specific and teachable.

**It fits the adolescent developmental task**, as described above.

**It is compatible with other modalities**, and the structural work is frequently combined with behavioural, cognitive, and attachment work.

**The map is a portable document** that can be used with a school, with a new practitioner, and in a referral.

### 9.2 Limitations

**It is culturally narrow.** The assumptions about the nuclear family, the parental authority, and the appropriate developmental path are specific to a particular cultural context, and the model's language of hierarchy and of parental responsibility carries implicit value judgements that do not transfer.

**It can be used to enforce conformity.** The model's language of the parent's authority and of the child's place can be deployed in ways that are not therapeutic at all, and the boundary work of Chapter 48 is required to prevent this.

**The challenge can be a confrontation.** The structural therapists' willingness to interrupt an enactment in the moment of the young person's greatest distress was, in retrospect, too confident, and the modern practice is more careful about the timing and the aftermath of the intervention.

**It under-accounts for the individual.** A young person who is depressed, or autistic, or has a trauma history, has difficulties that are not structural, and a model that explains everything structurally can obscure them.

**The evidence is modest**, and the model has been rather poorly served by the research literature.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 The cultural assumptions of the model

The structural model embeds a set of assumptions about family life that are not universal, and a student who does not examine them will impose them.

**The nuclear family with two parents.** The model assumes a parental subsystem, and the assumption fails for single-parent families, for families with three or more generations, for separated and never-married parents, for adoptive families, for families with a same-sex couple, and for families in which the kinship system is the extended family or the community.

**The hierarchy.** The model's position that the parents are in charge of the children is a position about parental authority, and parental authority varies enormously across cultures, families, and the contexts in which the young person lives. In some families and communities, the elder generation has authority and the parents do not; in some, the young person is expected to be self-managing from an early age; in some, the family's decisions are made collectively.

**The developmental path.** The structural-developmental formulation assumes a particular sequence: dependence, then differentiation, then autonomy, then leaving. Many young people follow a different sequence, and a family that does not follow the assumed sequence is not thereby pathological.

**The concept of the boundary.** The boundary metaphor is powerful and it is culturally specific. In cultures in which the self is understood as relational, in which interdependence is a value and not a failure of differentiation, the "too close" language of the model is a poor fit, and a family that is described as fused may simply be a family that functions well.

### 10.2 The risk of the model's misuse

The most serious risk in the application of this model is that it becomes an instrument of enforcement. The language of hierarchy, of the parent's authority, and of the child's place is readily turned into a justification for controlling a young person's behaviour, and the model's technical vocabulary lends an authority to that claim that it does not deserve.

Three safeguards follow. The practitioner should be alert to the moment when the work stops being about the young person's development and becomes about the parents' control of the young person. The young person's own account of the structure should be sought directly and weighted appropriately. And the practitioner should maintain the wider frame of Chapter 48, in which the family's structure is examined against the conditions the family lives in.

### 10.3 Ethical considerations

**Consent to family work.** A young person's consent to family sessions is required, and the structure of the sessions should be explained. A young person who is attending under protest is a young person who will be described as resistant, and the description will be believed.

**Confidentiality in a family setting.** Confidentiality cannot be absolute in a family session, and the limits should be stated at the beginning, in terms the young person can act on. The structural therapists' practice of asking the young person to keep the contents of an individual session private from the parents is ethically problematic and should be approached with care.

**The young person's right to a view.** The practitioner should not present a structural formulation to a family as an established fact. A formulation that is presented as a diagnosis will be experienced as a verdict, and the young person will be invited to accept a description of their family that they did not have a part in writing.

**The protection of the young person from the parental subsystem.** Where the work involves a challenge to a parent, the practitioner must anticipate the possibility that the parent will retaliate against the young person, and must plan for it.

**The limits of the work.** A structural intervention is an intervention in a family's organisation, and the practitioner should be clear about the limits of their mandate, about the limits of their competence, and about when the case requires a different or additional response.

**Equity of participation.** The structural model presumes that all family members can participate. The practitioner must consider the family members who cannot — a young person with a communication impairment, a parent with a learning disability, a family member who does not speak the language of the sessions — and must adapt rather than exclude.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Family structure** — The organisation of the family, defined by its subsystems, its boundaries, and its hierarchy, and reflected in its pattern of interaction.
- **Interaction pattern** — The observable pattern of who does what to whom, as described by the family and as enacted in the consulting room.
- **Subsystem** — A group of two or more family members more closely involved with each other than with the others, with its own rules, functions, and boundaries.
- **Individual subsystem** — The person as the smallest unit of family organisation.
- **Sibling subsystem** — The group of siblings, and the unit in which the family's peer relationships are organised.
- **Parent subsystem** — The marital or spousal unit, whose relationship with the child subsystem determines much of what occurs with adolescents.
- **Discrete subsystem** — A subsystem that is clearly bounded from the others, with no crossover.
- **Nurturing subsystem** — A subsystem that is bounded in some respects and permeable in others, allowing the flow of affection and support while preserving distinctness.
- **Boundary** — The line of influence and responsibility separating the subsystems and the individuals within them; invisible but functionally decisive.
- **Diffuse boundary** — An indistinct and permeable boundary, in which the other person's thoughts, feelings, and actions are experienced as one's own.
- **Clear boundary** — A defined boundary appropriate to the developmental stage, permitting the flow of affection and support while maintaining distinctness.
- **Rigid boundary** — An impenetrable and restrictive boundary, usually the residue of a history in which closeness was experienced as dangerous.
- **Hierarchy** — The line of authority across the subsystems, in which the parents are in charge of the children.
- **Hierarchy reversal** — A parent and child who have exchanged roles, with the parent seeking direction and emotional support from the child.
- **Parentification** — A child who takes a parental function, in the practical or the emotional domain, or both.
- **Sibling inversion** — A sibling who takes a parental function over another sibling.
- **Structural map** — The standard diagram of the family, with the subsystems delineated and the boundaries and the hierarchy marked, drawn with the family in session.
- **Enactment** — The invitation to a family to perform in the consulting room the interaction they typically perform outside it, so that the practitioner can observe the structure.
- **The interruption** — The intervention made during an enactment, in which the practitioner changes what the family is doing rather than commenting on what it has said.
- **Challenge** — The general term for the interventions by which the practitioner attempts to change the family's organisation.
- **Restructuring** — The goal of the structural interventions: the movement of a young person from the child subsystem to an adult position where the developmental stage requires it, the redrawing of the boundaries, and the restoration of the hierarchy.
- **Double bind** — Two incompatible messages given to a person with a restriction on the communication of the message, a position from which any response is a symptom.
- **Refusal of the triangulation** — The structural intervention in which the practitioner declines the role the family has assigned them and names the process.
- **Maintenance of change** — The work of keeping a restructuring in place, by securing the family's consensual and explicit commitment and by working with the parent's difficulties with the new structure.
- **Boundary renegotiation** — The developmental task of moving an adolescent from the child subsystem towards an adult position, with the accompanying changes to responsibility and participation.
- **The four structural tasks of adolescence** — Differentiation from the family of origin, the move from the child subsystem, the development of peer relationships, and the acquisition of a vocational and financial direction.
- **Two-boundary problem** — An adolescent who is closely enmeshed with one parent and rigidly distant from the other, the classic triangulated presentation.
- **Oscillating boundary** — A family that alternates between intense closeness and complete distance, so that the distance follows the closeness.
- **Enacting the sequence** — The clinical technique of asking a family member to describe a recent ordinary instance of the difficulty in detail, in order to obtain the interaction pattern.
- **Family projection process in the structural frame** — The projection of the parents' own difficulties onto a child whose structural position is shaped to receive it.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

The structural model was built on the claim that the family is a structure and that the symptoms are structural in origin. Minuchin's work with families of severe and entrenched difficulty produced the claim, and the model's central intervention is a restructuring of the family's organisation, with the enactment as its signature technique.

The five key concepts were developed in turn. Structure is defined both by the subsystems, the boundaries, and the hierarchy and by the observable pattern of interaction, and the distinction between the two was emphasised because each generates a different clinical conversation. Subsystems were described with the terms discrete and nurturing. Boundaries were described in their three forms — diffuse, clear, and rigid — with the clinical presentations of each, and with the observation that the rigid boundary is usually the residue of a history in which closeness was dangerous. Hierarchy was described with its three disorders: parentification, hierarchy reversal, and sibling inversion. The family projection process was described in the specific structural sense in which the projected child is also the structurally positioned child.

Structural diagnosis was described as a four-stage procedure, and the distinction between the structural and the interactional formulation was made explicit with a worked example, because the two produce different interventions and the structural formulation is the one that predicts what will happen if the practitioner works only on the interaction.

The five principles of the model were then developed: the structure causes the symptom, the family is the unit of treatment, the intervention is the restructuring, the enactment is the means of diagnosis, and the change must be maintained. The enactment was described in detail — the procedure, what it reveals, the interruption, and the risk — with the explicit warning that the modern practice is more careful than the original about the timing of the interruption and the young person's state afterwards. The maintenance of change was described with the three requirements of consensus, explicitness, and the family's own maintenance mechanisms.

The developmental fit with adolescence was developed through the four structural tasks, and the four boundary problems were described with the intervention appropriate to each. The clinical sequence was given, and the case of the adolescent who will not attend was treated with the model's pragmatic answer, in which the refusal is read as structural information.

The case of Declan demonstrated the model in full. His symptoms were not oppositional but the report of a young person who was parentified in a family whose parental subsystem had collapsed and whose grandmother's function had been absorbed by him. The enactment produced the decisive moment — the family reorganising around a sarcastic sentence — and the practitioner's interruption asked the question that produced the formulation. The intervention was four elements, and the most important of them was the practitioner naming, in front of the young person, the family's unspoken fear that it would collapse when he stopped, and promising that it would not.

The evidence was presented honestly: the model has modest controlled evidence, and the stronger evidence in the adjacent areas is for family-based treatment of eating disorders, for behavioural family therapy, and for multisystemic therapy. The evidence's limits were named, including the serious methodological problem of the exclusion of the identified patient's own account.

The strengths and limitations were set out, and the limitations included the cultural narrowness, the risk of the model being used for enforcement, the over-confident challenge, the under-accounting of the individual, and the modest evidence. The cultural analysis examined the assumptions of the nuclear family, of the hierarchy, of the developmental path, and of the boundary metaphor itself, and the ethical section addressed consent to family work, confidentiality in the family setting, the young person's right to a view, the protection of the young person from the parental subsystem, the limits of the mandate, and the equity of participation.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 31.1 — Drawing a Structural Map (90 minutes, field-based)

In a placement, take one family and draw the structural map: the subsystems, the boundaries, and the hierarchy. Mark each boundary as clear, diffuse, or rigid, and justify each judgement from observed or reported interaction. Then write a sentence naming the position of each young person in the family. Do not share the map with the family. The exercise teaches the difference between the structure as a description and the structure as a verdict, and the third stage of the activity — the position of the young people — is where the interpretive leap has to be made.

### Activity 31.2 — The Enactment Script (75 minutes, small group)

In groups of four, one student plays a sixteen-year-old, one plays the mother, one plays the father, and one plays the practitioner. The practitioner asks the family to enact a recent instance of a conflict, and the practitioner's task is to observe and then to interrupt. The three family members then describe what happened from their own positions, and the practitioner describes what they saw. The exercise is the most instructive in the book for the enactment, because the students discover that what the family can describe and what the family can do are quite different things, and that the practitioner's intervention is a change in the behaviour rather than a comment about it.

### Activity 31.3 — The Cultural Audit of a Map (60 minutes, individual)

Take a structural map from a family in a context that is not the one in which you were trained — a different cultural setting, a different family form, a different expectation about the developmental path — and audit it. For every element of the map, ask: is this a description of this family, or is this an expectation imported from my own context? Rewrite the map so that it describes the family as it is, without the imported expectations. Most students find that a substantial part of the original map was expectation, and this is the most useful single lesson in the chapter.

### Activity 31.4 — The Refusal of the Triangulation (60 minutes, pairs, discussion)

In pairs, one student plays a parent who has been confiding in their sixteen-year-old about their marital difficulties, and the other plays the practitioner. The practitioner must refuse the role — decline to be drawn into the confidences, name the process, and explain to the parent why the arrangement is being changed. The second half of the exercise is the harder part: the practitioner must then describe what will happen next, including the fact that the parent's relationship with the practitioner may deteriorate, and including what the practitioner will do about the young person in the meantime. The preparation for this conversation is more useful than almost any other exercise in Part V.

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model.** Read Minuchin (1974) and then Minuchin and colleagues (1981), and read Minuchin and Nichols (1993) for the modern statement. Read the 1978 paper as the shortest complete statement of the model.

**For the technique.** Read Minuchin, Sell, and Montalvo (1998) for the clinical practice, and read the chapters on enactment and on the challenge in any current structural text. The technique is learned by supervision and by practice, and the readings support rather than substitute for it.

**For the field.** Read Nichols (2013) for the whole family therapy field, so that the structural model can be placed alongside the strategic and the Bowenian. Reading only the structural school is how a student ends up unable to compare.

**For the evidence.** Read the family-based treatment literature on adolescent eating disorders, read the behavioural family therapy evidence, and read the multisystemic therapy evidence. These are the studies that actually support the parental-involvement claim, and the structural model's central clinical assertion.

**For the critique.** Read the critique of structural family therapy on the grounds of social construction, and read the critical literature on the use of family therapy in contested and safeguarding contexts. The combination is the preparation for the work in Part VIII.
