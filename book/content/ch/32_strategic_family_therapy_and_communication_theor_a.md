# Strategic Family Therapy and Communication Theory

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of the strategic school and describe the clinical problems that produced it.
- State the core concepts of the strategic model, including the problem-maintaining sequence, the double bind, the symptom as a metaphor, and the reframing.
- Describe the techniques of the strategic school: the enactment of the sequence, the directives, the prescription, the reframe, and the restriction.
- Explain the contribution of communication theory to the field, and distinguish it from the strategic model.
- Describe the "team of the strategic" approach and the concept of the problem as a solution.
- Apply the strategic framework to a case involving an adolescent and a family.
- Critically evaluate the ethical and cultural dimensions of strategic family work, particularly the use of directives and the question of deception.

## Chapter Overview

The strategic school began as an answer to a question the structural therapists had not asked. If a symptom is structural in origin, then the structure is the treatment target. But Jay Haley and his colleagues in the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto asked a different question: **what maintains the problem right now?**

Their answer was that a problem continues because it solves something. The young person who refuses to go to school, who is unable to say no, or who has become quiet and withdrawn, is doing something that has consequences the young person needs, and the symptom will not go away while the consequences continue. The strategic therapist's task is therefore to identify the sequence in which the problem is produced and maintained, to change the sequence, and to use the young person's own motivation for change rather than a confrontation.

The strategic school's technical repertoire is distinctive and it is worth knowing it in detail, because some of it is directly transferable to other modalities and some of it is ethically hazardous in ways that the profession has had to confront.

**The problem-maintaining sequence** is the central clinical object. It is the specific chain of events, from the trigger through the young person's behaviour to the family's response and back, that keeps the problem going. It is mapped in detail, and it is mapped in the family's own terms.

**The directive** is a specific, dated, behavioural instruction given by the therapist to a family member. It is the strategic school's most distinctive intervention and the one with the most interesting ethical profile.

**The prescription** is a task that is deliberately difficult, designed to fail in a way that produces a new and more adaptive response.

**The reframing** is the reinterpretation of the problem, offered to the family in a way that makes the symptom more understandable and more tolerable, and that implicitly suggests a way out.

**The restriction** is the instruction to suspend a behaviour that has become a trap, most commonly a parental response that has inadvertently become the solution to the problem.

**Communication theory** — the study of the patterned exchanges between people, and the observation that a communication is always about something and is always a response to a communication — sits alongside the strategic model in the same tradition and supplies the analytic vocabulary. The most famous contribution is the description of the **double bind**, and the observation that a family in which every communication is a metacommunication — a comment about a comment — has a very serious problem.

This chapter presents the model, the communication theory that underlies it, the techniques, and the ethical analysis, which in this school is unusually substantial because the school's techniques include the deliberate management of information.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The Mental Research Institute

The strategic school developed in the 1960s at the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto, California, in a project on the communication of families with a member who had been diagnosed as schizophrenic. The project was led by Gregory Bateson, with Don Jackson, Paul Watzlawick, and John Weakland, and it produced the work that is now called communication theory.

The founding observation was that the families of people diagnosed with schizophrenia communicated in distinctive ways, and the distinctive feature was not the content of the communications but their patterning. The families were characterised by **double binds** — in which a person receives two incompatible messages with a restriction on the communication of the message — and by **symmetrical and complementary** patterns of interaction, and by a communication system in which every message was a comment on a previous message, so that no message could be checked against reality.

The clinical significance of the finding was modest — the double bind did not explain schizophrenia, and the field has long since rejected the attempt to explain it — but the analytic contribution was enormous, and the vocabulary it produced is now the shared language of all the family therapies.

### 1.2 Haley and the strategic school

Jay Haley moved from the research group into the clinical work and developed the strategic model, and the shift is worth tracing because it explains the character of the model.

In the research setting, the goal was to describe. In the clinical setting, the goal is to change, and a description is valuable only insofar as it points to a change. Haley's contribution was to take the communication analysis and turn it into a set of **directives** — specific instructions, given by the therapist, that interrupt the sequence and produce a different outcome.

**Haley's "tricks"** — the paradoxical injunction, the paradoxical prescription, the ordeal, the team of the strategic — were his characteristic innovations, and they are technically brilliant and ethically contested, and the contemporary field has retained some of them and abandoned others.

### 1.3 The problem of the stuck family

The clinical problem the school addressed was the **stuck family**: the family in which the problem has been stable for a long time, in which everyone knows the pattern, in which previous attempts at change have produced more of the same, and in which the family has come to see the problem as the price of the family.

The strategic school's answer was to change the meaning of the problem before changing the behaviour. If the young person's symptom is understood as the solution to a problem the family cannot solve any other way, then the symptom will not go away until the family can solve the problem some other way. The reframing supplies that other way, and the directive tests it.

### 1.4 The place of the strategic school today

The strategic model's influence on the field is very large and diffuse. The **solution-focused** work of Chapter 35, the **narrative** work of Chapter 36, and the **communication-based** approaches to eating disorders and to substance use all descend in part from it. The concept of the **"problem is a solution"** has entered the general vocabulary of family therapy. The model's technical vocabulary of sequences, directives, and reframings is standard in the training of every family therapist.

The model's specific techniques, however, have been substantially modified, and the two most controversial — the paradoxical injunction and the deliberate management of information — are now used far more cautiously.

## 2. The Core Concepts

### 2.1 The problem-maintaining sequence

The **problem-maintaining sequence** is the central clinical object of the model. It is a specific, mapped, and dated chain of events that keeps the problem going, and it is mapped in a form that makes each element a candidate for change.

A typical sequence in a school-refusal case runs like this: the young person is asked to go to school → the young person says she is ill → the parent believes her → the parent contacts the school → the parent tells the school the child is ill → the school agrees → the young person stays at home → the day passes → the young person is at home again without having had to face the school → the next morning is another day on which the sequence can begin.

Every element is a candidate for change, and the mapping makes the clinical possibilities visible. A change at the "the parent believes her" point produces a different outcome. A change at the "the school agrees" point produces a different outcome. A change at the "the day passes" point — that is, an activity that makes the day at home less tolerable — produces a different outcome.

The mapping is done **in the family's own words** and with **specific instances**, and the practitioner should be able to state the general rule and the specific example. A generic sequence is useless.

### 2.2 The symptom as a solution

The strategic school's foundational claim is that **the symptom is a solution**. The young person's behaviour is not the problem; it is the best solution the family has found to a problem that is not being solved any other way.

The claim has three parts. The problem is a **dilemma** — it is a genuine problem and a solution at the same time, and the two cannot be separated. The solution has **side effects** that are the reason for referral. And the solution is **not the only one**, which is the space in which the therapy operates.

The reformulation of the referral is then something like: "the current solution to the problem of X is Y, and Y is now causing more difficulty than it was designed to solve, and there is another solution available."

### 2.3 The double bind

The **double bind** is the communication pattern in which a person receives two mutually incompatible messages, and is unable to comment on, question, or escape the situation in which they receive them. Bateson's formulation has four conditions, and a student should be able to state them.

1. There must be a **contradictory pair of messages**.
2. There must be a **repeated pattern** in which the contradiction is maintained.
3. There must be a **restriction on the recipient's ability to respond** — to question, to comment, to leave.
4. There must be a **punishment or a threat** for any attempt to resolve the contradiction by noticing it.

In practice, the double bind is rarely a matter of two spoken messages. It is more often a mismatch between what is said and what is done: a parent says "you are an adult, make your own decisions" while punishing any decision that differs from the parent's own. The young person is thereby unable to act, unable to refuse, and unable to name what is happening, and the inability to name it is a substantial part of why the pattern persists.

**Bateson's paradox.** A self-referential paradox occurs when the message denies the validity of the communication system in which it is transmitted, and a young person who is told "don't question me" and "you should question me" is caught in it. The clinical significance of the concept for the practitioner is that the young person is in a position in which the most reasonable responses are all pathological: compliance produces resentment, resistance produces punishment, and the attempt to discuss it is itself treated as an offense.

### 2.4 Symmetrical and complementary patterns

**Symmetrical** patterns are those in which the participants' behaviours mirror each other: the escalating and escalating, the withdrawing and withdrawing, the panic and the panic. Symmetrical interactions tend to escalate and to be difficult to interrupt.

**Complementary** patterns are those in which the participants' behaviours are complementary to each other: the one is dominant and the other is submissive, the one is anxious and the other is reassuring. Complementary interactions are stable and can be highly functional, and they are also the patterns in which a problem is most easily maintained, because the roles are mutually reinforcing: the more the one withdraws, the more the other pursues, and the more the other pursues, the more the first withdraws.

### 2.5 The metacommunication

A **metacommunication** is a communication about a communication. Every message carries, in addition to its content, a commentary on the relationship in which it is transmitted: *I am telling you what to do*, *I am asking you*, *I am warning you*, *I am apologising*.

A family in which every message is a metacommunication has a serious problem, because the content becomes unavailable for checking against reality. The young person who is told "I am just telling you for your own good" is receiving a message that cannot be examined, because examining it would be a comment on the metacommunication, which is the prohibited move.

The clinical task in work with such a family is to **restore the ability to make content statements** — to get the family to a place where a message can be said to be true or false — and this is often the first substantive change in the work.

## 3. The Techniques

### 3.1 The enactment of the sequence

As in the structural model, the sequence is enacted in the consulting room, but the purpose differs. The structural enactment is diagnostic of the *structure*; the strategic enactment is a **test** of the sequence, and the intervention is made at the point at which the sequence is about to complete itself.

The technique is delicate. The practitioner interrupts at the moment at which the young person is about to say the thing that will produce the family response, and says something that stops the sequence — a question, a request for information, a statement that the practitioner has noticed something. The interruption demonstrates that the sequence is not inevitable, and the demonstration is the intervention.

### 3.2 The directive

A **directive** is a specific, dated, behavioural instruction given by the therapist to a family member. It is the strategic school's most distinctive intervention, and its distinguishing features are its specificity, its attribution to the therapist, and its date.

**The attribution matters.** A directive is not a suggestion and not a request. The therapist says, "I am telling you to do this, and I am telling you because I am the person in this room with the most experience of this problem." The attribution provides the authority that the instruction requires, and the strategic therapists were clear that a directive issued as advice is a wasted instruction.

**The specificity matters.** A directive must be concrete enough to be performed: not "be firmer with him" but "when he asks to go up to his room, you will say 'you may go up to your room for ten minutes after you have done the washing-up, and I will knock on your door at seven'."

**The date matters.** A directive with no date is a wish.

**The directive must produce a testable result.** The strategic school's directives are designed so that something happens that either confirms or disconfirms the hypothesis, and the test is what keeps the work from becoming manipulation.

### 3.3 The paradoxical injunction

The **paradoxical injunction** is a directive that asks a family member to do the very thing the symptom involves, and to do it deliberately, and at a time and in a manner that makes it tolerable or even useful.

The reasoning is that the symptom is maintained by the family's attempt to prevent it. If the young person's withdrawal is met with escalating attempts to prevent it, the withdrawal continues. If the withdrawal is permitted, and even encouraged, in a way that is clearly deliberate, the meaning changes: the young person is no longer doing it to defy, and the family is no longer trying to prevent it, and the sequence that maintained it is broken.

The paradoxical injunction is the most technically elegant and the most ethically hazardous intervention in the family therapy field, and the ethical analysis in Section 10 is devoted to it.

### 3.4 The paradoxical prescription

The **paradoxical prescription** asks a family member to perform the symptom's function deliberately, in a way that will probably fail, in order that the failure will be instructive. If a young person is refusing to go to school, the paradoxical prescription might be: "I want you to make sure she does not go to school. I want you to be the one who keeps her at home. I want you to take over, completely, for the next two weeks."

The mechanism is that the prescription is impossible to perform, and the impossibility exposes the family to the fact that the solution they have been relying on does not work, and the exposure is what makes a different solution available. The technique is used in the treatment of problems that are maintained by a paradoxical pattern, and it is a sophisticated intervention with a small place in contemporary practice.

### 3.5 The ordeal

The **ordeal** asks a family member to undertake a difficult task with a defined and serious cost, undertaken after and for the sake of a change in the identified patient. The reasoning is that a task that is costly enough will mobilise a commitment that no instruction can.

The technique is now almost entirely discredited, and it should be understood historically. Its clinical rationale was that the ordeal demonstrated that the family would pay a real price for the change, and that this demonstration changed the family members' positions. Its ethical problems are severe: the burden falls on a family member who did not choose the symptom, the "cost" is real suffering, and the pressure on a vulnerable family member is difficult to distinguish from coercion.

### 3.6 The reframing

The **reframe** is the reinterpretation of the problem, and it is the most useful of the strategic interventions in contemporary practice, because it is also the least hazardous.

A reframe offers the family a different account of the symptom that makes it more understandable, more tolerable, and more tractable. It must be offered as a *hypothesis*, and it must fit the facts, and it must be a reframe that the family can use.

Some of the strategic school's reframes are famous and some are now regarded as objectionable. The reframe of the "identified patient" as the family's **sacrificial scapegoat** was central to the school's theory and is now understood as historically important and currently unacceptable, because it attributes a family member's illness to the family's need, and it can be heard as a claim that the young person is faking, which is both untrue and damaging.

A more defensible reframe in contemporary use: the young person who cannot leave the house is not refusing to leave but is unable to manage the state that leaving produces; the young person who cannot say no is not being difficult but is doing the only thing available in a system where saying yes has never been safe; the young person who cannot concentrate is not being lazy but is protecting herself from a level of stimulation that is intolerable.

The clinical test of a reframe is whether it opens a door or closes one. A reframe that the family hears as an accusation closes the door. A reframe that the family hears as a relief opens it.

### 3.7 The restriction

A **restriction** is an instruction to suspend a behaviour that has become part of the problem. The classic example is a parent whose vigilance has become the very stimulus the young person reacts to: a mother who checks on her daughter every twenty minutes, whose concern has trained the daughter to become alert at the sound of the door.

The restriction is straightforward to make and surprisingly powerful, and it is one of the interventions that most reliably produces a change in the sequence, because the sequence depends on the behaviour being restricted.

### 3.8 The team of the strategic

The **team of the strategic** is a technique in which a family member is instructed to become the therapist's ally in changing another family member, and to do so covertly. The father is instructed to cooperate with the mother's new plan while appearing to the mother to be the ally of the family against the practitioner.

The technique is powerful and it is ethically the most contentious in the family therapy field, because it involves the deliberate management of information in a way that the person being managed is not aware of. The ethical analysis in Section 10 addresses it directly, and the contemporary position is that the technique should be used only with the explicit knowledge of all parties and with the informed consent of the person whose information is being managed.

## 4. Communication Theory in Practice

### 4.1 The rules of communication

Communication theory's contribution to practice is a set of observations about the rules that govern family interaction, and the four most useful are as follows.

**You cannot not communicate.** Every behaviour, including the refusal to speak, is a message. This has a direct clinical consequence: the practitioner should not treat a young person's silence as an absence of communication but as a communication, and should ask what the silence is doing.

**Everything has a meaning. You cannot not have a meaning.** Every message implies a claim about the relationship and about the legitimate range of what may be said. The claim implied by "you should know better than to ask" is that the ask was illegitimate, and the recipient learns this.

**You cannot get a message other than the one you send. Communication is irreversible. And you cannot not understand a message. Once you have understood a message, you cannot forget it.** The clinical consequence is that the practitioner's own messages to a family are irreversible, and that a practitioner's poorly worded remark in a corridor will be understood and will not be forgotten.

**Communication is always contextual. Every message has a metacommunication, and every metacommunication is a comment on the previous message.** A family locked into a metacommunicative spiral has lost the ability to make content statements, and the first task is to restore it.

### 4.2 The double bind with adolescents

The double bind is particularly relevant to adolescents, and three common forms recur.

**The developmental double bind.** The parent requires the adolescent to be a child in the areas of dependence and an adult in the areas of responsibility. The adolescent cannot win: the child behaviour is punished as immature and the adult behaviour is punished as insolence.

**The love–control bind.** "I do this because I love you" combined with "you are not allowed to have a say in this" is a double bind in which the message of care is used to prevent the very response that would make the care effective.

**The two-message bind.** The verbal message and the non-verbal message contradict each other: the parent says "of course you can go out" while the facial expression, the sigh, and the tone say the opposite. The adolescent is caught, and the double bind is reinforced when the adolescent mentions it, because the parental response to the mention is a defence of the parent.

### 4.3 The communication audit

A practical technique from the communication tradition is the **communication audit**: the family member is asked to record, over a defined period, the messages they receive and send, and the practitioner and the family member then examine them together.

For an adolescent, a simplified version is often productive: the young person is asked to record the messages they receive about school, about curfews, about friends, and about their body, and to note the content and the metacommunication, and the two then look for the contradictions. The exercise is also a useful anxiety-management technique, since the recording converts a diffuse sense of being controlled into a specific set of statements that can be examined.
