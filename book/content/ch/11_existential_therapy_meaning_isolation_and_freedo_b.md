## 8. Evidence and Research Support

The evidence position for existential therapy is more qualified than the theory's standing in the professional imagination, and a student should be able to state the position precisely.

### 8.1 What is well supported

**The clinical relevance of the existential concerns with young people.** The developmental research on the adolescent identity task, on the possible self, on the sense of the future, and on the age-graded development of the capacity for abstract and symbolic thought provides strong support for the clinical observation that the four givens are pressing and developmentally appropriate in the second decade. This is not evidence for existential therapy as a treatment; it is evidence for the relevance of the problems the theory addresses, and it is the most substantial support the theory has.

**The relationship between meaning and adolescent mental health.** The research on meaning in life is substantial and consistent: meaning in life is associated with lower levels of depression, anxiety, and hopelessness, with greater life satisfaction and well-being, with better academic engagement, with greater resilience, and with lower rates of suicidal ideation. The association is bidirectional — the loss of meaning is a feature of depression, and depression corrodes meaning — and the studies are largely correlational, so the causal direction is not settled. The research does establish that meaning is a substantial and modifiable correlate of adolescent mental health, which supports the theory's clinical emphasis.

**The centrality of the therapeutic relationship.** The alliance research discussed in Chapters 5 and 6 supports the elements of the existential formulation that are relational. The finding that the outcome of therapy is predicted by the quality of the relationship, and that this holds across theories including those with no relational component, supports the claim that the conditions of the encounter are the active ingredient.

**The psychotherapeutic and philosophical tradition in the treatment of meaninglessness.** The evidence for the effectiveness of meaning-oriented interventions in a variety of populations, including in the treatment of depression in adults, is moderate and growing, and the "meaning" literature in occupational therapy, in rehabilitation, and in organisational psychology has developed a substantial evidence base of its own.

### 8.2 What is supported with qualifications

**Existential therapy as a treatment.** The outcome studies of existential therapy with adults are small in number and generally of low methodological quality. A small number of controlled trials in the 1980s and 1990s reported favourable results in comparison to wait-list controls and in some cases to other treatments, and the results have never been replicated at a scale that would permit confident conclusions. The systematic reviews consistently rate the evidence for existential therapy as limited.

**Existential therapy with adolescents.** The evidence is thinner still. A small number of controlled studies with young people, most of them in school settings, have reported positive effects, principally on meaning, on hope, and on the reduction of depressive symptoms. The samples are small, the designs are weak, the measures are largely self-report, and the follow-up periods are short. The honest statement is that existential therapy with young people is theoretically well-motivated by the developmental literature, clinically plausible, and empirically under-supported.

**The differential effectiveness of existential therapy.** There is no reliable evidence that existential therapy is more effective than other bona fide treatments for any condition, and the general finding of the field — the equivalence of bona fide therapies discussed in Chapter 6 — applies.

### 8.3 What is weak, contested, or absent

**The theory's metaphysical claims.** The propositions that existence precedes essence, that the person is radically free and responsible, and that existence is meaningless are philosophical claims, and they are not testable and are not supported or refuted by outcome data. A student should be clear that the theory's central propositions are of a different logical type from its clinical propositions, and that the clinical evidence does not bear on them.

**The determinism question.** The apparent contradiction between the existential claim of radical freedom and the psychological literature on the constraints of behaviour, mental illness, and the social determinants of health is not a contradiction within the theory but a tension between the theory and the empirical sciences. The tension has been sharpened by the developmental and neuroscience literature, which locates decision-making in a brain with substantial developmental constraints. A contemporary existential therapist who claims that the young person is entirely free is making a claim that the developmental and neuroscientific evidence does not support, and a student should be able to say so.

**Specificity.** The theory's lack of a specified technique repertoire is a genuine weakness for evidence-based practice, for treatment research, and for a service that needs to specify what it delivers.

**The cross-cultural question.** The existential account of the person is one of the most culturally marked in the field. The claims about individual freedom, individual responsibility, individual meaning, and the individual's relation to death are claims of a Western European philosophical tradition, and the cross-cultural psychology literature finds substantial variation in the endorsed meaning structures, with strong collectivist and relational alternatives. A practitioner working with a young person whose cultural framework locates meaning in relationship, in duty, and in community rather than in personal choice will find that the theory's questions need considerable translation.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Existential Therapy

**Strengths.** It takes seriously the questions that young people actually ask, which few therapies do. It locates the difficulty in the person's relation to their life rather than in a disorder, which resists pathologising. It offers a coherent and moving account of the therapeutic encounter, with an operational form in Yalom's four elements. It is a powerful framework for work with young people facing death, terminal illness, severe disability, displacement, and loss, where the meaning questions are the actual clinical questions. It attends to the limits of what a service can do, which is an ethical strength in a field with a tendency to promise more than it can deliver. And it integrates the affective, the cognitive, and the relational in a way that few theories manage.

**Limitations.** It is empirically weak, and its proponents' confidence in it exceeds the evidence by a considerable margin. It has little to offer the practitioner who needs a specific technique for a specific problem, and its generalisation to the full range of clinical presentations with young people is untested. Its language can be grandiose or evasive, and the practitioners who use it are not always the most disciplined about their own. Its cultural specificity is a genuine and serious limitation in a culturally diverse practice. And the question of how to work with a young person in acute crisis — where the existential questions are not accessible because the young person is in a state of terror — is one the theory addresses least well.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**The cultural specificity of the existential individual.** The following claims are all culturally marked and are worth naming individually: that the person is the primary unit of meaning-making; that meaning is a matter of personal choice; that isolation is a universal human condition to be borne; that responsibility for one's existence cannot be shared; and that confronting death is a task for the individual. The cross-cultural literature finds that many communities structure meaning around relationship, duty, filial obligation, and communal identity, and that in many of these contexts the individual's search for personal meaning is experienced as a Western imposition, and that the suffering that is described in existential terms is often experienced as a rupture of a relational or moral order.

The ethical consequence for the practitioner is direct. The existential questions are not neutral invitations; they carry an implicit theory of a good life. A young person from a family for whom filial obligation is the central moral structure may experience the practitioner who asks "what do you want?" as asking them to reject their family, and the "anxiety of freedom" that the practitioner names may be precisely the anxiety of a young person who has been told that their obligations are the source of their confinement. The culturally responsive practice is to hold the question — because the question is real — while holding the family and the culture as a possible answer rather than as an obstacle to it.

**The ethical danger of the existential framing of suicide.** The existential account of suicidal crisis — that the young person is in a coherent system of meaning in which death is the only available exit — is clinically illuminating and ethically dangerous if it is used to interpret rather than to assess. The ethical requirement is that risk assessment and management precede interpretation, that the practitioner does not treat a suicidal young person as being in search of meaning, and that the response to a young person's disclosure of suicidal ideation is immediate, concrete, and grounded in an appraisal of the risk. Chapter 54 sets out the procedures, and Appendix H provides the documentation templates.

**The ethical requirement not to aestheticise suffering.** The existential temptation is to make the young person's suffering beautiful, or significant, or part of a growth process. A young person's depression is not a chapter in a developmental novel, and a practitioner who treats it as one has taken something from the young person and given them a story in its place. The ethical requirement is to take the young person's suffering seriously as suffering, and to be careful with the language of growth.

**The ethical value of the approach.** And the ethical value of the existential approach should also be stated: it is one of the few theories in the field that takes a young person's own existential questions seriously, that declines to answer them with reassurance, and that treats the young person's search as legitimate. For a young person who has been told by every adult in their life that they are too young to think about such things, this is a considerable gift.

## 11. Case Illustration: Theo, Who Did Not Want to Be Helped

**The referral.** Theo, sixteen, is referred by his school for "low mood, disengagement, and a pattern of self-harm." The referral notes that Theo has cut in the past year, has missed substantial amounts of school, and has told a member of staff that "there's no point in any of it." His mother has been contacted and reports that Theo "has always been different" and that she "doesn't know what to do." There is a history of a bereavement: Theo's father died by suicide eighteen months ago.

**The assessment.** The practitioner begins with a risk assessment, because it is required and because the referral is explicit. Theo's self-harm is infrequent, superficial, and typically follows an argument with his mother. His statement about there being no point is a statement of hopelessness rather than of a plan, and he has no plan, no means, and no history of serious attempt. The risk is assessed as moderate, and a safety plan is made with Theo and his mother, with clear escalation steps. The practitioner also asks the question that the referral does not ask: what did Theo's father's suicide mean to Theo, and what has Theo concluded from it? Theo says, without being pressed: "That you can do it, and that it works. And that everyone ends up doing it eventually, so what's the point of trying to be different."

**The existential formulation.** Theo's difficulty is not primarily depressive in the clinical sense, and the referral's framing is misleading. It is a *determining system* in Yalom's sense, and it is doing serious work. Theo's conclusion — that everyone ends up doing it, so effort is pointless — solves four problems at once. It explains his father's death, so that the death does not require any further meaning. It renders the future unnecessary, so that the anxiety of freedom and the anxiety of meaninglessness are both resolved. It provides a "determining system" in which his disengagement is not a choice but a recognition of the truth, and it gives him a role — the person who has seen it — that carries a certain dignity.

The determining system is also, importantly, a *correct* appraisal in one respect. People do sometimes die by suicide, and Theo is not wrong that the possibility exists. The therapy therefore cannot consist of showing him that the possibility is false, because it is not.

**The first intervention.** The practitioner's first move is not an interpretation. It is a question about the specific: "You said everyone's ends up doing it eventually. Can I ask you about your father? What was he like?" This is the existential move — entering the determining system rather than arguing with it — and it is also the developmentally appropriate move with a sixteen-year-old, who is capable of a real dialogue about his father and who will not tolerate being psychoanalysed.

**The work.** Over the following sessions, Theo talks about his father, and the practitioner follows him into the territory without steering. What emerges is that Theo was the one who found his father, and that he has organised his entire life since around a single, unspeakable guilt: that he could have done something. The determining system is not a philosophical position. It is a catastrophic scene in which every effort is futile because he has already failed once in the most important matter of his life.

The therapeutic work is then straightforward and difficult. The practitioner works to establish that what happened that evening was not within Theo's power to alter, which requires the practitioner to spend a great deal of time on the specific and concrete circumstances of that evening, in the manner of a trauma-focused approach, and to help Theo to construct a version of the evening in which his presence or absence makes no difference, which is true and which Theo can verify. The existential framing is present throughout: the work is to enable Theo to relinquish a determining system that has cost him his future, and to make a different set of commitments available. But the work is cognitive, in the sense that it involves a specific reorganisation of a specific belief, and it is behavioural, in the sense that it involves the graded re-engagement with school and with his friends.

**The developmental dimension.** Theo's case is a developmental case as well as an existential one. The developmental literature is clear that the death of a parent in adolescence is one of the most significant developmental disruptions there is, and that it produces predictable disruption of the identity task, of the attachment system, of the peer relationships, and of the future orientation. Theo has responded to this disruption by an arrest of the developmental process, and the therapy is in part a resumption of the developmental process, and the framework in Chapter 9 is as useful here as the existential one.

**The ethical dimension.** Three ethical requirements were engaged. The first is the requirement that the risk be assessed and managed before the existential work begins, and the practitioner notes in the record that the order was deliberate. The second is the requirement not to aestheticise Theo's father's suicide, which is a live risk in this case, because the account of a father's suicide in a family narrative is very often a story in which the death was a completion of a meaningful arc, and the practitioner repeatedly returns to the specific and the ordinary: what the father was like, what he did on ordinary days, what was said in the car. The third is the requirement to include Theo's mother appropriately. She is, on the evidence, one of the two people most damaged by the death, and her account of Theo as "always different" is a deflection from her own grief. The work with the two of them is the conjoint part of the therapy, and the practitioner is careful to frame the work with Theo as a way of helping him, rather than as a way of helping her.

**The outcome.** Theo's improvement is partial and real. The self-harm stops. The school attendance improves substantially. The determining system loosens: Theo says, in the twelfth session, that he has begun to think about doing something after he leaves school, and that this is new, and that it is frightening. The therapy continues at a reduced frequency, and the termination is planned around the end of the school year, with the themes of the future developed as the explicit agenda. The practitioner judges the outcome to be good but not complete, and records the residual risk explicitly, which is the appropriate thing to do.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Existence precedes essence** — The Sartrean proposition that the person is a project who becomes something through choosing and acting, rather than a fixed nature who then acts.
- **The givens of existence** — The four existential facts — mortality, freedom and responsibility, isolation, and meaninglessness — whose implications are experienced as anxiety.
- **The nightmare of the possible** — The anxiety arising from the recognition that one might never have existed; the arbitrariness of existence rather than its ending.
- **Existential anxiety** — The anxiety that arises from the confrontation with the givens of existence, as distinct from the anxiety of a threatening situation.
- **The they-self** — The impersonal, conventional mode of being in which the person takes over the general view and thereby avoids the anxiety of individual existence.
- **Determining system** — Yalom's term for the structure of beliefs and commitments adopted to avoid the anxiety of a given, which thereby forecloses choice and takes responsibility off the person.
- **Bad faith** — Sartre's term for the flight from the anxiety of freedom through self-deception, particularly in the determination of one's own identity by a fixed project.
- **Existential vacuum** — The specific emptiness of a life in which nothing has been chosen because nothing needs to be; Frankl's term for the phenomenon most often presented as depression.
- **Existential isolation** — The irreducible condition of no one having access to one's own experience, which underpins every relationship.
- **Primary, secondary, and existential isolation** — Yalom's threefold distinction, distinguishing the infant's isolation, the isolation arising in a life, and the isolation of the individual as such.
- **Prime therapeutic factor** — Yalom's term for the client's discovery that there are more choices available than had been supposed.
- **The four elements** — Presence, empathy, authenticity, and emotional involvement; Yalom's operational formulation of the existential therapeutic stance.
- **Existential empathy** — An understanding of the client's lived situation, including their relation to finitude, freedom, isolation, and possibility, rather than an understanding of their experience as a set of problems.
- **Will to meaning** — Frankl's proposition that the primary human motivation is the striving to find and live a meaningful life, which is more basic than the pursuit of pleasure or power.
- **Paradoxical intention** — The logotherapeutic technique of asking the client to wish for the thing they fear, in order to transform a passive relationship to the fear into an active one.
- **Logotherapy** — Frankl's theory and method, organised around the will to meaning and the three paths to meaning.
- **Responsible choice** — The clinical requirement that the young person be helped to make and carry out a choice, rather than being relieved of the burden of one.
- **Unfinished business** — The clinical phrase for the material that a young person brings to the session that concerns their dead, and which the existential practitioner treats as legitimate material rather than as a symptom.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Existential therapy is the only theory in this book that begins with a philosophy of the human condition. It derives from Husserl's phenomenology, Heidegger's ontology of being-toward-death and the they-self, Buber's relational account of the self, and Sartre's analysis of freedom and responsibility, and it entered the profession through Binswanger, Boss, Yalom, and Frankl.

Its four propositions are that existence precedes essence, that the person is free and responsible, that the person is irreducibly alone, and that existence is ungrounded. Each of the four givens produces a characteristic anxiety — of mortality, of freedom and responsibility, of isolation, and of meaninglessness — and the clinical contribution of the theory is the account of defence as the adoption of a determining system: a structure of meaning that eliminates the anxiety of the givens by taking responsibility away.

Yalom's four elements — presence, empathy, authenticity, and emotional involvement — make the theory teachable, and the developmental modifications of these elements make it usable with young people. The developmental literature provides strong support for the *relevance* of the existential concerns in the second decade, and the meaning research provides substantial evidence for the association between meaning and adolescent mental health, while the evidence for existential therapy as a specific treatment remains limited.

The approach's greatest strength with young people is that it takes their existential questions seriously, and its greatest dangers are the premature confrontation of a given, the philosophical posture performed as technique, the aestheticisation of suffering, and the cultural specificity of the account of the person it takes as universal. The case of Theo illustrates the theory's power and its risks, and shows that the framework must be combined with a risk assessment, with developmental perspective, and with specific cognitive and behavioural work.

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 11.1 — The Givens with a Young Person (2 hours, groups of four)

Each group takes one of the four givens and works out three developmentally appropriate ways of entering the conversation with a young person of a specified age: one who would recognise the question, one who would engage with it obliquely, and one for whom it is premature. The groups then consider what the practitioner would say if the young person responded with a shrug, with irritation, and with a flood. The exercise builds the practical skill and it also exposes the difficulty, which is the point.

### Activity 11.2 — The Determining System Audit (90 minutes, individual, then pairs)

Take a young person you know — a client, a placement student, a family member, a friend — and identify the determining system in operation. Write it as a set of two or three beliefs, in the young person's own words if you can, that make a decision unnecessary. Then write the anxiety that the system is avoiding. Then write what it would cost the young person to relinquish it. The exercise is uncomfortable and revealing, and it develops the observation skill that the theory requires.

### Activity 11.3 — The Logotherapy Technique Under Scrutiny (60 minutes, groups of three)

Each group takes one logotherapy technique — paradoxical intention, the reflection technique, or the logotherapy interview — and constructs a scenario with a young person in which the technique would be helpful, one in which it would be neutral, and one in which it would be harmful. The third scenario is the requirement, and the exercise is designed to produce the caution that the technique requires.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the philosophy.** Read Buber (1957) first, because it is short and because it will make the clinical material intelligible. Then read the clinical accounts, and return to Heidegger and Sartre afterwards, if at all, with the clinical vocabulary in place.

**For the clinical application.** Read Yalom (1980) for the theory and the four elements, and Yalom (2008) for the craft. Yalom's second book is the one to read first, despite being the later, because it is the more honest about what the work actually consists of.

**For the developmental and empirical material.** Read the contemporary literature on existential therapy with young people, and then read the meaning-in-life research literature, which is substantial and considerably better evidenced than the therapy literature. Note the difference in evidential quality, and consider what follows from it for a service that must specify what it offers.

**For the critical reading.** Read the cross-cultural existential psychology literature, and the philosophical critiques of the free will proposition in the light of neuroscience. A student who has read only the clinical accounts will have a picture of the theory that its own literature would not support.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is yalom, May, Frankl, and the clinical use of existential confrontation. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is awareness, emotional experiencing, relational presence, agency, and the young person's own meanings. The mechanism to keep in view is how safety, attention, expression, and relational responsiveness allow experience to become available for reflection and choice. The characteristic professional risk is pressuring disclosure, treating emotional intensity as progress, or neglecting risk, family context, and practical action. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in individual sessions where trust, voice, and self-understanding are central, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Ines is fifteen and is seen in a school counselling service for low mood and withdrawal from friends. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Ines's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Ines explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on awareness, emotional experiencing, relational presence, agency, and the young person's own meanings.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Ines chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Ines has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Ines can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
