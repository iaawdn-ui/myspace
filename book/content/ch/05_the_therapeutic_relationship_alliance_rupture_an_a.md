# The Therapeutic Relationship: Alliance, Rupture, and Repair

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why the therapeutic relationship is regarded as a necessary condition of change and how this is compatible with a theory-specific account of technique.
- Distinguish the working alliance, the therapeutic alliance, the rapport, and the therapeutic bond, and describe the function each serves.
- Summarise the empirical findings on the relationship–outcome relationship, including its magnitude, its specificity, and its limits.
- Describe the four principal approaches to rupture and repair, and state the clinical implication of each.
- Apply the rupture-and-repair model to a case involving a young person's withdrawal, silence, or hostility.
- Describe what is known about the alliance with children and adolescents, and why it differs from the adult literature.
- Explain the ethical significance of the relationship, including the specific risks that attend work with minors.

## Chapter Overview

There is a common misreading of the finding that the therapeutic relationship predicts outcome. It is sometimes taken to mean that relationship is everything and technique is nothing, and that the theories surveyed in this book are therefore dispensable. Chapter 6 will take that misreading apart in detail. This chapter takes the finding at face value and draws the correct conclusion from it: the relationship is the medium through which every theory's technique operates, and a theory that does not specify the conditions of a working relationship has left a crucial part of its account of change unspecified.

The argument of the chapter has four parts. First, the therapeutic relationship is a necessary condition of change, and the reasons for this are not mysterious: a young person who does not trust the person they are speaking to will not say the things that need to be said, and no technique survives that failure. Second, the relationship is measurable, and a substantial research literature describes what a good alliance looks like, what predicts it, and what happens when it breaks down. Third, rupture and repair are ordinary and expectable features of the work rather than catastrophic failures, and there is now a well-developed clinical literature on how they are managed — one that differs sharply across theoretical orientations, and whose differences are clinically consequential. Fourth, the relationship with a young person is not the adult relationship, and the developmental adaptations that this requires are specific and teachable.

The chapter includes a substantial case illustration, because the alliance is the one element of counseling practice that every student, whatever their theoretical orientation, will have to get right.

## 1. The Relationship as the Medium of Therapy

### 1.1 The logical argument

Every therapy requires at least two things: a person who wants something to be different, and a set of activities directed at that change. The relationship between the practitioner and the client is the condition under which the second can operate on the first.

The argument is straightforward. A cognitive therapist's method requires the client to record thoughts, to examine them, to generate alternatives, to test predictions, and to report honestly on what happened when they tested them. A behavioural therapist's method requires the client to attempt feared situations and to report accurately on their anxiety. A family therapist's method requires the family to tolerate discomfort and to have the capacity to change an interaction in the room. In each case, the technique depends on information and on cooperation, and the availability of both is a function of the relationship.

This is why the relationship is best understood as a **necessary condition** rather than as a **sufficient cause**. A strong relationship does not by itself produce change; a young person can feel entirely safe with a practitioner and still not change. But the relationship is the condition under which the specific activities of therapy can take place, and this is a proposition about mechanism, not a claim about the irrelevance of theory.

### 1.2 What the relationship contains

The components of the therapeutic relationship are more specific than the phrase suggests, and specifying them is one of the principal purposes of this chapter. Four components are usually distinguished.

**The working alliance** is the collaborative, task-focused dimension of the relationship: the shared agreement about what the work is and what each party will do. It is the most directly relevant to young people, because a young person who does not know why they are in a room and what is supposed to happen will disengage regardless of how much they like the practitioner.

**The therapeutic alliance** is the broader relational bond: the shared trust, the shared belief that the work will be worth doing, and the mutual commitment to it. In the empirical literature, the therapeutic alliance is usually measured by a rating instrument — the Working Alliance Inventory and its revisions, the Session Rating Scale, the Client Outcome Questionnaire, the Therapy Alliance Scale, and the Helping Alliance Questionnaire — and it is this broader construct that correlates with outcome.

**Rapport** is the interpersonal comfort, mutual liking, and ease of interaction. It is the most superficial of the four, and the most important not to confuse with the others. Rapport is pleasant; the alliance is a working partnership; the bond is the affective commitment to the work. A practitioner can have excellent rapport and a poor working alliance, and this is a common configuration with adolescents, who may like a practitioner very much while agreeing to do nothing.

**The therapeutic bond** is the affective and emotional component: the sense of a genuine, caring, reliable connection. In work with young people this is the component most likely to be decisive, because a young person's willingness to disclose depends more on the perceived trustworthiness of the adult than on the perceived competence of the technique.

### 1.3 The relationship in theoretical terms

Each theory in this book specifies the relationship differently, and the specification is part of the theory rather than an addition to it.

The humanistic tradition makes the relationship the treatment. Rogers' hypothesis was that the conditions of growth are interpersonal conditions, and that the "actualising tendency" operates in the relationship or not at all. For a student of this tradition, the relationship is not a vehicle; it is the mechanism.

The cognitive tradition treats the relationship as a necessary condition for the collaborative examination of evidence, and it has been productive enough to generate a research literature on the alliance in cognitive therapy specifically, including the finding that the alliance mediates outcome in cognitive therapy as it does in other treatments.

The behavioural tradition treats the relationship as a behaviour itself, subject to the same contingencies as any other. Behaviour analysts have written a substantial literature on the therapist's behaviour as a reinforcer, on the "rapport" behaviours that increase the young person's verbalisation, and on the fact that the relationship is what makes the contingencies of therapy effective. The tradition's most bracing statement of this position is the claim that the therapeutic relationship is a behaviour, and should be treated with the same rigour as any other.

The systemic and relational traditions make the relationship a **parallel process**: whatever is happening in the relationship between the practitioner and the young person is a reflection of the relational patterns in the young person's wider life. A systemic practitioner working with an adolescent who is compliant with the practitioner and in constant conflict with the mother will read the compliance as data about the family system. The parallel process hypothesis is a powerful lens, and it is also easy to abuse, because it can be used to attribute to the young person a pathology of relationship that is in fact a reflection of the practitioner's own conduct.

The constructivist and narrative traditions make the relationship a **negotiated collaboration** in which both parties are understood to be shaping the account. The practitioner is not a blank screen and the client is not a recipient; the account of what is happening is a joint product, and the question of whose account prevails is itself a matter of power and of ethics.

## 2. The Empirical Literature on the Alliance and Outcome

### 2.1 Magnitude

The relationship between the quality of the therapeutic alliance and outcome is one of the most reliably replicated findings in psychotherapy research. Meta-analyses across adult populations report correlations in the range of 0.25 to 0.30, which is a moderate effect and is large by the standards of psychotherapy research. The finding holds across theoretical orientations, across treatment length, across diagnostic categories, and across practice settings. It holds in the adolescent literature as well, though the adolescent studies are fewer and more heterogeneous, and the magnitudes reported are generally somewhat lower.

### 2.2 Specificity

The finding is also not entirely nonspecific. The alliance-outcome relationship is stronger when the outcome is measured in the same domain as the therapeutic focus; a working alliance focused on the reduction of a specific symptom predicts that symptom's reduction more strongly than it predicts global functioning. And the alliance quality in the early sessions predicts later outcome better than the alliance quality in the middle or late sessions, which is the basis for the clinical recommendation to invest heavily in the first two or three sessions.

### 2.3 Directionality and cause

A finding of this robustness requires careful handling of the direction of causation. Three explanations are possible: that a good alliance causes better outcomes; that better outcomes cause a good alliance; or that a third variable produces both. The third explanation has the strongest empirical support. The factors that predict a good alliance are largely the same factors that predict a good outcome: the severity of the client's difficulties, the client's prior positive expectations, the therapist's empathic responding and experience, the match between the client and the therapist, the presence of a clear plan, and the client's overall social support. This does not make the alliance causally inert; it makes it a marker of a set of conditions including the therapist's skill, which does cause better outcomes.

### 2.4 The limits

Two limits should be stated. The alliance predicts variance in outcome, and the amount of variance is moderate; it does not predict most of the variance, and a practitioner cannot rely on the relationship alone. And the alliance is not a substitute for technique: in treatments for specific conditions, the specific procedures account for outcomes over and above the alliance, and the evidence from component studies indicates that removing a component while holding the relationship constant reduces outcomes for that component's target.

## 3. The Developmental Specificity of the Alliance with Young People

The adult alliance literature cannot simply be applied to a fourteen-year-old, and the reasons are worth making explicit.

**The developmental constraint on verbal processing.** A young person at the developmental stage at which she is will have a limited capacity for abstract, verbally mediated emotional processing, a limited capacity for metacognitive reflection, and a limited repertoire for articulating what she feels. The alliance researcher's instruments were designed for adults and assume a level of introspective access that a twelve-year-old does not have. This is a measurement problem and it is also a clinical one: the young person's inability to articulate her experience does not indicate a poor alliance.

**The authority asymmetry.** The adult client and the adult therapist are, in most respects, socially equal. The young person and the adult practitioner are not. The alliance with a young person is formed under conditions of considerable institutional power imbalance, which affects what can be said, what must be said, and what is safe to say. The developmental literature on the adolescent's increasing autonomy and the parent's corresponding withdrawal of authority is directly relevant.

**The time horizon.** The adult client is usually able to sustain a therapeutic alliance through a period of discomfort because the abstract possibility of benefit is available to her. A young person's temporal perspective is often shorter and more concrete, and a young person who cannot see a point to a process that is currently painful will disengage. The clinical implication is the importance of making the goal concrete, near, and in the young person's own terms.

**The developmental instability of the "self" the alliance is with.** The young person the practitioner met in the first session is not the same person who attends the eighth; adolescent identity is under active construction, and the alliance may have to be renegotiated in the light of developments outside the sessions. Chapters 46 and 9 address this.

**The third-party presence.** A young person's counselling usually involves adults who are not the client: parents, teachers, school staff, guardians, sometimes other professionals. These figures are present in the alliance as a matter of course, and their presence affects what can be said and what can be trusted. The systemic traditions theorise this explicitly; the individual traditions usually treat it as an external complication.

**The corroboration of the relationship by the environment.** For a young person, the most important evidence that the practitioner can be trusted is that the practitioner's behaviour is consistent with what the young person has been told by the adult in their life. If a practitioner tells a young person that what they say will be kept confidential and then a parent is telephoned without explanation, the alliance is not merely damaged — the young person learns something about the reliability of adults that generalises.

## 4. Rupture: When the Relationship Breaks Down

### 4.1 Defining rupture and repair

A **rupture** is a breakdown in the working alliance: a moment in which the collaborative frame of the therapy is interrupted. It may be overt — the young person refuses to speak, attacks the practitioner, or demands to leave — or it may be covert, expressed through lateness, minimal responses, or the withdrawal of a previously engaged participant. A **repair** is the process by which the collaborative frame is restored, whether by the practitioner alone, by the young person, or jointly.

The clinical significance of rupture is that it is a **reliable indicator of the state of the work**, and its content is **the most direct available information about the young person's experience of the therapy**. A young person who withdraws is telling the practitioner something about the relationship, and the more rapidly the practitioner can identify the rupture and the more accurately the practitioner can interpret it, the less damage is done and the more useful the information becomes.

### 4.2 Four models of rupture

**The psychodynamic model.** Rupture is a manifestation of the transference — the displacement onto the practitioner of the expectations, feelings, and unresolved relationships from the past. The rupture is the enactment of an old relationship pattern in the present, and its interpretation is the heart of the intervention. A young person who becomes angry with the practitioner in the seventh session is asked what it is about the practitioner that makes the anger possible, and the answer, if the work goes well, reaches the earlier relationships in which the young person's anger and the adult's response to it were formed. The psychodynamic model's risk is that it can be used to explain away everything, and a practitioner who interprets every behaviour as transference will eventually find that the young person has stopped being heard.

**The behavioural model.** Rupture is a behaviour to be understood functionally: what does the young person get from it, and what has it been reinforced for? The behavioural practitioner's first move is not to interpret but to change the contingencies: what happened immediately before the young person went silent, and what happened immediately after? Rupture in this model is often a behaviour that has been inadvertently reinforced — for example, a young person's refusal to speak that has been followed by extended attempts to persuade, which is itself aversive and which has taught the young person that the strategy works. The behavioural model's risk is that it misses the meaning of the rupture, and a young person whose withdrawal is a bid for a different kind of attention will not be helped by a contingency analysis alone.

**The humanistic and person-centred model.** Rupture is a failure of the relational conditions, and its repair is the restoration of those conditions: a return to accurate empathic understanding, to unconditional positive regard, to congruence. Ruth Be warned in 1963 that the counsellor is likely to become less accepting when the client becomes more difficult, and this "the counsellor has failed" model is both the most humane and the most demanding of the models, because it requires the practitioner to accept that the rupture is partly a failure of the practitioner's own conduct. The model's risk is that it can be used to absorb responsibility that the practitioner should be sharing with the young person, and that it can generate a great deal of self-reflection in place of actual change.

**The integrative and process model.** Rupture is a process event to be tracked, understood, and worked with collaboratively, drawing on all three of the above. The most useful formulation is that ruptures are inevitable, that their occurrence is a sign that the young person is engaged enough for the relationship to matter, that their content is the most valuable data available, and that the repair itself is often the most potent therapeutic event in the episode. The risk of the integrative approach is its very flexibility, which can become a way of avoiding commitment to any one understanding of what happened.

### 4.3 The mechanism of rupture

Ruptures have a common structure, which is worth teaching explicitly because it makes them recognisable in the moment. A rupture typically involves: a **disagreement** about what is happening or what should be done; a **disengagement** in which one party withdraws from the collaborative work; and a **displacement**, in which the affect belonging to the current relationship is transferred to a less threatening target — a symptom, a homework task, a complaint about the school, a demand for a different appointment time, or an attack on a third party.

The most useful single clinical rule in this area is the one that many trainees learn the hard way: **the displacement is the rupture**. When a young person begins talking angrily about their school in the middle of a session about their relationship with their mother, or becomes suddenly concerned about the format of the homework, the practitioner should treat the sudden shift as information about the relationship rather than as a topic. Asking, gently and with genuine curiosity, "it seems like something has just changed for you — what has happened in here?" is frequently more productive than following the topic.

## 5. Repair: Restoring the Collaborative Frame

Repair is a skill, and it is a learnable one. The components of an effective repair are as follows.

**Notice.** The practitioner must recognise the rupture, which is harder than it sounds, because ruptures are frequently covert and because the practitioner's own defensiveness is activated by them. The most common missed ruptures are the young person's boredom, the young person's apparent agreement that is in fact compliance, and the young person's deferential silence.

**Name it.** The rupture is named, in plain language, without blame, and with the invitation of the young person to correct the practitioner's account. "I think something has just happened between us, and I'm not sure what it was. Have I got something wrong?" The naming must be offered, not imposed. A practitioner who declares "we are in a rupture" to an adolescent is performing a technical concept rather than making a human observation, and it will be received as such.

**Take responsibility for the practitioner's part.** This is the most counter-intuitive and most effective element. A rupture in which the practitioner acknowledges some contribution to the difficulty, however small, is substantially more likely to be repaired than a rupture in which the young person is invited to explain what is wrong with them. "I pushed you on that question and I think I pushed too hard" is a sentence with a very high yield.

**Explore the meaning.** Once the frame is restored, the content of the rupture is explored, and this is the moment at which the most valuable material in a case often appears.

**Return to the task.** Repair is completed by returning to the work, and the return should be explicit: "where were we?"

### 5.1 The failed repair

Not every rupture is repaired, and the practitioner must know what failure looks like in order to respond to it. Signs of a failed repair include: the young person leaves the session in a worse state than they arrived; the topic is avoided for several sessions; the young person begins cancelling; the rupture is never named; or the practitioner returns to the same behaviour that produced the rupture. The response to a failed repair is not more technique. It is a change in the plan, a consultation, and sometimes a change of therapist — which is not a failure of the young person or of the practitioner but an honest recognition that the fit is wrong, and one that a young person is entitled to.

## 6. Consent, Collaboration, and Feedback

Three further elements of the relationship require attention, all of them contemporary developments in the field and all of them with particular force in work with young people.

**Informed consent as a relational process.** Chapter 54 treats the ethical and legal architecture of consent and Appendix A provides the model documents. The point relevant here is that consent is not a form signed at the beginning; it is an ongoing, revisable, and developmentally appropriate process in which the young person's understanding and agreement are actively sought and periodically revisited. A young person who signs an assent form without understanding what will happen has not consented to anything, and the practitioner who treats the signature as the end of the matter has converted a form into a substitute for a conversation.

**Collaborative goal setting.** The literature is clear that the alliance is stronger and outcomes better when goals are collaboratively constructed rather than prescribed. This is more difficult with young people, who may not have the language to state a goal, and it is more necessary, because a goal the young person does not own will not be worked on. Practical techniques include scaling, the miracle question, the "what would you like to be different" question, and the deliberate construction of goals in the young person's own words. Chapter 35 develops the solution-focused approach and Chapter 33 the work with parents and adolescents.

**Routine outcome and alliance feedback.** The contemporary standard is that practitioners should routinely ask about and provide feedback on progress, and that the feedback should be structured rather than informal. The Session Rating Scale and the Working Alliance Inventory in their short forms can be administered in two minutes at the end of a session and reviewed every four to six sessions. This is not a bureaucratic imposition; it is a way of making the rupture visible before it becomes a dropout.
