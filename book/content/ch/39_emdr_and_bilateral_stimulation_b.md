
## 7. EMDR with Children and Adolescents

### 7.1 The developmental modifications in practice

The following are the modifications that a practitioner working with adolescents should know, and they are more specific than the general list in Section 5.

**The parent is the co-therapist.** The parent is in the room, and the parent's job is to be a secure base: to sit quietly, to be emotionally available, to model calm, to not ask questions about the memory, and to hold the young person's hand if the young person wants that. The clinician coaches the parent before the session and gives the parent a specific role, and the parent who has not been coached will talk, and the talking will derail the procedure.

**The safe place is a concrete sensory place, not a fantasy.** A fifteen-year-old's "safe place" is a specific and sensory description: the beach at a particular time, a specific room, a specific piece of music. The clinician elicits it in detail and the young person draws it, and the drawing is used as the anchor throughout the session.

**The "external focus of attention" is taught as a body sensation.** The young person is taught to locate a physical sensation in the body — a point on the sternum, the top of the head, the hands — and to attend to it, and the sensation is the anchor when the memory's affect rises. With young people this works better than the cognitive "external focus" that the adult protocol specifies, and the paediatric protocol says as much.

**The clinician intervenes more often.** The adult protocol is relatively permissive about the young person's distress during the reprocessing. The paediatric protocol is not: the clinician monitors the affect continuously and intervenes with a resourcing intervention when the young person's affect exceeds a manageable level, and the interventions are frequent.

**The sets are short and the young person controls the stopping.** A set is a few minutes rather than a full "round", and the young person is told explicitly at the start that they can stop at any time and that stopping is not a failure.

**The installation is done with the young person's own words.** The new associations that emerge during the reprocessing are written down, in the young person's words, and the installation goes through them one at a time, and the young person approves each one.

### 7.2 The specific applications with young people

**Single-incident accidents.** The best-evidenced application in the paediatric population, and the protocol maps almost perfectly onto the presentation, and the young person is usually stabilised and in a school and can be treated in a small number of sessions.

**School and community trauma.** The evidence from the school-based and the community-based programmes is good, and the group delivery of the first-aid version of the protocol has a substantial evidence base.

**Medical procedures.** The protocol was developed partly for young people undergoing painful procedures, and the modification for this application is the "prescripted image" technique, in which the young person is given a prepared image to hold while the painful stimulus is applied, and the practice is now well established in paediatric pain services.

**Sexual abuse.** The evidence is good for the protocol with a discrete abuse event, and the evidence is much weaker for the developmental pattern, and the practitioner must be careful about the demands the protocol makes on a young person who is still in the situation.

**Grief.** The protocol has a well-established application to the traumatic grief of sudden loss, and the paediatric grief application is described in the literature.

**Specific phobias and the anxiety disorders.** The protocol is effective, and in this population the question of whether a trauma-focused protocol is indicated at all, as opposed to the graded exposure of Chapter 25, is a live one.

### 7.3 The limits with young people

**The developmental trauma presentation is a poor fit.** The standard target of the protocol — a discrete, bounded, sensory memory — is not available in the developmental presentation, and the clinician who applies the protocol to a young person with a chronic relational trauma will either find that there is no target or will find that the target is a composite that the young person cannot hold.

**The protocol requires a capacity to stay with an affect.** A young person who dissociates in session cannot do the reprocessing, and the clinician should be prepared for the possibility that the dissociative response is a poor sign for the suitability of the protocol and not only a technical difficulty.

**The protocol's structure can pressure the young person.** The eight-phase structure, the SUD rating, the repeated questions about the memory — a young person who has been assessed repeatedly and who is being asked to rate their distress on a scale can experience the procedure as another demand to perform.

**The parent in the room is a double-edged intervention.** The parent is the secure base, and the parent is also the person who may have been the source of the trauma, and the protocol requires the clinician to have established which before the session, and the clinical judgement required is significant.

## 8. Case Illustration: The Boy Who Kept Checking the Rear Window

**Presentation.** Ruben is twelve. Four months ago he was in a car that was hit by a van, and his mother was in the back seat with him. His mother was seriously injured and Ruben was not, physically. Since the accident, Ruben will not sit in the back of any car, he checks the rear window constantly, he has not slept in his own bed, he wets the bed twice a week, and his school attendance has dropped by half. The referral is from the paediatrician, who is asking for trauma-focused treatment.

**The history and the stabilisation.** The practitioner conducts the full history. The important findings are: no previous mental health history; no current risk; a stable and safe home; the mother is recovering well and is clinically stable; and the family are cooperative. The practitioner also takes the school and the paediatrician's details, and establishes that there is no ongoing threat.

The practitioner also establishes what the mother knows and does not know, and notes that Ruben's father, who separated from the mother before the accident and who is estranged, has not been informed. The practitioner discusses this with the mother and with the school and makes a safeguarding assessment, and the finding is that the risk to Ruben from his father is low and the question is whether informing him is in Ruben's interests, and the practitioner takes Ruben's view on this in the second session and records it.

**The preparation.** Over three sessions, the preparation is done. Ruben chooses the butterfly method, which he says he can do "without anyone noticing", and this is a good choice because it is discreet and because it gives him control. The safe place is established in detail: his grandfather's boat shed at the coast, the smell of the oil, the sound of the water, the blue chair. He draws it. The external focus is located: he and the practitioner find a point on his sternum that he can attend to, and the practitioner teaches him to move his attention there when the distress rises, and the teaching is repeated in each session.

The body work is done in the preparation phase, and it is given substantial time, because the practitioner has noticed that Ruben's first sign of distress is a change in his breathing, and the practitioner wants him to be able to notice it before the distress is visible.

The mother is coached: she sits on the boy's left, she does not speak during the processing, she breathes normally, she does not ask him what he is thinking, and if he reaches for her hand she holds it and says nothing.

**The assessment.** The chosen target is the moment of the impact, and Ruben describes it: the van came from the side, the noise, his mother's voice, and the moment after. The SUD is nine. The body sensations are in his jaw and his chest. The image is vivid. The practitioner charts all three and notes that the chart is going to be the main clinical document in this treatment.

**The reprocessing.** There are three sets over two sessions.

In the first set, the boy reports the image, the noise, his mother's voice, and then reports that the van "didn't even slow down" and that this is new information — that he had not remembered the detail that the van did not slow down, and that it had been going fast, and that it had been going fast because of the roadworks, and that the roadworks are always there, and the practitioner notes that this is the associative processing, that the memory is being linked to the ordinary world of the road and the roadworks and the ordinary traffic.

In the second set, the boy reports the moment after the impact, and this is where the affect rises sharply, and the boy's breathing changes, and the practitioner intervenes with a resourcing intervention and takes him to the boat shed, and the boy's breathing settles, and the practitioner says: "You can come back to it, or we can stop here today." The boy says: "I want to come back."

In the third set, the boy reports something that surprises the practitioner: "I keep looking at the back window because I keep thinking Mum's going to say she's hurt again." The practitioner says nothing and lets him continue, and the boy goes on: "She's not, is she. She's at home. She's walking. I saw her walking yesterday and I didn't even see the bandage." And then he says: "I think I keep looking in case she needs me to look after her."

The practitioner recognises this as the essential material, and it is the material that the reprocessing exists to reach: the boy's checking is not a fear of a repeated accident; it is a ten-year-old's vigilance about his mother's care, and the accident has given him a reason to perform it.

**The installation.** The new associations are installed one at a time, with the bilateral stimulation. The practitioner asks the boy what he would like to remember about the accident, and the boy says: "That I looked after her, and that she was all right, and that I can stop." The practitioner installs this. The body scan is done, and the boy's remaining sensation is in his jaw, and the body scan reduces it, and the boy reports that it is "like a tooth that isn't there any more".

**The completion and the review.** The SUD is two. The body sensations are zero. The vividness is three. The boy says: "It was a bad accident. It just isn't happening now."

**The follow-up.** The practitioner asks what the boy thinks will be different at home. He says: "I think I'll sleep in my bed." The practitioner does not tell him that he will; she asks him what he would need to do, and he says: "Nothing. I think I'll just do it." The practitioner says: "Let's see."

**The outcome.** At the one-month review the boy is sleeping in his bed, has no bedwetting, has returned to school, and has not checked the rear window in the car in the last two weeks. The mother reports that he has asked to sit in the back again, and that he has chosen the music. The practitioner is clear that this is a good outcome and is equally clear that the work was the work of the reprocessing and the installation and not the reassurance.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the protocol and its specific pedagogic point: the target memory was the accident, and the material that mattered was the meaning attached to it, and that meaning — that the checking was a way of looking after his mother — was only accessible through the associative processing and not through the history, and would not have been reached by any of the cognitive procedures in Chapter 16. It also illustrates the paediatric discipline: the parent in the room and coached, the short sets, the resourcing intervention and the offer to stop, the chart as the document, and the installation as the phase that makes the change durable.

## 9. Evidence and Research Support

### 9.1 The status of the evidence

**The efficacy of EMDR is well established.** The method has been subject to more controlled trials than almost any other psychological treatment, and the meta-analyses report large and consistent effects for the PTSD and the phobias in adults. It is recommended as a first-line treatment in the major international guidelines.

**The paediatric evidence is good and growing.** The randomised controlled trials with children and adolescents show substantial effects on symptom measures and on the measures of functional impairment, and the effect sizes are large.

**The comparison with the trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy is favourable.** The network meta-analyses of the paediatric trauma treatments have placed EMDR among the first-line recommendations, generally comparable with or slightly ahead of the trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, and ahead of the treatments that are not trauma-focused.

**The evidence on the mechanisms is genuinely inconclusive**, and the field's position is that the mechanism remains unknown and that the stimulation functions as a device for the associative processing. The studies that found effects with several stimulation methods, and with a non-bilateral control in some conditions, are the most informative.

**The evidence for the developmental trauma application is weak**, and the reasons are methodological and are described in Section 7.3.

### 9.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The samples are dominated by adults with single-incident trauma**, and the generalisation to children with developmental trauma is not supported.

**The measurement of the outcome within the protocol is subjective** and the SUD is a single-item measure that is vulnerable to expectancy effects, and the between-session measures are the ones on which the claims of the method have to stand.

**The fidelity measurement has been difficult**, and the protocol's eight phases and its specific instructions are detailed enough that delivery varies.

**The trials are frequently conducted by EMDR researchers**, and the allegiance effects have been examined in this literature and are substantial, and the field has itself commissioned critical reviews.

**The long-term follow-up is limited**, and the question of what happens to the young person in the years after the treatment, and whether the memory returns in a form, is under-researched.

**The comparison with the alternative trauma-focused treatments is relatively recent**, and it will take more and larger trials to establish the comparative position with confidence.

## 10. Strengths and Limitations of EMDR

### 10.1 Strengths

**It is brief.** A young person with a single-incident trauma may be treated in a small number of sessions, and brevity is a genuine clinical advantage.

**It is a first-line recommended treatment**, and the practitioner will be able to access it in the referral pathways of the major systems.

**The treatment does not require the young person to narrate the memory in detail**, which is one of its most important practical advantages in the paediatric population and one of the reasons it is well suited to young people who cannot or will not tell the story.

**It targets the autonomic component**, and the body work is integrated into the protocol rather than added to it.

**The installation phase is a specific and valuable technique** that is often omitted and that is one of the more transferable elements in the book.

**The chart is a good clinical document** and it makes the progress of the treatment visible to the young person, to the parents, and to the referrer.

### 10.2 Limitations

**The mechanism is unknown**, and a practitioner should be honest with a referring agent about this, while also being able to point to the efficacy evidence.

**The protocol's structure can pressure the young person**, and the repeated ratings and the structured questions are a poor fit with some young people.

**The developmental trauma is a poor fit**, and the protocol should not be applied to it by analogy.

**The training and the certification requirements are significant**, and the protocol should not be delivered by an untrained practitioner.

**The parent-in-the-room requirement is a genuine constraint**, and in some cases there is no safe parent, and the protocol's paediatric form then cannot be used in its standard form.

## 11. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 11.1 Culture, memory, and the treatment of the memory

**The meaning of a memory is culturally constituted.** The same event means different things in different contexts, and the processing of a memory is a process of linking it to a frame, and the frame the practitioner offers is culturally specific. A practitioner who assumes a universal phenomenology of traumatic memory will misdescribe the experience of some young people.

**Some events are not traumatic for the young person who experienced them**, and the decision to treat a memory as traumatic should be made on the basis of the young person's own account of it rather than on the basis of the event type. A young person who was in the accident and who says she was fine may be minimising, or may be telling the truth, and the assessment should be able to tell the difference.

**The cultural resources for processing a trauma are frequently available and are frequently ignored.** Many communities have established practices for the marking of loss and for the processing of frightening events, and the individual protocol can be respectful of these or can displace them, and the practitioner should ask.

**The language of the procedure is specific.** The protocol is translated into a great many languages, and the translation of the SUD scale, of the "safe place", and of the instructions for the bilateral stimulation is not a simple matter, and a practitioner working across languages should check the validated version.

### 11.2 Ethical considerations

**Informed consent with a developmental understanding.** The young person must consent in a form appropriate to their development, and the parent's consent and the young person's assent are both required, and the young person's ongoing consent during the procedure is monitored.

**The right to stop.** The young person must be told, at the start and repeatedly, that they can stop, and the protocol's design must make stopping easy and must not make it a failure.

**The prohibition of the compulsory retrieval of a memory.** The procedure works with the memories that the young person brings, and a practitioner who pressures a young person to retrieve a specific memory has departed from the protocol and has introduced a significant risk.

**The boundaries of the memory work.** The protocol is for the processing of a specific memory, and it is not an invitation to explore the whole history, and the practitioner who uses the reprocessing as a route into general history has left the evidence base.

**The confidentiality of the memory.** The young person must know, before the session, who will hear what is said, and the chart and the recording of the SUD are records of a young person's distress and are sensitive documents.

**The relationship to the safeguarding framework.** The disclosure of abuse within the protocol is a disclosure to a third party, and the practitioner must know the local reporting obligations, and must handle the situation with a specific plan agreed in advance.

**The prohibition on the use of the procedure with the child in a state of acute crisis.** The history and the stabilisation phases of Chapter 38 are the prerequisites, and a practitioner who proceeds to the reprocessing with an unstabled young person is doing harm.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Eye movement desensitisation (EMD)** — The original name of Shapiro's procedure, in which a distressing memory was held in focus while the eyes moved horizontally.
- **EMDR** — Eye movement desensitisation and processing, the current name of the eight-phase protocol.
- **Adaptive information processing model** — The model in which the pathology lies in the failure to complete the processing of a memory, and the treatment in the completion of that processing.
- **Unprocessed memory** — A memory stored in a form that remains vivid, is experienced in the present tense, retains the original autonomic response, and is disconnected from the rest of the autobiographical narrative.
- **The link** — The missing connection between the unprocessed memory and the general network of autobiographical knowledge, and the element whose restoration the treatment produces.
- **Provocative technique** — The contemporary understanding of the bilateral stimulation, as a device for eliciting and sustaining the associative processing rather than as a treatment in its own right.
- **The eight phases** — History and stabilisation, preparation, assessment, reprocessing, installation, body scan, completion, and review.
- **Subjective units of disturbance (SUD)** — The young person's own rating of the distress associated with the memory, and the primary outcome measure within the protocol.
- **Body sensations** — The physical sensations associated with the memory, charted alongside the SUD and the vividness.
- **Vividness** — The rating of how vivid the image of the memory is.
- **The chart** — The clinical document recording the SUD, the body sensations, and the vividness across the phases and the sessions.
- **Bilateral stimulation** — The horizontal eye movements, the alternating auditory tones, the alternating tactile taps, and the butterfly variant.
- **The butterfly method** — The variant in which the young person taps their own thighs alternately while tracking the moving hand, used with young children and with those who need discretion.
- **Installation** — The phase in which the new associations are explicitly established, one at a time, with the bilateral stimulation; frequently the most neglected phase.
- **Body scan** — The bilateral stimulation period in which the young person attends to the body and reports any remaining sensations, completing the autonomic component.
- **The internal and the external focus of attention** — The cognitive anchors used during the procedure, which in the paediatric protocol are taught as a body sensation.
- **The safe place** — A concrete, sensory, personally meaningful resource, elicited in detail and used as the anchor throughout the session.
- **Resourcing intervention** — The clinician's intervention that takes the young person back to the safe place and the positive resource when the affect exceeds a manageable level.
- **The set** — A short period of reprocessing, in the paediatric protocol lasting a few minutes, with the young person controlling the stopping.
- **The parent as co-therapist** — The coached role of the safe caregiver in the paediatric protocol: to sit quietly, to model calm, to be available, and not to ask questions.
- **Prescripted image** — The technique in which a prepared image is held during a painful procedure, developed from the EMDR principles in paediatric pain services.
- **The developmental trauma mismatch** — The problem of applying a protocol designed for a discrete, bounded memory to a young person whose trauma is a chronic relational pattern.
- **The single-event and the developmental distinction** — The distinction on which the strength of the paediatric evidence depends.
- **The biographical gap** — The separation of the young person from the event that is the target of the reprocessing, in which the affective component remains unprocessed while the cognitive component is accessible.
:::

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 39.1 — The Four Phases Audit (75 minutes, individual)

Take a young person from placement with a history of a frightening or frightening-adjacent event and write the eight phases as they would apply, with a specific plan for each. Then write the specific criterion you would use to decide whether to proceed to the reprocessing at all. Then write what you would do if, at the end of the preparation, you concluded that the young person was not stabilised enough. The third step is the one that the chapter is really about, and most students find it difficult to write.

### Activity 39.2 — The Installation Exercise (60 minutes, individual)

Take a case and write the installation as it would actually be spoken, in the young person's own words, with each new association given separately and with the bilateral stimulation noted between each. Then write the version that omits the installation, and compare. The comparison is usually striking, and it is the clearest demonstration in the book of why a specific technical detail matters.

### Activity 39.3 — The Mechanism Discussion (60 minutes, small group)

In groups of four, take the question "why does this work?" and produce four different answers: the ocular motor account, the attentional and associational account, the working memory account, and the "we do not know" answer. Then write the clinical decision that each answer would lead to. The last step is the important one, because it is the argument for why a practitioner can deliver the treatment competently without settling the mechanism, and the groups usually find that the clinical decisions are the same.

### Activity 39.4 — The Paediatric Suitability Screen (60 minutes, individual)

Write a suitability screen for the paediatric EMDR protocol, with a criterion for each of: the presence of a discrete, bounded target memory; the stability of the young person; the safety of the caregiver; the capacity to stay with the affect; the capacity to report the SUD; and the absence of ongoing threat. Then apply the screen to two cases from placement, one of which will fail it. The exercise teaches the selection decision, which is the decision with the largest clinical consequences in the whole protocol.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the protocol.** Read Shapiro (2017) for the contemporary text, and read the EMDR International Association's practice guidelines and the child and adolescent protocol. The guidelines are the clinical standard and they are short.

**For the model.** Read Brown (2013) and Shapiro and Luycky (2016) for the adaptive information processing model and the mechanism review.

**For the paediatric evidence.** Read the paediatric systematic reviews and the network meta-analysis of the paediatric trauma treatments, and read Bergmann's critical reviews. The critical reviews are essential and they are of a high quality.

**For the mechanism.** Read the comparative studies of the stimulation methods, which are the most interesting empirical literature in the field, and Stickgold's neurocognitive account.

**For the practice.** Read the paediatric protocol, and read the case literature on the use of EMDR in schools, in community settings, and in paediatric pain services, which are the applications most likely to be relevant to a practitioner in this programme.
