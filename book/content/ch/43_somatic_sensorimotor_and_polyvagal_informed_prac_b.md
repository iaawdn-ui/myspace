
## 7. Core Practices

### 7.1 Orienting

Orienting uses sight, hearing, and proprioception to establish present safety. The young person slowly looks around, names colours or objects, notices support from the chair and floor, and identifies exits without rehearsing escape. The exercise must be adapted where the current environment is genuinely unsafe; pretending safety is not treatment.

### 7.2 Grounding

Grounding anchors attention in present sensory information: feet on the floor, back against a chair, holding a textured object, listening for near and far sounds, splashing cold water, or naming five observable details. Grounding is not a command to “calm down”. It is an invitation to collect evidence of here and now.

### 7.3 Breath

Slow breathing with a comfortable extended exhale can reduce arousal for some people. Breath work can also provoke panic, traumatic memories, dizziness, or a sense of control. Alternatives include breathing through movement, humming, singing, walking rhythm, or simply noticing natural breath. No adolescent should be forced to close their eyes or breathe in a prescribed pattern.

### 7.4 Movement and posture

Small experiments are preferable to dramatic release. Press feet into the floor. Push palms together. Stand and feel balance. Uncurl slowly from a collapsed posture. Walk the corridor. Shake hands and shoulders. Notice whether energy, breath, or emotion changes. The practitioner asks, “What is different?” rather than announcing what should be felt.

### 7.5 Boundaries in the body

Boundary work may involve noticing comfortable distance, practising saying “stop”, imagining a protective space, choosing where to sit, or using gesture to mark “enough”. For adolescents whose boundaries have been violated, bodily choice is therapeutic content, not a preliminary courtesy.

### 7.6 Tracking sensation and impulse

The practitioner helps sequence experience: sensation, emotion, impulse, thought, action. “My chest tightens; I feel trapped; I want to run; I think she'll shout; I leave.” Once sequence is visible, intervention points multiply. The young person might move, ask for a pause, clarify the other's intention, or choose a safer exit—not merely suppress leaving.

## 8. Somatic Work with Adolescents

Adolescents may find body-focused work embarrassing, especially amid puberty, body image concerns, disability, chronic illness, gender dysphoria, cultural modesty, or previous medical trauma. Language should be neutral and permission-based. Mirrors, touch, close physical proximity, and comments about appearance are avoided unless clinically essential, separately justified, consented, and within professional scope.

Sport, dance, martial arts, drama, music, outdoor activity, and occupational therapy may provide regulating experiences, but adults should not label every enjoyable activity “therapy” or remove its intrinsic value by over-clinicalising it. Sometimes the best somatic intervention is helping a young person safely return to football, swimming, drumming, or walking a dog.

Sleep is a somatic intervention. So are regular meals, treatment of pain, reduced late-night caffeine and energy drinks, management of sensory overload, and a school timetable that does not demand constant vigilance. A counsellor who ignores these basics while teaching sophisticated nervous-system language has reversed the clinical order.

## 9. Case Illustration: The Student Who Went Blank

**Presentation.** Tomas, fourteen, is repeatedly sanctioned for ignoring teachers. During conflict he stares, stops answering, and later cannot recall instructions. Staff interpret this as selective defiance. At home he becomes loud and restless after school, then sleeps for hours.

**Assessment.** The counsellor notices that when a male teacher raises his voice in a role-played example, Tomas' shoulders rise, breathing stops briefly, gaze fixes, and his hands become still. Tomas reports “everything goes fuzzy”. He denies choosing not to answer: “I can hear, but words don't come.” There is a history of witnessing severe domestic conflict before age nine. Medical screening and hearing assessment are arranged to avoid assuming a trauma explanation.

**Formulation.** Tomas oscillates between mobilisation and shutdown. Raised voices and public correction rapidly narrow his window. Stillness is a protective hypoarousal response, not evidence of calm. After school, accumulated arousal discharges through restlessness, followed by exhaustion.

**Intervention.** Work begins without trauma narration. Tomas maps early signs: ears “buzz”, jaw tightens, thoughts slow. He chooses signals with teachers: a break card, a seat near the door, and permission to stand at the back for two minutes rather than leaving unsupervised. Staff are coached—through proper consent and school consultation—to lower voice volume, give one instruction at a time, and check understanding after he has settled.

In counselling, Tomas practises orienting with eyes open, pressing feet into the floor, slow walking, and extending exhale only when it feels comfortable. He dislikes body scans because attention to his chest increases panic; that preference is respected. He learns to say, “I need a minute; I'm not refusing.” Teachers learn to hear that sentence as the beginning of regulation, not a challenge.

His mother is helped to create an after-school decompression routine: food, thirty minutes without questioning, physical activity, then homework in two short blocks. The counsellor does not ask Tomas to recount violent incidents. Later, when stability improves, he chooses to discuss one memory in a trauma-focused treatment with a trained clinician.

**Outcome.** Sanctions decrease. Tomas still becomes blank occasionally, but he recognises early signs and uses the break card before shutdown. The central change is interpretive as well as physiological: adults stop treating stillness as proof of contempt, and Tomas stops treating his body as evidence that he is “broken”.

## 10. Evidence and Critical Evaluation

Evidence for body-oriented trauma therapies is promising but uneven. Sensorimotor psychotherapy, somatic experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, breath and movement interventions, and related approaches have supportive studies, but many trials are small, heterogeneous, and difficult to blind. Outcomes may reflect therapeutic relationship, expectancy, exercise, sleep improvement, and general support as well as specific mechanisms.

Polyvagal theory has been clinically influential, especially in translating safety and co-regulation into practice. However, scholars dispute aspects of its evolutionary sequence, vagal measurement, and interpretation of respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Ethical teaching therefore distinguishes between: well-supported observations that safety cues and relationships affect arousal; plausible clinical heuristics about states; and contested neuroanatomical claims. Students should be suspicious of any programme that claims a proprietary exercise can diagnose trauma from heart-rate patterns or “reset” a nervous system in one session.

## 11. Ethics, Touch, and Safeguarding

Touch requires exceptional care with minors. Default practice in counselling should be no therapeutic touch unless there is a clear rationale, professional training, organisational policy, informed parental consent where required, the young person's ongoing assent, a chaperone or visibility where appropriate, and immediate respect for withdrawal. Many somatic interventions require no touch at all.

The practitioner must avoid suggesting that bodily responses prove a particular history. A startle response does not prove abuse; pelvic pain does not prove sexual trauma; dissociation has multiple causes. Somatic work must not become a memory-recovery technique. If disclosures occur, safeguarding procedures govern the response.

Cultural practices around modesty, gender, disability, movement, eye contact, and bodily display vary. Ask what is acceptable. Offer alternatives. Never require removing clothing, being filmed, closing eyes, or physical contact as proof of engagement.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Somatic** — Relating to lived bodily experience.
- **Sensorimotor** — Linking sensation, posture, movement, and action impulses.
- **Co-regulation** — Regulation supported through a safe relationship.
- **Titration** — Working with activation in small, manageable doses.
- **Pendulation** — Moving attention between distress and resource to build flexibility.
- **Window of tolerance** — The arousal range in which processing and learning remain possible.
- **Orienting** — Using sensory attention to establish present-time safety.
- **Procedural learning** — Bodily patterns learned through repetition rather than conscious declaration.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Somatic and sensorimotor practice teaches counsellors to notice the body without worshipping or over-interpreting it. Breath, posture, sensation, movement, sleep, pain, and relational safety provide information and points of intervention. Used humbly, these approaches help young people recognise state changes, widen their window of tolerance, and recover choice before, during, and after talk-based work.

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For foundations.** Begin with Ogden's sensorimotor work and Dana's clinically accessible polyvagal translation.

**For critique.** Read scientific critiques of polyvagal theory alongside Porges, so that clinical usefulness does not become uncritical certainty.

**For adolescents.** Combine somatic reading with developmental neuroscience, sleep medicine, pain assessment, and trauma-informed school practice.

**For ethics.** Study professional guidance on touch, consent, physical interventions, and work with minors before using body-based methods.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is body-based approaches, autonomic regulation, and the neuroception of safety. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work. The mechanism to keep in view is how threat, arousal, avoidance, meaning, and relational safety shape what a young person can feel, remember, and learn. The characteristic professional risk is moving into traumatic material before stabilisation, confusing calm with shutdown, or making disclosure the price of care. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in stabilisation sessions, trauma-informed schools and services, caregiver work, and specialist treatment pathways, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Nia is fifteen and is seen in a school counselling service for low mood and withdrawal from friends. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Nia's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Nia explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Nia chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Nia has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Nia can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
