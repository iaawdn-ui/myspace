
## 7. Core Practices

### 7.1 Orienting

Orienting uses sight, hearing, and proprioception to establish present safety. The young person slowly looks around, names colours or objects, notices support from the chair and floor, and identifies exits without rehearsing escape. The exercise must be adapted where the current environment is genuinely unsafe; pretending safety is not treatment.

### 7.2 Grounding

Grounding anchors attention in present sensory information: feet on the floor, back against a chair, holding a textured object, listening for near and far sounds, splashing cold water, or naming five observable details. Grounding is not a command to “calm down”. It is an invitation to collect evidence of here and now.

### 7.3 Breath

Slow breathing with a comfortable extended exhale can reduce arousal for some people. Breath work can also provoke panic, traumatic memories, dizziness, or a sense of control. Alternatives include breathing through movement, humming, singing, walking rhythm, or simply noticing natural breath. No adolescent should be forced to close their eyes or breathe in a prescribed pattern.

### 7.4 Movement and posture

Small experiments are preferable to dramatic release. Press feet into the floor. Push palms together. Stand and feel balance. Uncurl slowly from a collapsed posture. Walk the corridor. Shake hands and shoulders. Notice whether energy, breath, or emotion changes. The practitioner asks, “What is different?” rather than announcing what should be felt.

### 7.5 Boundaries in the body

Boundary work may involve noticing comfortable distance, practising saying “stop”, imagining a protective space, choosing where to sit, or using gesture to mark “enough”. For adolescents whose boundaries have been violated, bodily choice is therapeutic content, not a preliminary courtesy.

### 7.6 Tracking sensation and impulse

The practitioner helps sequence experience: sensation, emotion, impulse, thought, action. “My chest tightens; I feel trapped; I want to run; I think she'll shout; I leave.” Once sequence is visible, intervention points multiply. The young person might move, ask for a pause, clarify the other's intention, or choose a safer exit—not merely suppress leaving.

## 8. Somatic Work with Adolescents

Adolescents may find body-focused work embarrassing, especially amid puberty, body image concerns, disability, chronic illness, gender dysphoria, cultural modesty, or previous medical trauma. Language should be neutral and permission-based. Mirrors, touch, close physical proximity, and comments about appearance are avoided unless clinically essential, separately justified, consented, and within professional scope.

Sport, dance, martial arts, drama, music, outdoor activity, and occupational therapy may provide regulating experiences, but adults should not label every enjoyable activity “therapy” or remove its intrinsic value by over-clinicalising it. Sometimes the best somatic intervention is helping a young person safely return to football, swimming, drumming, or walking a dog.

Sleep is a somatic intervention. So are regular meals, treatment of pain, reduced late-night caffeine and energy drinks, management of sensory overload, and a school timetable that does not demand constant vigilance. A counsellor who ignores these basics while teaching sophisticated nervous-system language has reversed the clinical order.

## 9. Case Illustration: The Student Who Went Blank

**Presentation.** Tomas, fourteen, is repeatedly sanctioned for ignoring teachers. During conflict he stares, stops answering, and later cannot recall instructions. Staff interpret this as selective defiance. At home he becomes loud and restless after school, then sleeps for hours.

**Assessment.** The counsellor notices that when a male teacher raises his voice in a role-played example, Tomas' shoulders rise, breathing stops briefly, gaze fixes, and his hands become still. Tomas reports “everything goes fuzzy”. He denies choosing not to answer: “I can hear, but words don't come.” There is a history of witnessing severe domestic conflict before age nine. Medical screening and hearing assessment are arranged to avoid assuming a trauma explanation.

**Formulation.** Tomas oscillates between mobilisation and shutdown. Raised voices and public correction rapidly narrow his window. Stillness is a protective hypoarousal response, not evidence of calm. After school, accumulated arousal discharges through restlessness, followed by exhaustion.

**Intervention.** Work begins without trauma narration. Tomas maps early signs: ears “buzz”, jaw tightens, thoughts slow. He chooses signals with teachers: a break card, a seat near the door, and permission to stand at the back for two minutes rather than leaving unsupervised. Staff are coached—through proper consent and school consultation—to lower voice volume, give one instruction at a time, and check understanding after he has settled.

In counselling, Tomas practises orienting with eyes open, pressing feet into the floor, slow walking, and extending exhale only when it feels comfortable. He dislikes body scans because attention to his chest increases panic; that preference is respected. He learns to say, “I need a minute; I'm not refusing.” Teachers learn to hear that sentence as the beginning of regulation, not a challenge.

His mother is helped to create an after-school decompression routine: food, thirty minutes without questioning, physical activity, then homework in two short blocks. The counsellor does not ask Tomas to recount violent incidents. Later, when stability improves, he chooses to discuss one memory in a trauma-focused treatment with a trained clinician.

**Outcome.** Sanctions decrease. Tomas still becomes blank occasionally, but he recognises early signs and uses the break card before shutdown. The central change is interpretive as well as physiological: adults stop treating stillness as proof of contempt, and Tomas stops treating his body as evidence that he is “broken”.

## 10. Evidence and Critical Evaluation

Evidence for body-oriented trauma therapies is promising but uneven. Sensorimotor psychotherapy, somatic experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, breath and movement interventions, and related approaches have supportive studies, but many trials are small, heterogeneous, and difficult to blind. Outcomes may reflect therapeutic relationship, expectancy, exercise, sleep improvement, and general support as well as specific mechanisms.

Polyvagal theory has been clinically influential, especially in translating safety and co-regulation into practice. However, scholars dispute aspects of its evolutionary sequence, vagal measurement, and interpretation of respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Ethical teaching therefore distinguishes between: well-supported observations that safety cues and relationships affect arousal; plausible clinical heuristics about states; and contested neuroanatomical claims. Students should be suspicious of any programme that claims a proprietary exercise can diagnose trauma from heart-rate patterns or “reset” a nervous system in one session.

## 11. Ethics, Touch, and Safeguarding

Touch requires exceptional care with minors. Default practice in counselling should be no therapeutic touch unless there is a clear rationale, professional training, organisational policy, informed parental consent where required, the young person's ongoing assent, a chaperone or visibility where appropriate, and immediate respect for withdrawal. Many somatic interventions require no touch at all.

The practitioner must avoid suggesting that bodily responses prove a particular history. A startle response does not prove abuse; pelvic pain does not prove sexual trauma; dissociation has multiple causes. Somatic work must not become a memory-recovery technique. If disclosures occur, safeguarding procedures govern the response.

Cultural practices around modesty, gender, disability, movement, eye contact, and bodily display vary. Ask what is acceptable. Offer alternatives. Never require removing clothing, being filmed, closing eyes, or physical contact as proof of engagement.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Somatic** — Relating to lived bodily experience.
- **Sensorimotor** — Linking sensation, posture, movement, and action impulses.
- **Co-regulation** — Regulation supported through a safe relationship.
- **Titration** — Working with activation in small, manageable doses.
- **Pendulation** — Moving attention between distress and resource to build flexibility.
- **Window of tolerance** — The arousal range in which processing and learning remain possible.
- **Orienting** — Using sensory attention to establish present-time safety.
- **Procedural learning** — Bodily patterns learned through repetition rather than conscious declaration.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Somatic and sensorimotor practice teaches counsellors to notice the body without worshipping or over-interpreting it. Breath, posture, sensation, movement, sleep, pain, and relational safety provide information and points of intervention. Used humbly, these approaches help young people recognise state changes, widen their window of tolerance, and recover choice before, during, and after talk-based work.

## 14. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Distinguish mobilisation, social engagement, and shutdown in observable terms.
2. Why can a quiet adolescent be more dysregulated than a noisy one?
3. Give three alternatives when breath-focused work increases panic.
4. Why is choice itself a regulating intervention?
5. Which claims of polyvagal-informed practice should be taught cautiously?
6. Write a no-touch somatic plan for a student who dissociates during conflict.
7. When should medical assessment take priority over somatic formulation?

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For foundations.** Begin with Ogden's sensorimotor work and Dana's clinically accessible polyvagal translation.

**For critique.** Read scientific critiques of polyvagal theory alongside Porges, so that clinical usefulness does not become uncritical certainty.

**For adolescents.** Combine somatic reading with developmental neuroscience, sleep medicine, pain assessment, and trauma-informed school practice.

**For ethics.** Study professional guidance on touch, consent, physical interventions, and work with minors before using body-based methods.
