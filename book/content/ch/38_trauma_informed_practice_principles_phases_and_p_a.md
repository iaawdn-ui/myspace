# Trauma-Informed Practice: Principles, Phases, and Precautions

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of the trauma-informed principle and describe the clinical and political context of its emergence.
- State the four core principles of trauma-informed practice and explain the operational meaning of each.
- Describe the threat response system, including the three states and their functions, and explain the clinical consequences of a dysregulated nervous system.
- Describe the three-phase model of trauma treatment and the clinical reasons for the phase structure.
- Apply the safety and the ethical precautions that govern work with a young person with a trauma history.
- Critically evaluate the trauma-informed principle, including the questions of the definition of trauma, of the risk of individualising a structural problem, and of the evidence for the phase model.

## Chapter Overview

The trauma-informed principle is not a therapy. It is a **stance**, and its most consequential feature is that it begins with the question of what has happened to a person rather than with the question of what is wrong with them. This is not a semantic preference. A service that asks what has happened will make different decisions about waiting lists, about the physical environment, about the involvement of a young person in decisions about their own care, about the behaviour of staff, and about the documentation of a young person's history.

The principle emerged from a specific convergence in the 1990s. Three developments made it possible. The **empirical** development was the accumulation of a large research literature on the neurobiology of traumatic stress, on the developmental consequences of chronic threat, and on the effects of adverse childhood experiences, which established beyond reasonable dispute that the experience of threat is itself a health event. The **clinical** development was the recognition that the therapeutic approaches to trauma had been developed for adults with single-incident trauma and were being applied to children with chronic developmental trauma, which is a different condition. The **political** development was the recognition, driven by the survivor movement and by the investigations into institutional treatment, that the systems which were supposed to treat trauma had frequently caused it.

The four principles are **safety**, **trustworthiness and transparency**, **peer support**, and **collaboration and mutuality** — and the fourth of these is the one most often confused with its opposite, because "collaboration and mutuality" means that the work is done *with* the young person, and a great many of the practices that are described as trauma-informed are in fact done *to* the young person.

This chapter presents the principles, the threat response system, the phase model, the precautions, and the critical analysis, which includes the serious question of whether the trauma frame can become a way of locating a young person's difficulties in their own history when the history has been produced by the institutions around them.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The history of the concept

The idea that a service should be organised around the understanding that many of the people it serves have been traumatised is not new in its content, but it is new in its scope.

The **early** history belongs to the veterans' and the disaster mental health services, in which the observation that the services themselves were retraumatising — through intrusive procedures, through the compulsory and unpredictable treatment, and through the environments — led to a first generation of trauma-informed principles, in the 1980s.

The **second generation** emerged in the 1990s from the children's services and from the foster care and adoption literature, and it was driven by the recognition that the children being placed in care had typically been traumatised by the very systems that then processed them.

The **third generation** is the current one, and it is largely the product of the **National Child Traumatic Stress Network** in the United States, which published a conceptual framework in 2005 and a practitioner toolkit, and which organised the field around the four principles. The framework is now the most widely used definition in the English-speaking world, and it has been adopted in national policy in several countries.

### 1.2 The four R's and the six principles

The National Child Traumatic Stress Network's framework is often summarised in the four R's — **recognise**, **respond**, **refer** — with a fourth, **re-educate**, added by others. The fuller statement is the **six principles** of the SAMHSA framework, which are:

**Safety.** Throughout the organisation, the physical and emotional safety of the people served is prioritised.

**Trustworthiness and transparency.** The organisation is clear about what it will do, and it is honest about what it cannot do.

**Peer support.** The organisation actively supports and links with the peer support available in the community.

**Collaboration and mutuality.** The organisation works with the people it serves, and the people it serves share power in the decisions.

**Cultural, historical, and gender issues.** The organisation recognises and responds to the cultural, historical, and gender issues in the lives of the people it serves.

**Empowerment, voice, and choice.** The organisation honours the voice and the choices of the people it serves.

The four core principles are the first, second, third, and fourth, and the last two are usually treated as cross-cutting. A student should be able to state all six, and should be able to give an organisational example of each, because the principles are properties of the organisation and not only of the practitioner.

### 1.3 The developmental context

The developmental context is what makes the trauma-informed principle different in child and adolescent services than in adult services, and it is essential to understand.

**The developing nervous system is more plastic and more vulnerable.** The adolescent brain is in the middle of the second great period of synaptic reorganisation, and the period is one in which the stress response systems are highly reactive and the prefrontal regulatory systems are not yet fully mature. The adolescent is therefore more affected by chronic threat and less able to modulate it.

**The "child is not a small adult" principle.** The approach of treating a child as an adult with a smaller body produced the cognitive behavioural therapies of the 1980s and 1990s applied to children, and it is now understood to be an error in the treatment of trauma. A child who has been abused does not have a distorted appraisal of a safe situation; the situation is dangerous.

**Trauma is not a single event but a pattern.** The diagnostic criteria of the DSM and the ICD were designed around the single-incident trauma of the adult, and the developmental trauma of childhood — chronic, relational, repeated, and occurring during development — produces a different presentation, and the "complex trauma" or developmental trauma framework of the 1990s was developed to describe it.

**The developmental impact is cumulative and is mediated by the presence of a safe adult.** The research on the developmental trauma literature has established that the single most important protective factor against the impact of developmental trauma is a consistent, responsive adult in the young person's life, and this is why the work with the caregivers is central to the treatment rather than ancillary to it.

## 2. The Threat Response System

### 2.1 The three states

The clinical vocabulary of trauma-informed practice is most easily taught through the description of the threat response system, and a student should be able to state the three states and their functions.

**The dorsal vagal state**, sometimes described as the immobilised or shut-down state. The functions are conservation and the shutdown of the system when fighting and fleeing are not available or are not effective. Its signs are the ones that are easily mistaken for defiance: the blank stare, the flat affect, the "nothing" answer, the failure to respond, the refusal to move, the dissociation, and in the young person, the appearance of not caring. It is important for a practitioner to know that this state is not a choice and is not an attitude, and that a young person in this state cannot be reasoned with, cannot be challenged, and cannot be engaged in any task.

**The sympathetic state**, the fight-or-flight state. The functions are mobilisation and the rapid preparation for action. Its signs are the increased heart rate, the shallow rapid breathing, the tremor, the increased muscle tone, the narrowed attention, the rapid speech, the raised voice, the agitation, and the inattention to everything that is not the perceived threat.

**The ventral vagal state**, the social engagement state. The functions are the maintenance of safety through connection, the moderation of the other two states, the capacity for calm, and the capacity for social engagement. Its signs are the settled affect, the steady voice, the eye contact, the capacity to receive comfort, and the capacity to think.

### 2.2 The importance of the state for practice

The single most practically useful thing a practitioner can know about the threat response system is that **the client's state determines what intervention is possible.** A young person in the dorsal vagal state cannot be engaged in a cognitive task, a young person in the sympathetic state cannot be given an insight, and a young person in the ventral vagal state can do both.

A great many clinical failures with young people with trauma histories are failures of state, not failures of technique. A practitioner who tries to reason with a young person who has gone blank is not being skilful; the technique is unavailable.

The clinical rules that follow are simple and a student should be able to state them.

**If the young person is in the dorsal vagal state, the intervention is orienting and physical.** The voice is low and slow, the language is concrete, the focus is on the external environment, and the goal is to restore the ventral vagal state. Talking about feelings will not work and may make things worse.

**If the young person is in the sympathetic state, the intervention is containment and reduction.** The arousal is reduced before any content is addressed, and the reduction is done through the body — the breathing, the grounding, the movement — because the young person cannot be talked down from a sympathetic state.

**If the young person is in the ventral vagal state, the work can proceed.** This is the state in which the cognitive, the emotional, and the narrative work of this book is possible.

### 2.3 The window of tolerance

The **window of tolerance** is the range of arousal within which a person can function and in which information can be processed. Above it is the hyperaroused state; below it is the hypoaroused state. The developmental and clinical significance is that a young person with a trauma history has a narrow window, that the narrowing is itself a symptom, and that a good deal of what is called "non-compliance" in this population is a young person who has been pushed outside the window.

A practitioner working with a young person with a trauma history should be able to estimate the width of the window and should be working to widen it, because the widening is a precondition for everything else. The practical implication is that the preparatory and stabilisation work of Chapter 40 is not a preliminary to the treatment; it is a substantial part of it.

## 3. The Three-Phase Model

### 3.1 The structure

The three-phase model, which was developed principally from the work on complex trauma with adults and then applied to children and adolescents, divides the treatment into three phases with explicit criteria for movement between them.

**Phase one: safety and stabilisation.** The establishment of safety, in the broadest sense: physical safety, emotional safety, relational safety, and the practical safety of housing, income, and routine. The work is on the reduction of the symptoms, the establishment of the coping and grounding skills, the building of the relational safety with the practitioner, and the resolution of the practical problems that keep the young person in a threat state.

**Phase two: trauma processing.** The deliberate, consented, and carefully paced work on the traumatic memories and on the meanings attached to them. The phase includes the specific trauma-focused interventions — the protocols of Chapter 39 — and the narrative and meaning-making work.

**Phase three: integration and rehabilitation.** The consolidation of the changes, the rebuilding of the young person's life, the re-engagement with education, with relationships, with the future, and the development of the ordinary skills of a life that has been interrupted by the trauma.

### 3.2 The criteria for phase movement

The phase model's most practically useful feature is its explicit criteria for movement, and a student should be able to state them.

**The criteria to move from phase one to phase two** are: the young person is able to remain present in session and is not repeatedly dissociating; the young person can use the grounding and coping skills independently; there is sufficient relational safety in the therapeutic relationship; the practical situation is stable enough that the processing will not be interrupted by a crisis; the young person is able to tolerate the affect associated with the trauma narrative; and the young person has consented to the processing work, informed and ongoing.

**The criteria to move from phase two to phase three** are: the trauma narrative has been processed sufficiently that the memories are no longer intrusive or overwhelming; the young person has made a meaning of the experience that they can live with; the symptoms have reduced; the young person's functioning has improved; and the young person has developed enough coping to manage the ordinary stresses.

**The criteria to move backwards** are as important and are frequently forgotten: a return to phase one when the young person's functioning deteriorates, when the processing is reopened, or when a new crisis occurs.

### 3.3 The criticisms of the phase model

The phase model is the most influential framework in the field and it is also the most criticised, and a student should be able to state the criticisms.

**The evidence for the model as a package is thin.** The phases have not been tested as an integrated protocol in controlled trials. The evidence is for the components: the evidence for the specific trauma-focused interventions is good, and the evidence for the stabilisation skills is more limited.

**The phases can become a way of avoiding the difficult work.** A practitioner who is uncomfortable with the processing phase can keep the young person in phase one indefinitely, and the young person's compliance with the stabilisation work becomes the price of admission.

**The model under-recognises the developmental reality.** The phases presume a capacity for sustained processing that many young people do not have, and the developmental trauma literature argues that a young person's processing may need to be much more gradual and much more embedded in daily life.

**The model's universality is questionable.** The phase model was developed in a particular clinical culture, and the sequence assumes that safety can be established before the trauma is addressed, which is not always true, and which is not true in some cultural and political settings where the safety cannot be established by the individual or the practitioner at all.
