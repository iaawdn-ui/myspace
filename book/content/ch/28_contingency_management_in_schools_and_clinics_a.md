# Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the rationale for using contingency management in schools and clinics, and state the conditions under which it is appropriate.
- Describe the design and implementation of a token economy in a classroom setting.
- Explain the design of a behaviour support plan, and describe the elements it requires.
- Describe the construction of a behaviour contract, and state its limits in the school context.
- Explain the role of the classroom teacher, the teaching assistant, and the parent in a contingency management programme, and describe the training they require.
- Describe the management of a classroom in which the behaviour of a small group is disrupting the class.
- Apply contingency management to a case in a school or clinic, and evaluate the ethical constraints.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of contingency management in institutional settings, and state the legal and regulatory context.

## Chapter Overview

Contingency management is the most direct application of the operant principles of Chapter 26 to a real setting, and it is also the application with the largest gap between its technical simplicity and its practical difficulty. The technique is easy to describe: define the target behaviours, define the consequences, make the consequences contingent, and review. The difficulty lies in the fact that a school is an organisation of many people, each of whom responds to a young person's behaviour, and a contingency system is only as good as the weakest of those responses.

The failure mode is predictable and it is the central clinical problem of this chapter. A programme is designed with the class teacher. The teaching assistant is not told. The dinner supervisor is not told. The supply teacher, who covers on the two days a week the class teacher is absent, is not told. On the first day the supply teacher sees a pupil being disruptive and responds with the customary escalation, and the pupil learns that the classroom contingency holds on Tuesday and Thursday and not on Wednesday. Two weeks later the data look like a mystery, and the practitioner must decide whether the hypothesis was wrong or the implementation was inconsistent, and the honest answer is usually the second and the diagnosis is usually harder than it should have been.

The other central theme is the status of the system within the setting. A token economy introduces an explicit exchange relation between the adult and the young person, and in a school that exchange is visible to peers. This is not a minor consideration: for a young person who is already struggling with status, a visible system of rewards and penalties can be more damaging than the behaviour it addresses. The practitioner must decide, in each case, whether the system is visible, and the decision is not always "no".

This chapter presents the technical design of school and clinic contingency systems, the implementation requirements that make them work, the failure modes, and the ethical and legal framework that applies.

## 1. The Setting as a Contingency System

### 1.1 Every setting is already a contingency system

The single most useful clinical idea in this chapter is that a school, a clinic, and a family are already systems of contingencies, operating whether or not anyone has designed them. The question is never whether to introduce consequences into a setting; the setting is already full of them. The question is only whether the consequences are the ones that serve the young person.

The inventory is short and it is diagnostic. For any behaviour that concerns a practitioner, the inventory asks: what happens immediately after it, for the young person, for the peers, and for the adult; what happens immediately before it; who is present; and what is the young person's alternative.

Three findings are common.

**The problem behaviour is reinforced.** The pupil who is sent out of class gets to leave a boring lesson; the pupil who is shouted at gets attention; the young person who refuses to attend gets to remain with the family. These are the classic patterns and they are everywhere.

**The desired behaviour is unreinforced.** The pupil who works quietly for forty minutes receives nothing; the pupil who asks for help is sent away; the young person who attends on time and quietly is invisible.

**The adults are inconsistent.** Two teachers in the same corridor respond to the same behaviour differently, and the young person has learned that the inconsistency is exploitable.

### 1.2 Why schools and clinics are the right sites for this work

Three features make schools and clinics the natural settings for contingency management.

**The contingencies are there.** The school's own organisation produces the reinforcements and the punishments; the practitioner does not have to build a system from nothing, only to change the existing one.

**The consequences are legitimate.** A school can legitimately offer a young person access to a preferred activity, a place on a team, a reduced homework load for a defined period, a role in a responsibility group. These are things the school controls and things the young person values.

**The setting is where the behaviour happens.** A behaviour changed in the clinic and not in the corridor has not been changed, and the school is the only place where the corridor behaviour can be observed and modified.

### 1.3 When contingency management is appropriate

Contingency management is appropriate when the following conditions are met, and the practitioner should be able to state the reasoning for the decision in each case.

**The behaviour is clearly defined and is a problem in itself.** The target must be observable and countable, and the case for changing it must be made in terms of the young person's or the setting's functioning, not in terms of the adults' convenience.

**The behaviour has been assessed.** A contingency designed on the basis of a guess is a guess with consequences attached, and a consequence that is wrongly specified is worse than no consequence, because it teaches the young person that the system's rules are arbitrary.

**The consequences are within the organisation's power to deliver.** A school cannot deliver a consequence that depends on another agency, and a clinic cannot deliver a consequence that depends on the family, unless the family is in the plan.

**The young person can succeed.** A contingency that the young person cannot meet has failed, and the response to failure is to adjust the system, not to increase the stakes.

**There is a plan for the transition.** A contingency system that is introduced and never withdrawn becomes a permanent dependency, and a young person's dependence on a system of external rewards is a developmental regression.

**The young person, or the group, has been consulted.** A system imposed on a group of students will be resisted, and the resistance will be rational.

### 1.4 When it is not appropriate

Contingency management should not be used where the behaviour cannot ethically be shaped, where the young person lacks the capacity to understand the system, where the consequence would be a deprivation of a right, or where the system's real function is to control rather than to teach.

A special caution is required in the case of young people with severe cognitive impairment. A token economy is dependent on the young person's understanding of the exchange, and a young person who does not understand it will respond to it randomly. For these young people, the contingencies must be direct and immediate and the system must be supported by the adults, and the plan for fading must be correspondingly slower.

## 2. The Token Economy

### 2.1 The design

A **token economy** in a classroom is a system in which specified behaviours are reinforced with tokens, which are exchangeable at a specified time for specified backup reinforcers. The design requires six decisions, and each of them is a decision that is usually made implicitly and should be made explicitly.

**The target behaviours.** A small number, clearly defined, observable, and countable, and chosen because their change matters. In a class of twenty-five with three pupils who are disruptive, the targets might be: hands up before speaking, remaining seated during the lesson, and completing the first three questions of the starter task. Six targets would be unworkable.

**The reinforcer.** The token itself, or an immediate item. The tokens should be small, durable, collectable, and immediately delivered. The backing reinforcers should be chosen by the young people and should be realistically obtainable.

**The schedule.** The density of reinforcement determines the behaviour. A dense schedule produces a rapid establishment; a sparse schedule produces a generalisation failure. The thinning plan should be written down from the beginning.

**The exchange procedure.** When, where, and by whom the tokens are exchanged. The exchange should be frequent, should be a positive event, and should not be a source of administrative friction for the staff.

**The response cost.** Whether tokens are lost for specified behaviours, and what the loss procedure is. The response cost should be small, immediate, and never a deprivation of a right.

**The fade plan.** The schedule on which the system will be withdrawn, and the criteria for withdrawal. A token economy with no fade plan is a permanent system.

### 2.2 The implementation

The implementation requirements are the part that determines success, and they are the part most often under-specified.

**Staff training.** Everyone who will deliver a token must be trained. The training should include, at a minimum: what the target behaviours are; what to do when they occur and what not to do; what to say and what not to say; how to deliver the token promptly and neutrally; and what to do in the situations that the training predicts will be difficult.

**A rehearsal.** The training should include a rehearsal, in which the staff practise delivering tokens in the presence of a simulated disruption, and in which the appropriate responses to the difficult cases are agreed.

**A written protocol.** The protocol should be on the desk, in the classroom, in the language of the setting, with the target behaviours, the responses, and the exception procedures. A protocol in a file in the office is not used.

**A monitoring system.** A record of the tokens delivered, which is both the fidelity check on the implementation and the data on the behaviour.

**A weekly review.** The review examines the data, the implementation fidelity, and the young people's own views, and it makes the adjustments.

### 2.3 Group versus individual systems

A class-wide token economy is a different instrument from an individual one, and the choice should be deliberate.

**The class-wide system** treats the class as a unit, with the consequences delivered to the class. It is simpler to administer, it does not single out any young person, and it has a significant additional effect: it changes the peers' behaviour towards each other, because a pupil who does not want to lose the class's tokens will manage a disruptive pupil in ways the teacher cannot.

The class-wide system has three limitations. It cannot address an individual young person's specific behaviour, because the consequences are too coarse. It risks the competitive dynamic in which a strong pupil dominates the token economy. And the target behaviours must be ones the whole class can achieve, which constrains them.

**The individual system** is more precise, more informative, and more visible to peers, and it is the appropriate choice for a young person working on a specific behaviour.

**The hybrid** is usually the best: a class-wide system for the shared classroom behaviours, and an individual system for a named young person working on a specific goal, with the individual system structured so that it is not publicly displayed.

### 2.4 The problems specific to token economies

**The contamination of the target behaviour.** Tokens become a currency, and young people learn to produce the behaviour for the token and to stop when the token stops. The fade plan is the only answer.

**The peer problem.** A visible token system makes some young people targets, and a young person with a history of being bullied may be in greater danger with tokens than without.

**The staff problem.** Token delivery is additional work, and the adults who do it least are usually those with the greatest need, and the result is an inconsistent system.

**The dismissal problem.** A pupil who is sent out of the classroom loses the opportunity to earn tokens, and the removal from the class is also a removal from the reinforcement system, which is why a token economy and an exclusionary response are incompatible in the same plan.

**The reward problem.** The backup reinforcers must be genuinely valued and genuinely available, and a school that promises a reward it cannot deliver, or that offers only what the young person considers worthless, has damaged the system rather than established it.

## 3. Behaviour Support Plans

### 3.1 The function

A **behaviour support plan** is the written document that specifies, for a named young person, the target behaviours, the strategies to be used, the contingencies, the roles of the adults, the data to be collected, and the review arrangements. It is the formalisation of contingency management for an individual, and it is the document that most schools lack and most need.

The function of the plan is partly clinical and partly organisational. Clinically, it ensures that the adults are working from a common specification rather than from individual impressions. Organisationally, it is the document that the practitioner can take to a meeting, and that survives a change of staff, and that can be reviewed.

### 3.2 The elements

**Student and staff information.** The young person's strengths, interests, and preferences; the adults involved and their roles; the timetable of the adults, so that the plan can be matched to the staffing.

**Target behaviours.** Stated in operational form, with the baseline data, and with the target specified in a way that is measurable.

**Strategies.** The specific techniques: differential reinforcement, planned ignoring, a specified response to disruption, a break procedure, a de-escalation script. The strategies should be specific enough to be followed and few enough to be remembered.

**The contingencies.** The rewards and the response costs, with the schedule.

**The response to escalation.** A specified, graduated, and rehearsed procedure, with the point at which the young person is removed from the setting and what is said when that happens. This element is the one most often missing and the one most responsible for failure, because when a young person escalates, the adult's improvised response is the treatment.

**Data collection.** What is recorded, by whom, and how the data are reviewed.

**A review schedule**, and the criteria for ending the plan.

**The involvement of the young person.** The young person's own words, their own goals, and their agreement, should be in the plan.

### 3.3 The graduated response and the escalation procedure

The escalation procedure should be specified in advance, in writing, and be common to all the adults, and it should include a clear statement that the removal from the class is itself a consequence and that the return is the reward. The commonest errors are to remove without a specified return condition, to remove with a variable duration, and to allow the young person to return as soon as they are calm, which reinforces the escalation, because the fastest route back is the loudest.

A better procedure specifies that the return is conditional on the completion of a defined task, and that the duration of the removal is short and fixed, and that the young person is accompanied on the return.

### 3.4 The behaviour support plan and the law

A behaviour support plan in a school is a document with legal and regulatory implications, and the practitioner must know the jurisdiction. In the United States, the framework is the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, under which a behaviour that results in a change of placement is a manifestation of a disability, under the discipline procedures of IDEA, and under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act the protections apply to students with disabilities in any setting. The protections include the requirement of a functional behavioural assessment before a change of placement for a student with a disability, and the requirement that the plan be developed by a team.

A practitioner in a school who writes a behaviour support plan without checking the regulatory context is taking a legal risk as well as a clinical one, and the check takes ten minutes. The equivalent framework in the United Kingdom is the statutory framework of behaviour support and, for example, the Equality Act 2010 and the Children and Families Act 2014; elsewhere the practitioner must identify the local equivalent. The principle is the same everywhere: the plan for a young person with a disability or with a special educational need is a document with legal standing and not merely a clinical convenience.

## 4. Behaviour Contracts in the School Context

### 4.1 The form

A school behaviour contract is a written agreement between a young person and the school, or between a young person and a class, in which specific behaviours are specified in return for specific consequences. The most useful form in the secondary school is the **class contract**, in which the class as a group agrees to a small set of behaviours in return for a set of privileges, and in which the young people have participated in drafting it.

The advantages of the class contract over the class-wide token economy are that it requires no currency, no counting, and no exchange procedure, and that it is far more visible and comprehensible to parents and to a school board. Its disadvantages are that it is less precise, that it is harder to modify mid-term, and that it has no natural fading mechanism — a class contract is typically a term-long arrangement, and the natural conclusion of it is its renewal, which is a decision that has to be made deliberately.

### 4.2 The limits

A behaviour contract in a school must not contract for the removal of a young person's rights: not for access to education, not for access to meals, not for access to the toilet, not for the removal of a statutory exclusion, and not for the removal of access to a member of staff who is the young person's advocate. A contract that trades an educational entitlement for compliance is unlawful in most jurisdictions, and a contract that trades it for a privilege is likely to be so.

A contract must also be genuinely voluntary, which in a school means that the young person can decline to sign without an academic or behavioural consequence, and the practitioner should be prepared for the case where the young person declines and the school wants to know what to do. The answer is to use the class contract, which involves the young person as a member of a group rather than as an individual signatory.

## 5. Case Illustration: The Class That Had Stopped Listening

**Presentation.** Mr Adeyemi teaches a Year 8 class of twenty-eight in a large secondary school. The referral from the pastoral team says that the class is "out of control" and that three students are responsible for the disruption. Mr Adeyemi has been teaching for twenty-two years and is close to the end of his tether. The head of year wants the class split.

**The assessment.** The practitioner asks for the data before the plan.

The class's behaviour log shows something that the referral has not. The disruption is concentrated in a single lesson, the last lesson on a Tuesday afternoon, and it is concentrated in a specific thirty-minute activity: the teacher setting a written task with a thirty-minute independent working period. The lesson before it — the same subject, the same class, the same teacher, an hour earlier — has a behaviour log with almost no entries. The difference is the activity, not the class, and the difference is not the teacher.

The practitioner observes the lesson. The pattern is clear. At the start of the independent working period, Mr Adeyemi sets the task and then circulates to the students who are stuck. This leaves the students who are working unattended, and three of them — the ones the referral names — start talking. The noise rises. Mr Adeyemi returns to the front and says, in a voice the class has heard a thousand times, "Some people are finding this really hard." The class knows who he means. The three boys laugh, because the statement is a ritual and not an instruction, and the two students nearest them join in, and the class as a whole spends the last fifteen minutes of the lesson in a low-level state of disruption that no single student could be held responsible for.

**The functional analysis.** This is a classroom-level functional analysis, and it is the analogue of the individual analysis of Chapter 24.

*Antecedent.* An unsupervised independent working period, and the teacher's attention leaving the class.

*Behaviour.* Talking, escalating to noise, occasional refusal to work.

*Consequence.* A ritualised, non-specific warning from the teacher, followed by the teacher's return to the front, followed by the class's attention, which is entertaining. The behaviour reliably produces a change in the adult's activity and a change in the attention of the class, and it does so whether or not it produces any work.

The behaviour is maintained by **peer attention**, and the adults' ritualised response, which is intended as a signal of disapproval, is a form of attention that the class reads as a signal of significance. This is the most common classroom dynamic the practitioner will encounter, and it is invisible in a referral that describes a "disruptive class".

**The plan.** Four elements, and the plan is a plan for the whole class.

*Element one: change the antecedent.* The independent working period is broken into shorter segments with a defined check-in at the end of each, and Mr Adeyemi's circulation is planned, with a defined route that keeps him in view of the class, and with a defined task during the circulation — a "look at this" card that he uses to ask a student a question about their work, which gives him a legitimate reason to be at the back of the room and keeps his attention available to the whole class.

*Element two: the class contract.* The class agrees to a set of behaviours — three of them, drafted with the students — in return for a set of privileges that the class chooses, and the privileges are things the school can deliver: the last five minutes of the lesson for a chosen activity, a weekly slot in the school newsletter's quiz section, and a class vote on the music for the last lesson. The contract is displayed in the room, signed by the students, and reviewed at the half-term.

*Element three: the response to disruption.* The teacher response is specified in advance and is the change with the largest effect. When the noise rises, Mr Adeyemi does not address the class; he addresses the students who are working, and he says something that acknowledges them specifically — and the class is told in the first week that this is what happens. The three boys are not named, and the class is not told to notice who is being addressed. The logic is that the previous response was a public ritual of generalised disapproval, and the new response is a public signal that the working students are the ones the teacher's attention is for.

*Element four: the data.* Mr Adeyemi records the number of interventions and the number of students working at the end of each of the last ten minutes of the lesson, and the class's work output. The recording is minimal, and the review is with the practitioner every fortnight.

**The outcome.** The interventions drop substantially in the second fortnight and the working output rises. Mr Adeyemi's own summary is that the class is "not the same" and that the biggest change was not the class contract but the change in what he did when the noise started. The head of year withdraws the request to split the class.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the central theme of this chapter. The referral described three students; the analysis described a class-wide response pattern maintained by an adult's ritual, and the plan that changed the adult's behaviour changed the class. It also illustrates the theme of implementation: the plan required a teacher to change a deeply ingrained classroom habit, and the fortnightly review with the practitioner was the mechanism that sustained the change, because the old habit returns within days of the plan lapsing.
