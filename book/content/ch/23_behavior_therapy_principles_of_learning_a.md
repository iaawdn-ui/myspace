# Behavior Therapy: Principles of Learning

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the historical emergence of behaviour therapy from the psychology of learning, tracing the work of Pavlov, Thorndike, Watson, and Skinner.
- State the epistemological commitments of radical behaviourism and explain both their power and their limitations for clinical work.
- Describe the general procedure of classical conditioning, and the phenomena of acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation, and discrimination.
- Distinguish the four operant contingencies, and distinguish negative reinforcement from punishment in the terms the literature actually uses.
- Explain the law of effect, and critically discuss the problem of establishing the reinforcing value of a stimulus.
- Describe shaping, chaining, and prompting as behavioural technologies, and specify the circumstances in which each is indicated.
- Explain the methods used to measure behaviour, and why the measurement question is not a technicality.
- Apply the principles of learning to a case involving a young person, and identify where the learning account is incomplete.

## Chapter Overview

Every therapy in this book contains an implicit theory of how people change. The behavioural theories state theirs most plainly, and that plainness is both their greatest strength and the source of most of the misunderstandings that surround them.

The behavioural account begins with a claim about evidence. Human beings are complex, but the complexity is not a reason for mystery. Behaviour is a function of conditions; conditions can be specified; specified conditions can be altered; and altered conditions produce altered behaviour. The chain from condition to consequence to behaviour is the causal spine of the tradition, and almost every technique in the behavioural repertoire is a way of arranging that chain deliberately.

The account begins in the laboratory, and it is important to be accurate about what the laboratory actually established. Pavlov showed that a neutral stimulus could acquire the capacity to elicit a response through pairing with a stimulus that already elicited it. Thorndike showed that the strength of a habit increased with its satisfying consequences and decreased with its annoying ones. Watson converted both findings into a methodological manifesto about the irrelevance of consciousness. Skinner extended the operant analysis to voluntary behaviour and made the analysis of behaviour the organising principle of a whole discipline, including the analysis of language, thought, and self-control.

What the laboratory did not establish is that a simple and universal set of principles explains all human action. Behavioural therapists work with children who have been abused, who are grieving, who are autistic, who are in families where the contingencies are not merely misarranged but actively hostile. The learning account is a powerful tool for understanding a great deal of that, and it is not a sufficient account of any of it. A student who learns to hold both of these statements at once is doing something the discipline itself has found difficult.

This chapter presents the principles in their original and technical form, because the clinical techniques of the following chapters cannot be understood without them. It then examines the epistemological position from which the principles were derived, because the position is genuinely powerful and genuinely constraining, and a practitioner needs to know both.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The reflex arc and the era of the reflex

The behavioural tradition inherited its basic unit of analysis from the physiology of the nervous system. In the decades before the First World War, psychology in Europe was dominated by a set of questions about the reflex: what counts as a reflex, how is a reflex arc organised, what is the difference between a voluntary and an involuntary response? The Russian physiologists, following Sechenov, Pavlov, and Vvedensky, treated the nervous system as a machine for transforming stimuli, and the psychologist's task as the description of that transformation.

Two features of this inheritance are worth noticing, because they shaped everything that followed. The first is the commitment to a mechanistic vocabulary: *stimulus*, *response*, *reflex*, *conditioned*. The second is the treatment of the organism as something that is acted upon. The reflex arc was an incoming channel, and the research programme of the early twentieth century was to trace the channels.

This inheritance supplied behavioural psychology with a vocabulary and a method, but it also supplied a limitation that would take fifty years to recognise. If behaviour is a chain of responses to stimuli, then the individual is passive, and there is nowhere in the picture for intention, meaning, or choice.

### 1.2 Pavlov and the conditioned reflex

Ivan Pavlov was a physiologist of digestion. His celebrated work on the conditioned reflex began, by his own account, as a methodological annoyance: he was measuring salivary secretion in dogs and noticed that the dogs began secreting before the food arrived, in response to the sound of the metronome or the sight of the experimenter. Rather than treating this as an inconvenient artefact, Pavlov made it the object of study, and the "physiological" method of the conditioned reflex became the paradigm for the experimental analysis of learning.

The core demonstration is now so familiar that it is easy to underestimate how unusual it was. A bell produces no salivation. Food produces salivation. Pair the two, and after a number of pairings the bell alone produces salivation. The new stimulus has acquired the capacity of the old one.

Pavlov's work established the *laws* of conditioning: acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation, discrimination, and the phenomena of higher-order conditioning and inhibition. These laws are the raw material of every behavioural therapy in the field, including the exposure therapies of Chapter 25 and the systematic desensitisation of the fear that the account of classical conditioning was originally designed to explain.

Pavlov also made a methodological move that matters enormously: he insisted on analysing the *whole* nervous response, including the orienting response and the emotional and vegetative components, and not only the salivation. His conceptual nervous system was a system of stimulus and response relations, and his theory of conditioned inhibition in particular anticipated, in a different language, a great deal of what the cognitive and mindfulness traditions later described as the inhibition of a learned response by a competing one.

### 1.3 Thorndike and the law of effect

Edward Thorndike began with a puzzle box: a cat that had to step on a pedal to escape, and that, on being placed in the box, took a long time to escape by accident. Thorndike timed the escape and recorded the curve, and the curve became the first learning curve in psychology. It rose steeply at first and then flattened, and Thorndike's explanation of the flattening was the law of effect:

> Of several responses made to the same situation, those accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal will, other things equal, be more firmly connected with the situation, so that, when it recurs, they will be more likely to recur; those accompanied or closely followed by discomfort will, other things equal, be less firmly connected with the situation, so that, when it recurs, they will be less likely to recur.

Three features of the formulation matter. It is hedged ("other things equal"), which is a technical admission that other variables matter. It is about the *connection* between a situation and a response, which is a claim about selection rather than about instruction. And it is empirical rather than mentalistic, because Thorndike was explicit that his experiments contained no consciousness, and the law of effect was offered as a replacement for the appeal to consciousness that he found useless.

Thorndike's curriculum is also the origin of the *law of readiness* and the *law of exercise*, both of which appear later in this book in contemporary form.

### 1.4 Watson and the behavioural manifesto

John Broadus Watson's 1913 paper, "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It," was the founding statement of the school, and it is worth quoting its most famous sentence in full, because the sentence is more radical than it usually appears:

> Psychology as the behaviorist views it is a purely objective experimental branch of natural science. Its theoretical goal is the prediction and control of behavior. Introspection forms no essential part of this method, for the reliability of introspection is secured only by those who have trained themselves to use it in accordance with the very conditions under which it is employed.

Watson was not claiming that inner experience does not exist. He was claiming that it cannot function as a datum in a science of behaviour, because the conditions under which it is reported are not subject to the controls that make other data reliable. The methodological proposal, in other words, was a proposal about what counted as evidence.

This is the point at which behaviour therapy inherited a characteristic and still controversial stance: that the practitioner's data is what can be observed and counted, and that reports of internal states are data only under specified conditions. Much of what follows in this book — the cognitive, the humanistic, the existential traditions — exists in part as a response to this stance, and the arguments between them are more interesting when the student understands that they are partly arguments about epistemology.

### 1.5 Skinner and the functional analysis of behaviour

Burrhus Frederic Skinner's work from the 1930s onwards was the decisive extension of the tradition, and it transformed it in two directions.

The first was the extension from reflexes to *operant* behaviour, that is, to behaviour that is emitted rather than elicited. Skinner demonstrated that behaviour is shaped by its consequences, and that the analysis of a behaviour is the analysis of the contingencies that produce and maintain it.

The second was the extension into a full *methodology*. Skinner argued that the study of behaviour was not an exotic specialism of psychology but the fundamental discipline, from which psychology itself was a branch, and he insisted that the analysing of behaviour required no appeal to hypothetical inner causes. His 1953 book *Science and Human Behavior* set out the system; his 1957 *Verbal Behavior* extended it to language; and his later work on *Schedules of Reinforcement* (with Ferster) and on the analysis of private events developed the experimental apparatus that became the therapy.

Skinner's methodological claim was the important one, and it is often caricatured. He did not say that inner events do not occur. He said that inner events are behaviour, that is, they are subject to the same analysis, and that when one is describing behaviour one should describe the conditions under which the event occurs and the consequences that follow it, rather than positing a private cause that explains the observable. A private event is defined by its consequences.

### 1.6 The later developments

The period from the 1960s to the present has seen the behavioural tradition absorb, and then sometimes reject, a series of additions:

- **Mediated learning** (Bandura, Rotter) added the concept of expectancy and self-efficacy between stimulus and response. The social learning theory of 1977 argued that most human learning occurs without direct reinforcement, through observation and modelling. This is the single most important development for work with young people, because it means that a child can learn a behaviour from watching a peer, and that learning can be arranged in a classroom without waiting for the child's own behaviour to be shaped.
- **The cognitive revolution** (Lazarus, Beck, Ellis) returned to the interior, but in a form that the behavioural tradition could partly accommodate: attention, memory, and belief are treated as mediational processes with their own contingencies, and the thought record is best understood as a data-collection procedure.
- **The contextual tradition** (Bowers, Goldstein, Heiman, Jacobson) restored the importance of context and of the client's frame of reference, and produced a body of research on the language of therapy that is directly relevant to the cultural considerations in Chapter 48.
- **Applied behaviour analysis** (Baer, Wolf, Risley, and the applied literature) institutionalised the methodological standards of single-case experimental designs, functional analysis, and behaviourally defined treatment targets, and it is the applied wing that produced the functional assessment model in Chapter 24.

## 2. The Epistemology of Observable Behaviour

### 2.1 The radical behaviourism of Skinner

Skinner's position is usually described by the formula that behaviour is a function of the organism and its environment, and that the environment selects behaviour. Both halves of that formula carry weight.

The first half — the functional relation — asserts that behaviour is not caused by something hidden; it is a *function of* specified conditions. A functional relation is not a cause in the ordinary sense. It is a statement of covariation under specified controls. If a behaviour varies systematically with a specified environmental condition, and the condition is experimentally manipulated, the relation can be used to predict and to control. The precision of the claim is what makes it useful, and the modesty of it is what makes it defensible.

The second half — selection by consequences — asserts that the environment does not merely trigger behaviour but *selects* it, in the sense that those behaviours that produce consequences that the organism wants to repeat are more likely to be emitted in the future. The metaphor of selection replaced the metaphor of release, and it displaced a long tradition in which behaviour was thought of as something that issued from within and was then released by a stimulus.

### 2.2 Public and private events

Skinner's most consequential analytic move was the treatment of private events. A private event — a thought, a feeling, an expectation, an intention — is defined not by its phenomenology but by its discriminative stimulus and its consequences. "Anger" is not a cause; anger is a pattern of behaviour (in the broad sense, including verbal behaviour) that is evoked by particular stimuli and that has particular consequences. The clinician's task is to specify the antecedent conditions and the consequences, and to find the functional relation.

The analytic value of this is considerable, because it makes the private event available for prediction and control in exactly the way that public events are. Its philosophical cost is considerable too, because it appears to reduce the person to a black box that is opened only at the input and output ends. Almost all the major criticisms of behaviour therapy — from humanistic, existential, feminist, and cultural perspectives — are in some form a criticism of this position.

### 2.3 The limits of the epistemological position

A student should be able to state three limits without caricature.

**First**, the account is descriptively complete for a great deal of behaviour and explanatorily thin for a great deal more. The question "why did she refuse to come to school?" is easy to answer behaviourally and unhelpfully so. The behavioural answer is a set of contingencies. The question "what would it take for her to come?" is the answer the behavioural account gives best, and it is a much better question than the first.

**Second**, the account under-specifies the *meaning* of consequences. Whether a given consequence is a reinforcer is not a property of the consequence but of the relation between the consequence and the organism's current state, and there is a circularity in the analysis that behaviour analysts have addressed through a variety of techniques, none of which is entirely satisfying.

**Third**, the account struggles with the phenomena of trauma, loss, and attachment, where the clinically relevant events have no current contingency that would maintain them. A child who does not speak after a year of silence at school is not maintained by a contingency in the present. The behavioural account of such a case is not wrong, but it is radically incomplete, and this incompleteness is the reason that the trauma-informed chapters in Part VI exist.

## 3. Classical Conditioning

### 3.1 The general procedure

In the general procedure, a previously neutral stimulus (the **conditioned stimulus**, or CS) is repeatedly presented with a stimulus that naturally produces a reflexive response (the **unconditioned stimulus**, or US), and the response that the US produces becomes the **unconditioned response** (UR). After pairing, the CS alone elicits a similar response, which is the **conditioned response** (CR).

The technical vocabulary matters because the therapy that follows from it uses it literally. In the treatment of a child's fear of the school canteen, the canteen is the CS, the pain of the allergic reaction is the US, the nausea is the UR, and the anticipatory nausea at the sight of the canteen is the CR. The aim of the behavioural treatment is to extinguish the CR while ensuring the US never occurs, which is the logic of counterconditioning and, in its more intensive form, the exposure work of Chapter 25.

### 3.2 Acquisition, extinction, and spontaneous recovery

**Acquisition** is the phase in which the CR is being learned. In most human fear conditioning, acquisition is rapid: one or two pairings suffice to establish a substantial conditional response, and much of the apparent power of phobias comes from the fact that a single traumatic pairing can be enough.

**Extinction** is the phase in which the CS is presented repeatedly without the US. The CR declines. Importantly, extinction does not destroy the conditioned connection; it inhibits it, and the original learning is masked by new learning. This is the basis of the phenomenon of **spontaneous recovery** — the reappearance of the CR after a rest interval, sometimes to a substantial proportion of its original strength, without any further pairing.

**Spontaneous recovery** has direct clinical significance. It explains why a young person's fear may return at full strength after a holiday, after an illness, or after a gap between sessions, and why a single successful exposure is not evidence of cure. It is also the reason that the treatment of fear disorders requires *retrieval* work — repeated re-exposure over time — rather than a single in-session success.

**Reinstatement** is the third recovery phenomenon and the most clinically alarming: after extinction, presenting the US once again (a single painful event) can restore the extinguished CR to full strength. A young person whose allergy-related fear has been treated and who then has one severe reaction in the canteen is a reinstatement case, and the clinician should anticipate it and prepare for it.

### 3.3 Generalisation and discrimination

**Generalisation** is the tendency for a response conditioned to one stimulus to be elicited by similar stimuli. A child conditioned to fear the school dentist's chair may fear all chairs, all white coats, all waiting rooms. Generalisation is the reason a single specific fear can organise a wide and generalised pattern of avoidance, and it is the reason that a treatment which only targets the original stimulus may leave the pattern intact.

**Discrimination** is the inverse process, in which the organism learns to respond to one stimulus and not to the others, and it is learned through differential reinforcement. Clinically, discrimination training is used both to *narrow* an over-generalised fear (so that the child responds to the specific stimulus only) and to *broaden* an under-discriminated pattern of behaviour, in which a child cannot tell which of a set of adult instructions is being given.

Generalisation and discrimination are in a functional relation to each other that parallels the relation between the cognitive and behavioural traditions: generalisation supports flexibility but also pathology; discrimination supports precision but also rigidity. Clinical work usually involves moving in both directions at once.

### 3.4 Higher-order and second-order conditioning

A CS can itself be paired with a second neutral stimulus, which then acquires the capacity to elicit the CR. This is **second-order conditioning**, and it is how symbolic and verbal fears arise: a child who is frightened by an animal develops a fear of the word for the animal because the word was presented in the presence of the animal.

Second-order conditioning is of considerable importance with young people because it means that the range of stimuli that can maintain a fear is much larger than the range of stimuli that were directly paired with the aversive event. In a school, a pupil who has been teased about an interest may be conditioned to fear not only the teasing but the word used for the interest, the room in which the teasing occurred, and the appearance of the pupils who did the teasing. The clinician who treats only the original incident will find the fear returning.

### 3.5 Inhibition, overshadowing, and blocking

**Conditioned inhibition** arises when a stimulus is repeatedly presented in the absence of the US, so that it comes to signal safety. In treatment terms, a stimulus that reliably accompanies safety can be used to inhibit fear, and this is the theoretical basis for the "safety signal" component of many exposure-based protocols and for the use of a trusted adult as a co-therapist in the treatment of a young person's avoidance.

Two other phenomena have direct relevance to treatment design:

- **Overshadowing** occurs when a more informative stimulus in a compound comes to dominate learning. In treatment terms, if a young person is exposed to a feared stimulus in the presence of a highly salient alternative stimulus — a crying sibling, a looming deadline, an argument between parents — the alternative will capture the learning, and the exposure will fail to generalise. The practical implication is that exposure must be designed so that the target stimulus is the most informative element in the situation.
- **Blocking** occurs when a prior CS has already been learned, so that a new stimulus in the same compound is blocked from acquiring associative strength. Clinically, blocking explains why a second fear may not be learned, and why a young person with a well-established fear of one thing may appear unmoved by a second aversive experience. It also explains an apparent clinical paradox: a person who is already very anxious may not learn a new fear because the existing learning blocks it.

### 3.6 Counterconditioning

**Counterconditioning** is the procedure in which a CS that has acquired aversive properties through pairing with a US is paired with a stimulus that produces an incompatible response, so that the CS comes to evoke the new response instead. The classic example pairs a fear stimulus with relaxation.

The technique has two historical clinical forms. In **systematic desensitisation** (Wolpe, 1958), the patient is taught to relax, constructs a hierarchy of anxiety-provoking images, and pairs each image with relaxation while moving up the hierarchy. In **aversion conditioning** (also Wolpe), the same principle is used in reverse, pairing an undesirable behaviour with a stimulus that produces an aversive response.

The two forms have sharply divergent evidence bases. Systematic desensitisation has a long tradition of controlled studies and remains a component of many established protocols. Aversion conditioning is used far more sparingly, and its modern use is largely confined to specific applications (for example, in the treatment of specific paraphilias) under tightly controlled conditions. A student who reads Wolpe's original case material without this distinction will badly overestimate the clinical acceptability of aversive techniques with young people, and Chapter 25 returns to the topic with the contemporary evidence and the safeguarding constraints.

## 4. Operant Conditioning

### 4.1 The four contingencies

Operant behaviour is behaviour that operates on the environment and is selected by its consequences. The basic analysis distinguishes four contingencies, and the distinction is defined by two questions: what happens *before* the behaviour, and what happens *after* it.

| Contingency | Consequence | Effect on behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Positive reinforcement | A stimulus is added after the behaviour | Behaviour increases |
| Negative reinforcement | A stimulus is removed after the behaviour | Behaviour increases |
| Positive punishment | A stimulus is added after the behaviour | Behaviour decreases |
| Negative punishment | A stimulus is removed after the behaviour | Behaviour decreases |

![The four operant contingencies arranged in a two-by-two matrix by whether a stimulus is added or removed and whether the behaviour increases or decreases.](fig41_operant_quadrants.png)

The most consequential error in applied practice is the confusion of negative reinforcement with punishment. **Negative reinforcement** increases behaviour: it is the removal of an aversive stimulus contingent on a behaviour, as when a young person is permitted to leave the classroom after saying that they are being bullied. **Negative punishment** decreases behaviour: it is the removal of a valued stimulus contingent on a behaviour, as when a teenager loses phone access after an outburst.

The clarifying statement that a student should be able to make without hesitation is this: reinforcement and punishment are defined by their *effect on the future frequency of the behaviour*, and positive and negative are defined by whether something is added or removed. Nothing in the terminology refers to anything being good or bad.

### 4.2 Reinforcement

A **reinforcer** is a stimulus that, when made contingent on a behaviour, increases the future frequency of that behaviour. Four properties distinguish reinforcers, and the differentiation is practically important in the design of any behavioural intervention.

**Value** is the degree of preference the organism shows for the stimulus, which determines the amount of behaviour that can be acquired and maintained by its delivery. A young person's preference ordering is not fixed and cannot be assumed from developmental stereotypes; it must be established empirically, which in practice means asking the young person what they want, and taking the answer seriously.

**Deprivation** refers to the organism's current state with respect to the stimulus. A reinforcer that is abundant has little value. The clinical significance for adolescents is direct: the substances and behaviours that concern us most are, in the language of the framework, highly valued and reliably deprived, and the most successful programmes for these behaviours are typically those that offer something of competing value.

**Number of and size of reinforcers** determine how much behaviour can be acquired per unit of effort. A small reinforcer delivered frequently can shape behaviour effectively; a large one delivered rarely cannot sustain a dense reinforcement schedule.

**Immediacy** of the consequence is critical. The closer in time the consequence follows the behaviour, the stronger the association. This is why a token system, with its immediate small reinforcers and a delayed backup reinforcer, works considerably better in practice than an abstract promise of a reward next week — and it is one of the strongest arguments for the concrete and immediate in behavioural work with young people.

**Motivating operations** are events that establish or abolish the reinforcing effectiveness of a stimulus. A young person who has been standing outside in the cold for an hour has a high motivating operation for the reinforcer of going inside, and the value of that reinforcer will decline as the young person is warmed. A behaviour that is reinforced while a motivating operation is in effect, and is then left in place after the operation is abolished, will extinguish quickly. This is the mechanism behind a very common practical failure: a behaviour that was effectively reinforced in the school corridor stops being maintained once the underlying need is met, and the young person is then described as "non-compliant."

### 4.3 Punishment

The term carries strong connotations and the literature uses it technically. Four distinctions are essential.

**Positive punishment** is the application of an aversive stimulus contingent on a behaviour. In practice it is used sparingly, and where it is used it is applied immediately, briefly, and with a clear functional purpose.

**Negative punishment**, also called *response cost* or *time-out from reinforcement*, is the removal of a valued stimulus contingent on a behaviour. It is far more common than positive punishment, and its most widely used forms in clinical settings — time-out, response cost, loss of privileges — are the ones that most often produce a *temporary* suppression of behaviour accompanied by an increase in escalation or aggression, because the removal is experienced as arbitrary and the behaviour becomes a protest rather than a choice.

**Punishment by time-out from negative reinforcement** is the withdrawal of the opportunity to escape an aversive situation. It is the mechanism behind many programmes that are described as time-out from demand, and it is different in important respects from time-out from reinforcement.

**Punishment by type II error** is the presentation of a task the individual cannot perform, in order to place the individual in an error-correcting contingency. It is used in some educational settings to teach error correction, but it is rarely appropriate for a young person in distress, and it is a technique with a considerable history of misuse.

Three cautions apply to all punishment. The first is that punishment teaches nothing: it suppresses a behaviour without establishing an alternative, and a suppressed behaviour typically returns when the contingencies change. The second is that punishment does not suppress behaviour that is maintained by negative reinforcement, and in many cases it increases it, because the escalated behaviour produces a more effective escape. The third is that punishment is an ethical event, and the ethical analysis in Section 15 of this chapter treats it as such rather than as a technical one.

### 4.4 Shaping

**Shaping** is the systematic reinforcement of successive approximations to a target behaviour. It is one of the most useful and most under-used techniques in the behavioural repertoire, precisely because it means that a young person can be taught a behaviour that they cannot yet perform.

The procedure requires four things: a clear specification of the target behaviour; a specification of the successive approximations, ordered by difficulty; a defined reinforcement for each approximation, chosen to be immediate and highly salient; and a rule for moving up and for stepping back. The rule for stepping back is what distinguishes competent practice from enthusiasm: if the young person's behaviour deteriorates, the therapist returns to the previously successful approximation and rebuilds from there rather than pushing forward.

Shaping is particularly valuable with young people because it permits the therapist to begin at a level of success rather than at a level of failure. A young person who cannot tolerate the school canteen can begin with tolerating the door, then the smell, then the queue, and each step is reinforced. The "first day at the door" design of behavioural exposure work in Chapter 25 is a specific instance of this principle.

### 4.5 Chaining and prompting

**Chaining** builds a complex behaviour by reinforcing a sequence of component behaviours, in one of three orders: **forward** chaining, in which the first component is reinforced and then each subsequent one; **backward** chaining, in which the last component is reinforced and the earlier ones are prompted; and **middle** chaining. Backward chaining is particularly useful in work with children, because the terminal step of a self-care routine is usually the most reinforcing and the most likely to be completed, and reinforcing it from the outset keeps the young person engaged in a task that is often difficult to start.

**Prompting** is the provision of a stimulus that assists the initiation or performance of a behaviour, with a planned process of fading the prompt. The prompt hierarchy runs from least to most intrusive: a natural cue, a gesture, a verbal direction, a visual prompt, a modelling demonstration, a physical or hand-over-hand prompt, and finally a physical prompt. The fade plan is not optional. A behaviour maintained by a physical prompt is not the behaviour the therapist wanted, and a plan that does not include the fading has not specified the treatment.

### 4.6 Extinction and the resurgence of the operant

**Extinction** in the operant case is the discontinuation of reinforcement. The behaviour declines, in most cases rapidly, and then — as in the classical case — it can return spontaneously, and it can return at a higher rate than before extinction if the behaviour is put into a different context. **Resurgence** is the term for this phenomenon: behaviour that has been extinguished in one context reappears when the organism is returned to a context in which the behaviour was previously reinforced.

Resurgence is an argument for planning maintenance rather than assuming that a behaviour that has stopped will stay stopped. A young person who has stopped hitting when calm but is returned to a setting with unpredictable provocation may resume, and the clinician should plan for that prospectively.

## 5. The Law of Effect and the Problem of Reinforcer Value

### 5.1 The law of effect in its modern form

The modern statement of the law of effect is a single sentence with a great deal of practical content:

> Behaviour followed by a consequence that the organism finds reinforcing will, other things equal, increase in frequency.

Three clauses in that sentence do a great deal of work. "Other things equal" concedes that other variables — the organism's state, competing contingencies, the immediacy and salience of the consequence — also matter. "Finds reinforcing" makes the consequence relative to the organism, not absolute. And "increase in frequency" makes the law a claim about future behaviour, which is what makes it testable and what makes it a criterion of success rather than a description of what happened.

### 5.2 The problem of reinforcer value

The problem is that the analysis of reinforcement is circular in a way that has never been entirely resolved. A reinforcer is defined by its effect on behaviour, and behaviour is observed in the context of a reinforcement history that includes the reinforcer. The standard approaches to the problem are each partial.

**The functional analysis of reinforcer value** establishes it empirically, by observing which stimuli the young person actually works for, and by comparing the rate of responding under different conditions. This is the most rigorous approach available and it is entirely appropriate in settings where behaviour is frequent, clear, and consequential — which is the situation in most applied behaviour analysis work with severe behaviour.

**The reinforcer assessment** approach uses a standardised instrument, typically the *Questionnaire on Resources and Reinforcers* or a comparable measure, in which the young person or their carers rate the value of candidate reinforcers. It is efficient and it is a good first step, but it is self-report, and the literature on self-report with young people, and on the discrepancy between what young people say they want and what they work for, is not reassuring.

**The established-preference** approach relies on the young person's history, which is useful for the obvious and uninteresting reason that a young person will work for something they have worked for before.

A reasonable practitioner combines the three, treats the combination as provisional, and — this is the clinically important step — **tests the reinforcer rather than assuming it**. The child who appears to be working for the reward chart is working for the attention that delivering the chart requires, and the discovery of that fact is itself a clinical event.

### 5.3 Reinforcement and motivation are not the same thing

A further distinction is worth making, because the literature is often read as if it were not. Reinforcement determines what behaviour is *selected*. Motivation determines whether the person is in a state in which the behaviour can be performed at all. A young person who is exhausted, hungry, or in an environment where the reinforcer is unavailable may be perfectly capable of performing the behaviour and simply not be in a state to do it. A behavioural plan that ignores state will fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the analysis of the contingencies.

This is the point at which the behavioural tradition and the person-centred tradition meet most constructively. Rogers' concept of the "person's frame of reference" and the behaviourist's concept of the "motivating operation" are not the same idea, but they are not incompatible, and a therapist who holds both will practise better with young people than one who holds either.

## 6. Measuring Behaviour

### 6.1 Why measurement is not a technicality

The measurement question is where the behavioural tradition is most distinctive and most instructive. Behavioural therapists do not ask how the young person is doing; they ask what the behaviour looks like, how often it occurs, how long it lasts, and what it is contingent on. The reasons are practical rather than philosophical.

A behaviour defined in terms of a global impression cannot be tracked across sessions, cannot be shared with a teacher or a parent, and cannot be evaluated. A behaviour defined as an operationally countable behaviour can. In applied work this is the difference between an opinion and a data series, and in a court or a school meeting it is the difference between an assertion that can be tested and one that cannot.

The discipline also has to be honest about the limits of counting, and three of them deserve emphasis.

**Behaviour is not the person.** A count of tantrums is a count of a pattern of behaviour, and it is entirely possible for a young person to show a marked reduction in a counted behaviour and a marked deterioration in their overall state. Behavioural practitioners who are only counting have made a serious error.

**Frequency is not the only dimension.** Frequency, latency, duration, and intensity are separate dimensions, and a change in one may not be accompanied by change in another. A young person who is expelled from class twice as often but for shorter episodes may be in a worse position, and a young person whose tantrums have dropped in frequency but doubled in intensity has had a more concerning outcome, not a better one.

**Observer agreement is essential.** The interobserver agreement statistic — the extent to which two independent observers code the same behaviour identically — is the basic reliability check of the whole enterprise, and a student should be able to state what a low agreement figure means and what should be done about it.

### 6.2 The ABC model and its limits

The **ABC model** — Antecedent, Behaviour, Consequence — is the standard observational framework. The Antecedent records what immediately precedes the behaviour, the Behaviour records the behaviour in operationally defined terms, and the Consequence records what follows. Data sheets built on the ABC model are the workhorse of applied behaviour analysis and the entry point to the functional assessment in Chapter 24.

The ABC model is also a partial representation of a functional analysis, and the limitation is worth stating. The ABC model describes a *sequence*. A functional analysis makes a *claim about function*: that the behaviour occurs in order to produce the consequence, and that the occurrence of the consequence is what maintains it. The distinction is the difference between a description and a hypothesis, and a student should be careful not to conflate them.

## 7. Behaviour Therapy as a Frame

### 7.1 The unifying features

Behaviour therapy is not a single technique. It is a frame, and the elements that hold it together are worth stating, because they are what allow the widely varying interventions of Part IV to be discussed together.

1. **A functional rather than a symptomatic conception of the problem.** The question is what the behaviour does, not what it is called. This is the feature that most distinguishes the behavioural approach from the diagnostic approaches that precede it.
2. **A commitment to measurement.** If the intervention works, the data will show it, and the data are collected in advance.
3. **A preference for experimentally verifiable claims.** The tradition is unusually sceptical of claims that cannot be tested, and this scepticism is one of its contributions to the discipline.
4. **A distrust of inferred inner causes.** The clinician is asked to specify the conditions rather than to posit a private cause.
5. **A set of technologies.** Shaping, chaining, prompting, fading, contingency mapping, and the schedules of reinforcement are transferable across conditions, and they are what makes the approach feel different from a collection of tips.

### 7.2 The methods of the behavioural therapies

The behavioural therapies are typically organised around a method rather than around a technique. Exposure therapy is a method for treating avoidance and fear by arranging the systematic confrontation with the feared stimulus under controlled conditions. Applied behaviour analysis is a method for understanding the function of a behaviour and then altering the contingencies that produce it. Behavioural activation is a method for treating depression by scheduling activity and monitoring the effect of the schedule on mood. Contingency management is a method for altering the reinforcement structure of a setting — a classroom, a ward, a family — so that the desired behaviour is more efficiently reinforced than the undesired behaviour.

The organising principle of all four is the same: **change the conditions, and let the behaviour follow**.

### 7.3 What the frame cannot do alone

Three examples illustrate the limits.

A young person who has not attended school for four months because of panic does not respond to a contingency plan, because the behaviour is maintained by escape and by the relief of not going, and the contingencies that would maintain attendance are contingent on her being there. The behaviour has to change first, and the technique that gets her through the door is exposure, and the technique that keeps her there is a contingency plan, and neither is sufficient alone.

A young person who has been sexually abused is not exhibiting a learned behaviour that can be extinguished, and an attempt to do so will cause harm. The clinical literature on the misuse of behavioural techniques with trauma survivors is unambiguous on this point.

A family whose members reinforce one another's symptoms will continue to do so, and the work has to be directed at the family system rather than at the identified patient, which is the argument of Part V.
