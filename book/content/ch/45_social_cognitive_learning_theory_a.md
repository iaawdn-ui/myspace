# Social Cognitive Learning Theory

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain Bandura's move from social learning theory to social cognitive theory.
- Describe reciprocal determinism among person, behaviour, and environment.
- Define self-efficacy and identify its four sources.
- Distinguish outcome expectations, goals, self-regulation, and observational learning.
- Apply social cognitive theory to academic behaviour, peer influence, aggression, health, digital life, and therapeutic change.
- Design interventions that build mastery rather than merely encourage confidence.
- Evaluate limitations, cultural considerations, and ethical concerns.

## Chapter Overview

Albert Bandura transformed learning theory by insisting that people are not merely shaped by rewards and punishments. They observe, anticipate, interpret, set goals, judge their capabilities, regulate themselves, and alter the environments that alter them. Social cognitive theory therefore bridges behavioural and cognitive traditions while retaining a strong interest in social context.

For adolescent counselling, the theory is exceptionally practical. Young people learn from peers, siblings, influencers, teachers, fictional characters, and adults who may not know they are modelling. They avoid tasks not only because they lack skill but because they expect humiliation. They repeat behaviour because it produces status. They may know what to do yet lack confidence, opportunity, feedback, or a setting in which the behaviour is safe.

## 1. From Modelling to Agency

Bandura's early experiments demonstrated observational learning: children who saw a model behave aggressively could reproduce novel aggressive acts, especially when the model was rewarded or held status. Later theory expanded beyond imitation. Human agency involves intentionality, forethought, self-reactiveness, and self-reflectiveness.

People act as contributors to their own development. But agency is not unlimited free will. A young person in poverty, unsafe housing, discriminatory schooling, or coercive relationships has real constraints. Social cognitive theory is strongest when the reciprocal model includes those constraints rather than converting every outcome into a test of personal belief.

## 2. Reciprocal Determinism

Personal factors (beliefs, affect, biology, knowledge), behaviour, and environment influence one another bidirectionally.

A student believes she is “bad at maths” (person). She avoids practice and hides confusion (behaviour). The teacher gives less feedback and peers become the audience for jokes about failure (environment). The belief strengthens. Intervention can enter at any point: mastery practice, teacher feedback routines, peer grouping, success experiences, or reinterpretation of difficulty.

This model prevents simplistic counselling. Telling the student to “believe in yourself” addresses one corner while leaving task design and classroom humiliation intact.

## 3. Observational Learning

Observational learning requires attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation.

**Attention** depends on salience, status, similarity, emotional arousal, and complexity. Adolescents often attend more to peers and online creators than to adults.

**Retention** involves symbolic coding and rehearsal. A young person may remember a humiliating comeback word for word and forget safety instructions.

**Reproduction** requires capability and opportunity. Watching assertiveness is not the same as having language, timing, and social permission to use it.

**Motivation** depends on observed and expected consequences. If aggression produces laughter and status, moral instruction alone competes poorly.

Counselling can therefore select models carefully: credible peers, coping models who struggle and improve, video self-modelling, teacher modelling of repair, and parent modelling of calm disagreement.

## 4. Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy is belief in one's capability to organise and execute actions required for a specific task in a specific context. It is not general self-esteem. A student may have high efficacy for football and low efficacy for speaking in class.

![Sources of self-efficacy: mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological or affective states.](fig71_self_efficacy.png)

### 4.1 Mastery experiences

Successful performance is the strongest source, especially when success follows effort on a meaningful challenge and is attributed to strategy and capability. Tasks must be graded. Artificially easy praise produces fragile confidence.

### 4.2 Vicarious experience

Seeing someone similar succeed raises efficacy; seeing similar peers fail can lower it. Similarity matters: age, background, disability, gender, prior attainment, and social identity may affect identification.

### 4.3 Verbal persuasion

Encouragement helps when credible, specific, and paired with attainable steps. Empty reassurance can backfire after failure.

### 4.4 Physiological and affective states

Anxiety, fatigue, pain, excitement, and calm are interpreted as information about capability. Young people can learn to distinguish arousal from inability: a racing heart before speaking may signal importance, not inevitable failure.

## 5. Outcome Expectations, Goals, and Self-Regulation

People ask not only “Can I do it?” but “What will happen if I do?” Positive outcome expectations may involve grades, status, safety, belonging, money, freedom, or adult trust. Negative expectations may involve ridicule, punishment, exclusion, or loss of identity.

Goals organise behaviour when they are specific, challenging but attainable, proximal, and connected to feedback. Distal goals such as “get into college” rarely regulate a tired Tuesday evening. Proximal goals—complete two problems, attend first lesson, revise for fifteen minutes—create mastery information.

Self-regulation includes self-observation, judgement against standards, and self-reaction. Diaries, apps, checklists, teacher feedback, and self-graphing make progress visible. Standards should be developmental and humane; perfectionistic self-monitoring can become another source of shame.
