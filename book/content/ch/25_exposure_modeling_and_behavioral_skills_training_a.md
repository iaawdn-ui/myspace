# Exposure, Modeling, and Behavioral Skills Training

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the origin of the exposure therapies in the learning theory of fear, and describe the evidence that overturned the psychoanalytic account of phobia.
- Distinguish the exposure-based procedures from the behavioural therapies that preceded them, and state the single principle on which all of them rest.
- Describe the common elements of contemporary exposure protocols, including the identification of the feared stimulus, the construction of a hierarchy, and the planning of the first session.
- Explain the varieties of exposure — in vivo, imaginal, interoceptive, and virtual — and state the indications and limitations of each.
- Describe the rules of exposure practice and explain the rationale for each.
- Explain the "first day" design and justify its use with young people.
- Describe modelling, the conditions under which it works, and the ways in which it can be adapted for young people.
- Describe behavioural skills training, its four components, and its relation to the cognitive and behavioural traditions.
- Apply exposure and skills training to a case involving a young person, and evaluate the ethical constraints on the work.

## Chapter Overview

The exposure therapies are the most successful behavioural interventions in the history of the profession, and the most successful single psychological treatment for anxiety disorders. They emerged from a specific theoretical dispute, and understanding the dispute is the key to understanding the treatment.

The psychoanalytic account of phobia held that the phobia was a symbol — an intrapsychic conflict displaced onto a stimulus that happened to be present. The learning account held that the phobia was an *acquired* fear, produced by the pairing of the feared stimulus with aversive events, and that the fear was maintained because the person avoided the stimulus and thus never learned that it was safe. The treatment followed directly from the account: the fear would be unlearned by the systematic re-presentation of the feared stimulus without the aversive event, in conditions in which avoidance was prevented.

For fifty years, the two accounts produced different treatments and different predictions. The decisive evidence came from the experimental comparison of exposure and psychoanalytic interpretation in the treatment of phobias, and from the observation that a behavioural treatment producing no insight whatever could abolish a phobia in a small number of sessions. The insight that the field took from this was methodological rather than theoretical: a treatment that works changes the standing of the theory that explains it, and the theory of phobia had been wrong.

The principles are simple. The feared stimulus must be presented. The contingency between the presentation and the aversive event must be broken, so that the person learns that the feared outcome does not occur. The avoidance must be prevented, because avoidance is what maintains the fear. The presentation must be repeated, because one success is not learning. And the work must be done by the person themselves, because a fear that is endured in the presence of a therapist is not the same fear as one endured alone.

This chapter presents the exposure therapies, the modelling and skills-training methods, and the adaptations that young people require, together with the ethical constraints that the work imposes. It is a chapter in which the technical detail matters more than usual, because the details are what make the treatment safe.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 Desensitisation and the imagery dispute

Joseph Wolpe's *Psychotherapy Reciprocity* appeared in 1958, and the technique it described — systematic desensitisation — was the first behavioural treatment of a specific phobia to be subjected to controlled evaluation. Wolpe's argument drew directly on the conditioning literature: a patient was taught to relax, was asked to imagine the least anxiety-provoking scene on a constructed hierarchy, and was taught to replace the anxiety response with the relaxation response as the hierarchy was ascended. The treatment was radical in its time, because it proposed that a disorder with a fifty-year history of psychological interpretation could be treated by a procedure that took no account of the patient's inner life.

It was also a compromise, and the compromise turned out to matter. Wolpe retained the imagery technique of Jacobson, and the use of imagined scenes rather than actual ones is the element of the treatment that later work identified as its principal weakness: a young person can imagine a scene of the canteen and relax, and can therefore be taught to relax while thinking about the canteen, and can do all of this while still refusing to enter the canteen. The avoidance is intact and the fear is intact. The treatment produced improvement in the laboratory and inconsistent results in the field.

### 1.2 The exposure movement

The 1960s and 1970s saw the gradual replacement of imaginal desensitisation by **in vivo exposure**, in which the feared stimulus is confronted in its actual context. The shift was driven by three developments.

**The recognition of the importance of avoidance.** Clinical observation showed that the patients who improved most were those who did the things they had been avoiding, and that the patients who could imagine their way through a hierarchy without confronting the real stimulus did not maintain their gains.

**The development of behavioural rehearsal and homework**, which moved the confrontation out of the consulting room and into the young person's week. A fear treated only in the consulting room is a fear treated in an unusual context, and it will not generalise.

**The development of the self-administered exposure** in which the young person constructs and executes their own hierarchy, with the therapist as consultant.

### 1.3 The decisive evidence

The comparison of behavioural and psychoanalytic treatment in the 1960s and 1970s produced results that the profession could not ignore, and the results were consistent: the behavioural treatment produced markedly better outcomes on measures of the symptom itself, on measures of the functional impairment, and on the follow-up.

The reason the findings were so decisive is worth understanding, because it is a general methodological lesson. The comparison was made possible by the fact that both treatments could be specified in enough detail to be delivered consistently, and by the fact that the outcome measures were anchored to the symptom rather than to the process. A treatment that does not relieve the symptom does not work, whatever its theory of change, and the discipline's willingness to measure the symptom rather than the theory was what allowed the evidence to accumulate.

### 1.4 The modern position

Contemporary exposure practice is a synthesis, and the synthesis is more sophisticated than any of its components. The following elements are now standard.

**The behavioural experiment.** The treatment is framed explicitly as an experiment in which the young person is testing a belief, and the therapist's role is to help design the test so that the belief is genuinely put at risk. This framing comes from the cognitive tradition and it has become the dominant way of delivering exposure.

**The alliance and the collaborative protocol.** Modern practice does not impose an exposure on a young person. The hierarchy is constructed collaboratively, the young person determines the pace, and the therapist's task is to make the exposure as safe and as tolerable as the evidence allows while not letting the young person avoid it.

**The modern protocols.** For panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder, the field now has structured protocols with detailed manuals, and the outcome of a course of exposure therapy is markedly better when a manualised protocol is followed.

**The extension to complex cases.** Exposure therapy is now used routinely with children, with young people with developmental difficulties, and with people whose phobia is embedded in a complicated family or school context, and the adaptations are well described.

## 2. The Core Principles

Five principles run through all of the exposure-based treatments, and a student should be able to state them and explain the rationale for each.

**The feared stimulus must be encountered.** A fear is learned and maintained by the avoidance of the feared stimulus. The prediction that avoidance makes the fear stronger is the basis of the whole treatment, and it is the reason that a young person's refusal to attend school, or to use the canteen, or to go to the dentist, is the target rather than the problem.

**The feared outcome must not occur.** The exposure must be arranged so that the aversive event that maintains the fear does not happen. This is the principle of *inhibitory learning*: the new learning is that the feared outcome does not occur, and the old learning is that it does. The CS–US association is not erased; it is superseded.

**Avoidance must be prevented.** If the young person is allowed to leave the exposure at the first moment of distress, the avoidance is reinforced, the anxiety is relieved, and the fear is strengthened. This is the point at which the treatment becomes a test of the therapist's resolve as well as of the young person's courage, and where the ethical questions in Section 12 are live.

**The exposure must be repeated.** One successful encounter does not constitute learning. Extinction is fragile, and the evidence from the laboratory and the clinic is that a single exposure produces a large but temporary effect, and that repeated exposures produce a larger and more durable one.

**The young person must do it themselves.** The fear must be confronted in the absence of the safety signals that would otherwise accompany it. This is the point on which the treatments differ most from the classic desensitisation imagery, and it is the point on which modern practice most clearly departs from Wolpe.

Two further points complete the account, and they are refinements rather than principles.

**The learning must be generalised.** The young person's learning should attach to a range of variations, and to the actual contexts in which the fear occurs, not to the therapy room or to the specific therapist.

**The learning must be retrieved.** A single successful exposure is vulnerable to spontaneous recovery, and the treatment must build in retrieval work across time — repeated exposures, in varied contexts, over weeks. The relapse-prevention work of the cognitive tradition and the maintenance phase of the behavioural tradition are the same idea reached from different directions.

## 3. The Varieties of Exposure

### 3.1 In vivo exposure

In vivo exposure is the confrontation with the feared stimulus in its actual physical context, and it is the default for most specific phobias with young people. A young person with a school refusal is taken to school; a young person with a dental phobia is taken to the dentist; a young person with a needle phobia is taken to the clinic.

Its advantages are that it is ecologically valid, that it addresses avoidance in the place where the avoidance occurs, and that it produces generalisation naturally, because the actual context contains all the cues of the actual context and not a reduced set.

Its difficulties are real. It requires planning for every contingency, and it requires the presence of staff who know the plan. It is more taxing on the young person than the alternatives, and it is more taxing on the therapist, because the therapist's anxiety is a legitimate variable in the session and is a reason to prepare carefully rather than a reason to abandon the procedure.

### 3.2 Imaginal exposure

Imaginal exposure uses a carefully constructed set of images, scenes, or narratives, and the treatment is effective in a number of phobias. It is particularly useful where the stimulus is genuinely unavailable — a feared animal, a feared situation that has ended, a feared place that no longer exists — and where the young person can construct vivid images, which is a skill that varies considerably.

Its limitation is the one identified by Wolpe's own successors: the young person can learn to relax while imagining, which is not the same learning as tolerating the real stimulus, and the treatment may therefore produce a change in the imagery response without a change in the avoidance. Where imagery is used, it should be combined with in vivo work, and the young person's own reports of the vividness and the felt response to the images should be monitored.

### 3.3 Interoceptive exposure

Interoceptive exposure deliberately induces the bodily sensations that the young person fears and misinterprets. A young person who believes that a rapid heartbeat means they are about to die is asked to produce a rapid heartbeat, by jumping on the spot, or by running up and down stairs, and is helped to examine what actually happens.

The technique is useful in panic presentations and in some somatic presentations, and it is one of the simplest and most direct illustrations of the behavioural logic: the feared sensation is produced, the feared outcome does not occur, and the young person learns the difference between the sensation and the catastrophe.

### 3.4 Virtual reality and other mediated exposure

Virtual reality exposure is used where the real stimulus is dangerous, scarce, or prohibitively expensive — fear of flying, of spiders, of heights, of social situations. In child practice it is more often used for social anxiety, where a virtual avatar allows rehearsal of difficult interactions, and for medical procedures, where a virtual environment allows rehearsal of a painful or frightening procedure.

The advantages are ecological control, the ability to repeat without real-world risk, and the ability to generalise across virtual contexts. The limitations are the cost and availability of the equipment, the transfer problem — whether learning in a virtual context generalises to the real one — and the specific difficulty with young people who are frightened by the technology itself.

### 3.5 The exposure hierarchy

Whatever the modality, the treatment requires a **hierarchy**, a ranked list of situations from the most tolerable to the most difficult, each with a subjective rating of the expected distress. Three rules govern its construction.

**The hierarchy is built collaboratively and specifically.** A generic "fear of dogs" hierarchy is useless. The hierarchy is built from the young person's own circumstances: which specific dog, in which specific place, at which specific distance.

**The steps are graded, and the first step is chosen to be successful.** A young person who cannot tolerate the first step of their own hierarchy has been given a hierarchy that is too steep, and the answer is to subdivide.

**The young person rates the distress at each step**, and the ratings are recorded. The rating is both a measure of the progress and the mechanism by which the young person learns that the distress has a shape, rises to a peak, and then falls — which is one of the most useful things a young person can learn about their own fear.

### 3.6 The first-day design

The **first-day** design is the specific contribution of the behavioural tradition to work with young people, and it is the most important developmental adaptation in this chapter.

The design begins with a single, planned, and bounded exposure to the most tolerable version of the feared stimulus, on the first day of treatment, before any substantial assessment, homework, or psychoeducation has been attempted. The young person is asked to do the thing they have been avoiding, in the smallest possible dose, and to report afterwards what happened.

The rationale is developmental and is stated in three claims.

**The young person who has never done the feared thing has no evidence about it.** A fourteen-year-old who has not attended school for four months has no idea what will happen, and the anticipation is invariably worse than the reality. The first-day design supplies the missing evidence.

**A success produces a treatment alliance that no amount of explanation can produce.** The first session often ends with the young person reporting something close to disbelief. That report is the most powerful single event in the early phase of therapy, and it is available only by doing the thing.

**It is diagnostic.** The young person's response to the first-day exposure tells the counsellor more about the maintaining contingencies than an extended assessment would, because it reveals what the young person does when the avoidance is broken.

The design has ethical requirements that must be respected. The exposure must be genuinely small. It must be planned, with the young person's consent to the specific plan and with an explicit understanding of what will happen. There must be a plan for what happens if the young person cannot cope, which means that the counsellor must be willing to end the session, and must be present to end it. And the first-day exposure must not be a bluff, and must not be a test of whether the young person is "ready".

## 4. Rules of Exposure Practice

Modern manualised protocols specify the rules of exposure practice in detail, and the rules are the treatment. The core elements are as follows.

**Repeated, planned exposure to the feared stimulus.** Not a single graded session, but a programme of exposures distributed across the treatment.

**A clearly specified goal for each session**, agreed with the young person. "Stay in the corridor for five minutes and speak to one person" is a goal. "Do some exposure" is not.

**Reminders of the feared outcome.** A surprising number of young people find that the feared outcome does not occur, but that they have never actually checked. The exposure protocol includes a planned check of the feared outcome, and this is one of the most important elements of the modern protocols, because it directly targets the *misappraisal* that maintains the fear.

**Repetition of the successful exposure.** The young person is asked to repeat the successful exposure, often in a slightly harder form, because a single success can be attributed to chance or to the presence of the therapist, and the attribution must be dislodged.

**Prevention of avoidance and safety-seeking.** The young person is asked to give up safety behaviours — the held breath, the reassurance-seeking, the companion, the escape route — and this is often the hardest and most important element.

**Work on the feared outcome and the feared self-image.** The young person is helped to examine what the feared outcome would mean, and what it would mean about them if it occurred, and the cognitive work of the exposure protocol is directed at these two targets.

**Attention to the young person's own predictions.** The young person is asked to predict their distress beforehand and to compare the prediction with what occurred. The comparison of prediction with outcome is the mechanism that produces the learning, and it is why the therapist is a consultant rather than a director.

**Grading the difficulty.** The exposure is graded so that it is difficult but achievable, and the young person's response is used to recalibrate. Repeatedly assigning a task that is far beyond the young person's capacity produces abandonment; repeatedly assigning one that is easy produces no learning.

**Homework between sessions.** The work continues outside the session, and the homework is the point at which the generalisation occurs.

**No pharmacotherapy dependence for the exposure work.** Where medication is used for a co-occurring condition, the protocol is designed so that the exposure is not the thing that the medication is doing.

## 5. Modelling

### 5.1 The principle and the evidence

**Modelling** is the demonstration of a behaviour by another person, with the expectation that the observer will acquire or perform the behaviour. Bandura's social learning theory established three mechanisms by which it works: **attention** to the model, **retention** of the modelled behaviour, and **reproduction** of the behaviour, which requires the physical and cognitive capacity to perform it and the motivation to attempt it.

The demonstrations that established the phenomenon are among the most cited in the psychology literature. Vicarious reinforcement — the increase in the observer's performance of a behaviour when the model is reinforced for it — was demonstrated with children and aggressive behaviour, with the striking result that the children reproduced the model's aggressive behaviour even when the model's aggression was punished rather than rewarded. The Bobo doll studies established that children reproduce novel aggressive behaviours they have only seen modelled, and that the reproduction is reduced when the model is punished.

The clinical application followed, and the **modelling** and **participant modelling** procedures are now established components of treatment for phobias, social anxiety, assertiveness deficits, and a range of social skills difficulties. In **participant modelling**, the therapist models the feared behaviour and then guides the young person through it, in a graded sequence, with the therapist present and then progressively less present. In **co-participant modelling**, the model is a peer or a member of the group.

### 5.2 Modelling with young people

The adaptation of modelling for young people has some features that are worth stating.

**The model must be credible.** Children are acute judges of whether a model is competent, and a therapist modelling a task they are not good at is worse than useless. Where the therapist is not an appropriate model, a peer, a teacher, or a video is used.

**Multiple models are better than one.** A young person who has seen several different people perform the feared behaviour has more options available to them as a model.

**The modelling must be accompanied by information.** The observed behaviour is more likely to be adopted when the young person understands what is being modelled and what the expectations are.

**The fear must be addressed in the modelling itself.** In the treatment of a specific phobia, the model demonstrates the feared behaviour *while remaining calm*, and the demonstration of the discrepancy between the young person's prediction and the model's actual experience is the therapeutic element.

**Peer modelling has particular value with social anxiety and with school refusal**, because the peer can demonstrate that the feared interaction is survivable in a way that an adult cannot.

### 5.3 Behavioural rehearsal

**Behavioural rehearsal** is the practice of the behaviour in a graded sequence, in the presence of supportive feedback, before the young person attempts it in the natural setting. It is the component that makes modelling into treatment, because it is the transfer of the demonstrated behaviour into the young person's own repertoire.

Rehearsal has three components that should be distinguished.

**Instruction** — the explicit teaching of the behaviour, including a rationale and a script. This is the component that most clearly joins the behavioural and the cognitive traditions.

**Rehearsal** — the performance of the behaviour in a low-risk setting, with feedback.

**Homework** — the performance in the natural setting, which is the generalisation step.

The distinction matters because the three components fail differently. A young person can have a perfect rationale and fail at rehearsal; a young person can rehearse perfectly and fail at homework; and a young person can succeed at homework and not maintain it, if the homework is not repeated.

### 5.4 Cognitive behavioural rehearsal

The modern version adds the cognitive component explicitly: the young person rehearses not only the behaviour but the *belief* that the behaviour is built on, and the sequence is deliberately run in the "worst case" form. A young person who believes that any question from a teacher means they are in trouble rehearses a range of teacher questions, including hostile ones, and the point is that the rehearsed scenarios are survivable.

## 6. Behavioural Skills Training

### 6.1 The four components

**Behavioural skills training** is a structured approach to the teaching of a specific interpersonal or self-management skill, and it has four components, which are usually taught in a fixed order.

1. **Instruction** — an explicit description of the target skill, in concrete behavioural terms, with a rationale, and often with a model of the behaviour. The instruction is specific: not "be more assertive" but "when your mother says you're going to bed at nine, say 'I've still got homework' in a calm voice and don't raise it to a shout."

2. **Modelling** — a demonstration of the skill, by the therapist, by a peer, or on video. The model demonstrates both the behaviour and the calmness, which is the point.

3. **Rehearsal** — the young person's own performance of the skill, with coaching and feedback, repeated until the performance is adequate.

4. **Homework** — the performance of the skill in the natural setting, between sessions, with the recording of attempts and outcomes.

The order is not arbitrary. Instruction without rehearsal produces a young person who can describe the skill and cannot perform it; rehearsal without homework produces a skill that exists only in the room.

### 6.2 The skills that are taught

The most common targets in work with young people are: **basic interpersonal skills** (conversation initiation, asking for help, saying no, disagreeing, apologising); **assertiveness**; **anger-management skills**, including the recognition of the early physiological signs of arousal and the use of a response delay; **problem-solving**; **social skills** including the interpretation of others' nonverbal cues; **emotion-regulation skills**; and **study and organisational skills**.

The selection of the target skill should be a clinical decision, and the criteria are straightforward: the skill should be **specific** enough to be taught and observed; it should be **functional** in the young person's life; it should be **attainable** in the developmental context; and the young person should have a reason for acquiring it. A skill that the young person does not want is not acquired.

### 6.3 The relationship to other traditions

Behavioural skills training is where the behavioural and cognitive traditions are most completely integrated, and a student should be able to trace the components. The **instruction** component is a cognitive intervention, in that it works on the young person's attributions and expectations about the skill. The **modelling** component is social learning theory. The **rehearsal** component is behavioural rehearsal with in-session feedback, and its efficacy depends on the behavioural principle that behaviour is selected by its consequences, which is why the feedback must be immediate and specific. The **homework** component is generalisation training.

What behavioural skills training does not do is deal with the underlying motivation, the underlying relationship, or the underlying developmental context. A young person who is taught assertiveness and who lives in a home where assertiveness is punished has acquired a technique that will be extinguished, and the practitioner who stops at the skills training has not finished the work.
