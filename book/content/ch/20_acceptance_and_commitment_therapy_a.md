# Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the development of acceptance and commitment therapy and explain the context of the debate in which it emerged.
- Explain functional contextualism and relational frame theory at the level required to use the therapy competently.
- Define cognitive fusion, the struggle switch, and the experiential avoidance cycle, and explain the mechanism each describes.
- State the six processes of psychological flexibility and the ACT hexaflex.
- Describe the creative hopelessness perspective and explain its clinical function.
- Describe the principal ACT exercises and state their indications and cautions.
- Apply ACT with children and adolescents, and specify the developmental modifications.
- Evaluate the evidence for ACT and for its components.

## Chapter Overview

Acceptance and commitment therapy was developed by Steven Hayes and his colleagues in the 1980s, and it emerged from a specific and still-running argument within the behaviour therapy movement. The argument was the "acceptance versus change" debate: is a therapeutic success the reduction of symptoms, or is it the construction of a valued life? The behaviour therapy movement had historically been on the side of symptom reduction, and Hayes argued that this commitment had produced a quiet and damaging orthodoxy — the assumption that unwanted internal experiences are medical symptoms to be eliminated, and that a client who has a symptom has a disorder that must be treated before anything else can happen.

Hayes' position, which was and remains unfashionable in parts of the field, is that the struggle to control unwanted thoughts and feelings is itself the mechanism that maintains the problem, and that the answer is not to win the struggle but to stop fighting. The therapeutic task is to develop **psychological flexibility** — the capacity to remain in contact with the present moment and with the person one is, and to act in accordance with one's values, while unwanted internal experiences are present.

For a graduate student in a course on counseling young people, the theory is of interest for three reasons. It is the most fully developed of the third-wave approaches, and it is therefore the theory most likely to be used in a contemporary service. It contains an unusually well-specified account of the relationship between the practitioner's conduct and the young person's development, in the "five senses of the therapist's behaviour." And it offers a specific answer to the question that most often defeats a new therapist working with an adolescent — what do I do when the young person will not do the thing that will help them?

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The acceptance–change debate

The debate within behaviour therapy had three positions. The **traditional position** was that symptom reduction was the goal, and that symptom reduction was necessary before anything more important could be addressed. The **exposure-based position**, developed in the 1980s by the clinical researchers around the Centre for the Treatment of Fear at the National University of Ireland in Galway, was that a client must approach the feared internal and external stimuli and learn to tolerate them, and that the experience of tolerating them was itself therapeutic, and that avoidance was the problem. The **acceptance position**, advanced by Hayes, was that the trying to control thoughts and feelings was itself the problem, and that the attempt to eliminate symptoms was frequently an avoidance in itself.

The 1990s and 2000s saw the acceptance position become dominant, in part because of the empirical findings of the comparative trials — the acceptance-based treatments generally produced outcomes at least as good as the symptom-focused treatments on the symptoms, and better on the measures of well-being and of functional outcomes.

### 1.2 Hayes' own development

Hayes' path to the theory was unusual and is worth noting, because it explains the theory's character. He was trained in behaviour analysis and spent the early part of his career working on the reduction of self-destructive behaviour, including the treatment of chronic pain, and he was a vigorous opponent of the medical model of chronic pain and of the assumption that the treatment of a "symptom" was the treatment of the problem.

His interest in language and cognition began with his work on the believability of thoughts and on the treatment of depression, and it developed into the relational frame theory, which is a formal account of how human beings relate to language and to each other. The therapy followed from the theory, and the theory followed from the clinical observation that people become trapped in their own struggle with their own minds.

### 1.3 The development of the model

The 1980s and 1990s saw the development of the model, the metaphor of the tug-of-war, the six core processes, and the first controlled trials. The 2000s saw the expansion of the evidence base, the development of the "five senses" account of the therapist's behaviour, the integration with the somatic and trauma fields, and the diffusion of ACT's ideas through the field in the form of acceptance-based behaviour change, which is a more general framework than the therapy.

## 2. The Theoretical Foundations

### 2.1 Functional contextualism

The philosophical position of the therapy is **functional contextualism**, and a student should be able to state it because it explains a great deal of the practice.

Functional contextualism holds that behaviour is best understood in terms of the function it serves in a context, and that the appropriate unit of analysis is the whole person-in-context in relation to a specific problem. It is committed to a metaphysics of practical science: it suspends the question of what is ultimately true in favour of the question of what works, and it is agnostic about the internal causes of behaviour, requiring only that they be operationally defined if they are used explanatorily.

The practical consequence is that ACT asks what is the young person doing, and what is the behaviour accomplishing, rather than what is happening inside them. It is a theory of behaviour in the broadest sense, and it is why the theory has been able to incorporate so many of the field's other findings.

### 2.2 Relational frame theory

The formal account of language is the **relational frame theory**, and a student does not need its details but does need its central claim.

The claim is that human beings learn to relate to words, and to the relations between words, and to the functions of those relations, and that this capacity — rather than any particular content — is what makes the human mind distinctive and what makes a great many psychological problems possible. Specifically, the theory proposes that the capacity to derive the meaning of one event from another is the platform on which the entire behavioural repertoire of the species is built, and that the same platform on which a solution is built is also on which a problem is built.

The clinical application is the concept of **cognitive fusion**: a state in which the person is entangled with the content of their thoughts, in which the thought and the person who thinks it are treated as one and the same, and in which the person's behaviour is governed by the believability of the thought rather than by what is actually happening.

### 2.3 The struggle switch

The **struggle switch** is the central metaphor of the theory, and it is the most useful single idea in it.

The metaphor is of a man standing in a pool with a big rubber ball, holding it under water. The ball is his unwanted thoughts and feelings; the man is the person; and the water is the struggle itself. The man exerts enormous effort, and the effort is the direct cause of the fatigue, the muscular tension, the exhaustion, and the depressed mood. The man believes that letting go of the ball will mean drowning. The ball pushes up; the man pushes down; and the whole system is sustained by the effort.

The clinical claim is that the attempt to control unwanted internal experiences does not only fail to remove them; it produces a set of predictable side effects — the exhaustion, the constriction of behavioural repertoires, the reduced engagement with valued activity, and the worsening mood — and that these side effects are frequently what brings the young person to treatment.

The clinical implication is direct and it is the therapeutic pivot of the whole approach: **the goal is not to change the content of the thoughts and feelings, but to change the young person's relationship to them.** The metaphor, whatever its own problems, communicates this with an efficiency that no other theory in this book has matched.

## 3. The Processes of Psychological Flexibility

### 3.1 The six processes

**Psychological flexibility** is the central construct, and it has six processes.

**Acceptance** is the process of making room for unwanted private experiences without struggle. It is not approval, not resignation, and not the assertion that the experience is good. It is the recognition that the experience is present and that the fight with it is unnecessary.

**Defusion** is the process of seeing thoughts as thoughts. The experiential avoidance strategy of "if I can stop thinking about this, I will be fine" is replaced with the capacity to notice that a thought is occurring, to hold it lightly, and to let it be present without acting on it. Defusion is not suppression and it is not distraction; it is a change in the functional relationship between the person and the content.

**Contact with the present moment** is the process of engaging with the current experience with full attention, in the here and now, without a filter of abstraction, evaluation, or avoidance.

**The self as context** is the process of holding a perspective on one's experience — a sense of "I am the person who is having this thought" rather than "I am this thought" — which creates the possibility of perspective-taking and of a stable, flexible self.

**Values** are the process of determining, consciously and continually, what matters and of choosing a direction of movement that is meaningful.

**Committed action** is the process of taking sustained, concrete action in the direction of the chosen values, with the specific purpose of changing the world rather than the person.

### 3.2 The ACT hexaflex

The six processes are arranged in a hexagonal configuration, in which the three "open" processes on the left — acceptance, defusion, and contact with the present moment — are the processes that loosen the grip of cognitive fusion, and the three "closed" processes on the right — the self as context, values, and committed action — are the processes that move the person in the direction of a valued life.

The flex is the movement around the hexaflex, and it represents the person moving back and forth between loosening and moving, which is the normal pattern of psychological life. The pathological pattern is a *stuck* position: stuck in the closed processes without loosening (which produces rigidity, over-control, and the avoidance of the present moment) or stuck in the open processes without movement (which produces passivity, drifting, and the failure to act on what matters).

The stuck positions map neatly onto the two most common presentations in youth services, and a practitioner who knows the hexaflex can diagnose them quickly. The rigid, over-controlled, perfectionistic, or hyper-competent young person is stuck on the closed side, in the demanding parent mode of Chapter 19 or the demanding mode of the schema model. The young person who is passive, apathetic, dissociated, or profoundly withdrawn is stuck on the open side, having "accepted" everything and moved nowhere.

### 3.3 The creative hopelessness perspective

The **creative hopelessness** perspective is the alternative to the medical model, and it is the ACT analogue of the "depression is not a disease but a problem" formulation.

The claim is that a great many young people's difficulties consist of a single, coherent, and unsuccessful strategy: they have tried to control their internal experiences, in a variety of ways, for a long time, and the strategy has not worked and has made things worse. The evidence for this is the young person's own history, and the therapist's task is to present it to them.

The technique is the **matrix**, a structured comparison of the attempts to control on the one hand and the costs of those attempts on the other. The therapist asks the young person to complete the "Have you tried..." column, and then the "What did it cost you?" column, and the young person produces the evidence themselves. The creative hopelessness follows, and it is the moment at which the therapy's central pivot becomes available: if controlling has never worked, what else is there?

The clinical significance of the creative hopelessness perspective is that it replaces the moral and the medical framing of a young person's difficulty with a strategic one. The young person is not defective and is not ill; they have been pursuing a strategy that has not worked, and there is another strategy available. This is a very different conversation from the one most young people have had, and it is often the first moment of relief in the treatment.

## 4. The Practice of ACT

### 4.1 Defusion exercises

The defusion exercises are the most characteristic of the ACT techniques, and their function is to demonstrate, experientially, that the content of a thought is not the same thing as the person who is thinking it.

**The "Thank you, mind" or "I'm noticing I'm having the thought that..." exercise.** The young person is asked to notice a thought and to prefix it with the words "I am noticing that I am having the thought that..." The exercise seems trivial and is not: the point is the change in the *relationship* to the thought that the phrasing effects, and the young person frequently experiences a small but real shift, and the practitioner should be careful to notice it and to ask about it.

**The "Tug of war with the mind" metaphor.** The young person is asked to imagine being on one side of a tug-of-war with a personification of their mind, and then to notice what would happen if they dropped the rope. The therapeutic point is the difference between the rope being pulled and the person being dragged. In the classic form, the young person is then invited to notice a second rope, attached to their chest, and to pull that one instead.

**The "Taste it" exercise.** The young person is asked to have the thought and to notice its taste, its texture, its temperature — so that the thought, which is ordinarily experienced as a statement of fact, is experienced as an event. The exercise is very effective with young people who are prone to catastrophising or to rumination.

**The "Milk, milk, milk" exercise.** The young person is asked to say the word "milk" repeatedly until it loses its meaning, and then to notice how long the word takes to regain its meaning. The point is to demonstrate that the meaning of a word is a product of a context, and that a thought's authority is a product of a context too.

### 4.2 The experiential avoidance exercises

**The "Quicksand" metaphor.** The young person is asked to imagine that their difficult thoughts and feelings are quicksand, in which struggling makes it worse and stillness makes it worse too, and that the only workable response is to move slowly. The metaphor is used to establish the acceptance work.

**The "Passengers on the bus" metaphor.** The young person is asked to notice that their thoughts and feelings are like passengers on a bus, and that they are the driver, and that the passengers may be screaming but they do not drive. This is one of the most used and most effective of the ACT metaphors with young people, and it is the one that most directly dismantles the struggle switch.

### 4.3 The values and committed action work

The values work is where ACT departs most sharply from the symptom-focused therapies, and it is also where the developmental adaptation is most needed.

The procedure is: identify what matters; identify the values that are already present in the young person's life; identify the difference between the values and what the young person is currently doing; and then identify the smallest concrete action in the direction of the value.

The question the practitioner must hold throughout is the one the theory insists on: **the question is not "how do I feel about this?" but "what do I do because of this?"** The distinction between feelings and actions is the distinction ACT is built on, and it is also the most clinically useful single question in the therapy with a young person who is stuck.

### 4.4 The "five senses" of the therapist's behaviour

The functional contextual approach specifies the practitioner's conduct in a way that is unusually practical, and it is the part of ACT that most directly addresses the new therapist's central difficulty.

**Evocation.** The practitioner evokes a full range of experience in the young person, including the difficult experience, and does so in a way that allows the difficult experience to be present. The practitioner does not avoid the young person's pain, and does not require the young person to avoid it either.

**Freedom.** The practitioner supports the young person's autonomy, and does so explicitly, with a genuine offer of alternatives and a genuine willingness to be guided by the young person's preferences where these are compatible with the ethical requirements.

**Presence.** The practitioner is fully present, with a stance of curious non-judgement, and treats the young person's experience as a mystery to be explored rather than as a problem to be solved.

**Attunement.** The practitioner attunes to the young person's experience and is willing to be affected by it, and does so without the need to be in control.

**Potency.** The practitioner does things that make a difference, and does so in a way that is responsive to what the young person needs in the moment. This is the element that distinguishes ACT from the non-directive therapies, and it is the one that is most often omitted in practice by therapists who have over-corrected from their earlier training.

The five senses are a description of a stance, and they are also the answer to the question of what a beginning therapist does when the young person will not do the thing that will help them. The answer is: the practitioner is present, is affected, evokes, and does something that makes a difference in this moment.

## 5. ACT with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The developmental fit

The developmental fit of ACT with young people is good, and the reasons are specific.

**It respects the young person's difficulty without pathologising it.** The creative hopelessness perspective says that the young person's strategy has not worked, which is a description of a competent person's attempts rather than of a defective person.

**It works with the developmentally available "I don't have to" of adolescence.** The adolescent's developmental task involves the renegotiation of dependence, and ACT's core move — the distinction between the person and the content of the mind — is a developmental achievement that a young person can learn.

**It is compatible with the behavioural activation tradition.** The committed action work and the values work are, in the appropriate cases, behavioural activation, and the two are compatible in a way that the third wave's rhetoric has sometimes obscured.

**It is compatible with play and with the body.** ACT has been adapted to work with young children through play, and the "passengers on the bus" and "quicksand" metaphors have obvious child versions. The somatic and trauma fields have absorbed ACT's ideas about the difference between sensation and evaluation, and the compatibility is genuine.

### 5.2 The developmental modifications

**The metaphor must be developmentally appropriate.** The "passengers on the bus" metaphor is excellent for a ten-year-old and requires adaptation for a sixteen-year-old who will find it childish. The developmental rule is that the metaphor should be pitched at the edge of the young person's capacity and should be able to be taught to a peer, and the practitioner should ask the young person what they think of it before using it.

**The values work must be developmentally framed.** A fourteen-year-old's values are not a list of abstract principles; they are about specific people, specific activities, and specific futures. The work is therefore concrete: "What matters to you?" becomes "Who do you want to still be able to talk to in a year's time?" and "What would you still be doing if nothing changed in your head?" The last of these is the most useful question in the whole therapy.

**The "smaller, better" approach.** ACT with young people requires the therapist to keep the tasks small, the metaphors accessible, and the pace such that the young person can consolidate one skill before the next is introduced. The adult protocol is a twelve-session course and the developmental version is more often a series of brief engagements, and a practitioner who tries to deliver the protocol to a young person will fail.

**The commitment to the present moment.** ACT's contact with the present moment work is very natural with young people, because the young person's own difficulty is often that they are living in an imagined future or an imagined past, and the developmental task of many of the presentations is precisely the development of a usable future orientation.

**The caution on trauma.** ACT is frequently described as incompatible with trauma-focused work, and the concern is real: the acceptance work can be experienced as the requirement to tolerate an unbearable memory, and the defusion work can be experienced as the dismissal of the reality of the abuse. The resolution is the phase-oriented approach of Chapter 38, in which ACT is used in the stabilisation phase rather than in the processing phase, and in which the young person is never asked to defuse a thought about the abuse but is helped to defuse the "I am permanently broken" conclusion that follows from it.

## 6. Modern Developments

**Acceptance-based behaviour change.** The broader framework, which provides the theoretical basis for a wide range of behavioural interventions in health psychology, education, and organisational practice, has diffused the core ideas throughout the profession. The therapist's language of "how do I feel about this?" becoming "what do I do because of this?" is now widespread.

**ACT in schools and in the community.** A substantial body of work has been done on school-based ACT programmes, and the flexibility of the approach and its compatibility with the brief and preventative models of school work make it particularly well suited to it.

**The integration with the somatic and trauma fields.** The recognition that the acceptance and the exposure approaches are addressing the same thing from different directions — the exposure approach by approaching what is avoided, the acceptance approach by ending the fight with what is present — has led to a productive integration, and the "flexible" exposure work of the trauma field is an obvious descendant of the acceptance work.
