# Focusing-Oriented Therapy and the Felt Sense

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe Eugene Gendlin's intellectual development and explain the events that led him to develop the theory of the felt sense.
- Explain what the felt sense is, how it differs from emotion and from bodily sensation, and why Gendlin claimed it is the foundation of psychological change.
- State the six principles of focusing and give a clinical example of each.
- Describe the five-step focusing procedure and the role of the practitioner in each step.
- Explain what the theory has to say about bodily symptoms and their relation to psychological difficulty.
- Apply focusing with children and adolescents, and specify the developmental modifications required.
- Describe the modern developments, including low-resourced focusing and the integration of focusing with other modalities.
- Evaluate the evidence for focusing and for the felt sense construct.

## Chapter Overview

Eugene Gendlin's discovery was made in the 1940s, in the course of a very unusual set of circumstances, and the story of how it was made is itself instructive. Gendlin was a member of a research team at the University of Chicago studying what happened to patients in psychotherapy, and he noticed something that the rest of the team did not: that a small number of patients reported a particular kind of experience, describable as a wordless, bodily, whole, and yet indefinable sense of their own situation, and that these patients improved, and that the improvement correlated with their capacity to access and to stay with that experience.

The finding was so odd that it was dismissed by the research team. Gendlin spent the next two decades establishing what he had found, and the result was a theory of experiencing that is one of the most important and least known contributions to the field. The theory holds that the felt sense — the bodily, pre-verbal, whole, and undefinable experience of the current moment — is not a symptom, or a by-product, or a doorway to the "real" feelings, but is itself the location in which psychological change occurs. A person who can access and stay with their felt sense has access to the resources for their own change, and a person who cannot is, in a sense, cut off from their own living.

The theory is a natural companion to the somatic and trauma-informed work of Part VI, and it is also a distinctly humane technology: it is teachable in a few sessions, it works with people who cannot articulate, and it respects the young person's own experience in a way that no technique requiring verbal insight can.

This chapter presents the theory in its intellectual context, describes the felt sense and the experiential process, sets out the principles and the procedure, and addresses the developmental and ethical questions.

## 1. Historical and Intellectual Context

### 1.1 The research project

Gendlin trained as a philosopher at the University of Chicago, where he was a member of the philosophy faculty, and he was a colleague of Carl Rogers, whose client-centred work was then in its productive middle period. In 1946 Gendlin was recruited into a research project funded by the Rockefeller Foundation, which was studying the process of psychotherapy at the University of Chicago's Counseling Center. The project was examining, among other things, what made some clients improve and others not, and it was a period in which the research team was deeply invested in the client-centred account of the relationship.

Gendlin noticed that a particular group of clients, a small minority, reported a distinctive experience. They described something they could not put into words, which was located in the body, which was a whole rather than a part, and which was somehow the experience of their own situation rather than of any particular content. These clients were the ones who improved. The finding did not fit the project's framework, and the research team's published conclusions did not include it. Gendlin was, by his own account, told that he had found nothing.

### 1.2 The next two decades

Gendlin's subsequent work was an extended effort to establish the phenomenon empirically and to develop a theory from it, and it is one of the more striking examples of a researcher refusing to abandon a finding that his professional community did not accept. Three strands developed.

**The phenomenological enquiry.** Gendlin and colleagues used phenomenological methods — detailed description of the experience of the moment — to characterise the felt sense and to describe its structure, which he characterised as a triple structure: a bodily location, a sense of a whole, and a sense of a direction or vector of movement. The third element is the one that is most often missed and it is the most useful clinically: the felt sense has a *movement*, an orientation, a sense of where the experience is going, and a practitioner who can help a young person track that movement has found the therapeutic process.

**The experiments.** Gendlin's "focusing experiments" used a single-subject design in which participants were given a specific bodily task — typically a set of sentence stems referring to body sensations, such as "the part of me that..." — and were asked to find the bodily location and the quality of their experience. Participants who located the bodily sense of their situation in the specific way Gendlin described showed measurable changes in self-reported emotional state and in their sense of the situation, and the changes exceeded those produced by other conditions, including conditions in which participants were asked to think about their feelings in a more conventional manner.

**The development of the method.** Focusing was developed as a client-led practice in which the practitioner does not guide the process but creates the conditions in which it can occur.

### 1.3 The tradition

Gendlin's position in the field is unusual: he was a philosopher by training, a researcher by temperament, and a clinician by practice, and he is respected in the humanistic tradition, in the somatic field, in the contemplative traditions, and in the phenomenological philosophy. The theory's specific contribution has been greater than its diffusion, and a student who knows it will find that it supplies a missing element in several of the other theories in this book.

## 2. The Theory of Experiencing

### 2.1 The felt sense

The **felt sense** is the living, bodily, whole, and largely undefinable experience of one's current situation. Gendlin's own description is characteristically precise: it is a sense of the whole of one's situation as it is being lived, which is located in the body, which is felt more than thought, which is vague and difficult to put into words, and which is nevertheless distinguishable from any particular emotion, sensation, or thought.

Four of these features require emphasis, because each distinguishes the felt sense from something it is often confused with.

**It is a sense of the whole.** The felt sense is not an emotion, and it is not a localised sensation. A young person who says "I feel anxious" is reporting an emotion, which has a name and a phenomenology that the emotion recognition literature has studied. The felt sense is what it is like to be in that state, in a way that is more general than the emotion and that includes an implicit sense of the situation and of what is going on. It is the felt "being-in-it".

**It is bodily.** The felt sense is located in the body, and it is experienced as a physical sense of the situation, often in the centre of the body, though not always. Gendlin's experimental method exploited this, and it is the empirical anchor of the theory.

**It is pre-verbal.** The felt sense is typically not in words, and the attempt to put it into words usually results in a change of experience, in which the living quality is replaced by a static concept. This is the practical reason why the focusing method requires that the young person not talk while they are in the felt sense.

**It is undefinable but not vague.** The felt sense is precisely felt; it has a quality that the young person can distinguish from other qualities. It is not analysable, but it is not nebulous either, and a practitioner who is learning the method will find that they can be told when a young person is in the felt sense and when they have moved into intellectualisation.

### 2.2 The claim

Gendlin's claim is the theory's central and most radical proposition, and it should be stated in its strongest form:

**Psychological change happens in the felt sense, and the felt sense is the locus of the person's own resources for change.**

The implication is that a person does not need to be given anything, and does not need to be interpreted, and does not need to acquire a new technique. A person who can access their felt sense can find their own answers in it, and this is why the practitioner in focusing does not direct the process. Gendlin described the practitioner as providing a "special kind of listening" — an attention to the whole of the client's experience, including the bodily and the wordless, and a stance of "being with" rather than of interpreting.

The claim is also the reason the theory is difficult to place in the field's conventional taxonomy. It is a humanistic theory in its respect for the young person's autonomy, a somatic theory in its respect for the body, a phenomenological theory in its epistemology, and a process theory in its account of what therapy is.

### 2.3 Implications

Several implications follow, and they are clinically substantial.

**Symptoms and the felt sense.** A physical symptom, in this account, is the felt sense of a situation that has not been resolved, localised and held in the body. The theory therefore treats somatic complaints as meaningful rather than as either medical or imagined, and it does so in a way that is compatible with the contemporary somatic literature without dismissing the need for medical assessment.

**The primacy of experience over explanation.** A young person who understands what is happening to them may nevertheless be unable to change it; and a young person who does not understand may be able to change. The theory privileges the second case, and its claim is that the felt sense is where change is possible.

**The therapist's stance.** The therapist's role is to provide a specific quality of attention, not to interpret, advise, or direct. This places focusing in a lineage with the person-centred and the phenomenological traditions.

**Bodily attention as the route to emotional processing.** A young person who cannot talk about feelings can be helped to find the bodily sense of the situation, and the emotional work becomes possible from there. This is the theory's most important practical contribution to work with young people.

## 3. The Experiential Process

### 3.1 The structure of the process

Gendlin's account of the experiential process, developed with the help of the philosopher Eugene Boss and others, is the theoretical core of the theory, and it is a model of a mechanism.

The process has three components.

**A felt sense of the whole.** The person's experience of their situation as a whole, in the body, undefinable in words.

**The sense of a shift.** The experience that something is different, that something has changed, that the situation is not as it was. The shift is felt as a movement, a directional change, a sense of lifting or opening or settling, and it is the process element of the experience.

**A change in the sense of the situation.** The result: the person experiences the same situation differently, because the way in which they hold it has changed. Gendlin described the result as a "different sense of the situation" rather than as a new thought or a new feeling.

### 3.2 The process and the self

The theoretical claim connecting the three is that the "self" is not a thing but a process, and that the felt sense is the medium in which the process occurs. Gendlin's account of the self is a process model, and it converges with the person-centred formulation of the person as a process and with the contemporary "person as process" position of Schwartz and colleagues discussed in Chapter 10.

### 3.3 The relationship to other theories

The connections are instructive. The theory's account of the felt sense as the locus of change corresponds to the somatic tradition's account of the body as the site of the affective and the trauma-relevant. Its account of the practitioner's stance as a particular quality of listening corresponds to the person-centred account of empathic understanding. Its account of the self as a process corresponds to the contemporary person-as-process formulations. And its account of the "edge" — the boundary between the felt sense and the conceptual — corresponds closely to the concept of the window of tolerance that is central to the somatic and trauma-informed work of Part VI.

## 4. The Six Principles of Focusing

Gendlin's six principles are the practical core of the method, and a student should be able to state each and give an example of its application with a young person.

**First principle: the felt sense is distinct from the content of the problem.** The young person's difficulty is not the same thing as the felt sense of the difficulty. A young person who is frightened of the school corridor may have a felt sense that is not about the corridor at all, but about something in the corridor that resonates with a long-standing sense of being exposed and judged. The practitioner does not work with the content; the practitioner helps the young person to find the felt sense, and the content changes from the changed sense.

**Second principle: a bodily location.** The felt sense is located in the body, and finding the location is the first practical task. A young person who cannot find a felt sense in a specific bodily place is usually trying to think the feeling rather than find it, and the practitioner's task is to redirect the attention to the body and to the particular.

**Third principle: the felt sense is wordless.** The young person does not need to describe it in order to have it, and describing it in words tends to move the experience out of the felt sense and into conceptualisation. The practitioner must therefore tolerate the young person's silence during a focusing session, and must be able to tell the difference between a productive silence and a withdrawal.

**Fourth principle: the shift is the process.** The work is to find and to stay with the shift — the sense of something changing — rather than to analyse the situation. This is the most counterintuitive and the most important of the principles, because the young person's natural tendency in the session is to analyse, to explain, and to solve, and the whole of the practitioner skill lies in holding the attention on the felt quality.

**Fifth principle: there is a bodily felt sense of a process, and a direction.** The felt sense has a direction, and a practitioner who helps the young person track the direction of the movement is doing the most therapeutic thing available. Gendlin's formulation of the "felt sense of a shift" includes the possibility of the direction being blocked, and the work then is to find what is blocking it, which is often a felt sense of a different kind.

**Sixth principle: there is a difference between the felt sense and the conceptual.** The felt sense is pre-verbal and the conceptual is verbal, and the practitioner must be able to help the young person move back from the conceptual to the felt. The "edge" is the term for the boundary between them, and the "inner edge" is the felt sense itself.

## 5. The Focusing Procedure

### 5.1 The five steps

The standard focusing procedure, which a student should be able to describe, has five steps, and they may be practised in a single session or across several.

**Step 1: Set the frame.** The practitioner establishes the conditions: a quiet setting, a comfortable position, and a statement of what will happen — that the practitioner will listen and will not give advice, and that the young person will be doing the work. The framing is a precondition, because the theory's claim is that the young person has the resources and that the practitioner's task is to make them available.

**Step 2: Find a bodily location of the problem.** The young person is invited to locate the difficulty in the body, using a set of sentence stems if helpful: "The part of me that..." "The place in my chest where..." "The tight feeling in my..." This step is frequently the easiest and frequently produces the first significant shift, because the young person discovers that the difficulty has a location and a size.

**Step 3: Find the felt sense.** The young person is invited to find the quality of the experience, distinct from the emotion and from the thought. The classic instruction is: "Instead of telling me how it is, could you be aware of how it feels?" The practitioner's task is to listen with a particular quality of attention — attending to the whole, including the wordless, and listening for the shift when it occurs.

**Step 4: Stay with the felt sense.** The young person stays with the felt sense, and the practitioner stays with the young person, attending to the direction of the movement and to any sense of blockage. Gendlin described this as a state of "patient waiting" in which the practitioner holds a quality of attention that is warm, wide, and free of interpretation.

**Step 5: Invite the shift.** When the shift occurs, the practitioner may say: "Something is different, isn't it?" or "There's a change there." The acknowledgement of the shift is itself part of the mechanism, because it brings the change into the verbal awareness of the young person and consolidates it.

### 5.2 The practitioner's role

The practitioner's role in a focusing session is distinctive and is often the hardest part of the method for a trainee. The practitioner listens, attends to the whole of the experience, listens for the wordless, tracks the direction of the felt sense, waits patiently, and does not interpret. The temptation to interpret — to say what the felt sense means, to make it meaningful, to connect it to something in the young person's history — is strong, and it is the principal way the method is spoiled.

Gendlin's description of the practitioner's listening as a "special kind" of listening is worth unpacking: the attention is not on the content of what is said but on the quality of the experience as it is lived, and it includes an attention to the body and to the shifts.

### 5.3 Variants

Several variants of the procedure exist, and a student should know them. **Intensive focusing** is a form in which a practitioner guides the process more actively, and it is used with people who have difficulty finding the felt sense on their own. **Low-resourced focusing** is a form in which the process is conducted in a single session and with minimal framing, and it is the most practical version for use in a school or community service with a limited number of sessions. **Focusing with a specific problem** in which the felt sense is located in relation to a particular concern. And **focusing as a practice rather than a technique**, in which the young person is taught the method for their own use, which is the most durable form and the one most consistent with the theory.

## 6. Focusing with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The developmental fit

The developmental fit of focusing with young people is very good, and it is good for reasons that are specific rather than general.

**It does not require verbal sophistication.** The method works with a young person who cannot articulate, and the age-related limits on abstract verbal processing described in Chapter 9 are not obstacles to it. A seven-year-old can locate "the tight bit" in their chest, and the process will work.

**It respects the body's primacy in emotional processing.** The somatic and developmental literatures describe the body as the primary site of the young person's emotional life, and the contemporary consensus in developmental neuroscience is that the body's representations of emotion are available in awareness before the verbal ones are. The focusing method is, in effect, a technique for working with the earliest and most accessible level of emotional representation.

**It respects the young person's autonomy.** A young person who is invited to find their own bodily sense and to follow it is being given an unusual degree of control in a setting in which they usually have very little, and the developmental literature suggests that the experience of control is itself therapeutic.

**It is compatible with play.** Focusing has been adapted into play-based work with young children, and the "felt sense" can be located through sensory and imaginal play rather than through attention to the body, which is developmentally appropriate for children whose bodies are not yet a reliable source of self-attention.

### 6.2 The developmental modifications

**With younger children (approximately 4–8)**, the method is conducted through play, drawing, movement, and imaginal work, and the "felt sense" is located as a place, a colour, a texture, a size, or a character. The words "felt sense" are not used. The shifts are described in movement and image rather than in sensation.

**With pre-adolescents (approximately 8–12)**, the method is conducted in a more recognisable form, with body scanning and with the sentence stems, and the practitioner can introduce the vocabulary of the felt sense, which many children find liberating because it names a private experience that they had not known was shareable.

**With adolescents**, the method is at its most effective, and there is a specific clinical use that should be named: **the method of the felt sense is one of the fastest available routes to a young person's own resources in the presence of an emotional activation that has made verbal processing impossible.** A young person in acute distress who cannot speak can be helped to find the bodily sense and to follow it, and the shift that follows is frequently the first moment of relief in the session.

### 6.3 The specific cautions

**A young person in acute crisis** may be unable to stay with a felt sense, and the attempt may increase arousal. The developmental and clinical judgement is that the felt sense is not accessible to a young person who is flooded, and that stabilisation work must come first.

**A young person with a history of dissociation** may find the attention to the body's interior extremely difficult or frightening, and the somatic literature is clear that this requires careful preparation and pacing. The instruction "go inside your body" is a serious one in a young person who has learned to leave it.

**A young person who has been told that their body is wrong**, for example in the context of a disability, chronic illness, trauma-related body symptoms, or a body that has been commented on, may have a complicated relationship with bodily attention, and the practitioner must proceed with great care.

**A young person in a cultural context in which bodily attention is not the ordinary mode of processing feeling** may find the method strange or inappropriate. Chapter 48 addresses this.

## 7. Modern Developments

### 7.1 Low-resourced focusing

The most practically significant development has been **low-resourced focusing**, developed by Gendlin and colleagues in the 1990s in order to make focusing practicable in settings with limited time and limited resources. It consists of a single session in which the practitioner guides the process directly, using a set of structured instructions, and it has been used in a variety of settings including schools, primary care, and brief therapy services. The research literature on it is small but growing, and it is the variant most likely to be usable by a student in a placement.

### 7.2 Focusing in the somatic and trauma fields

The theory has been considerably more influential outside the person-centred tradition than within it, and its principal influence has been on the somatic and trauma-informed fields, where the concepts of the felt sense, the inner edge, and the direction of the felt shift have been absorbed into the practical vocabulary of stabilisation and resourcing work. Chapter 40 develops this application in detail, and the student who knows the focusing theory will find the stabilisation protocols considerably easier to understand.

### 7.3 The contemplative connection

The theory's structure — the attention to the whole, the wordless, and the direction of the movement — is closely parallel to the account of attention in several contemplative traditions, and the contemporary literature on mindfulness has drawn the parallel explicitly. The parallel is instructive for two reasons. It shows that the phenomenon Gendlin identified in a psychotherapy setting in 1946 has been independently described in a great many traditions, which is a substantial piece of evidence for its reality. And it shows that the differences are as instructive as the similarities: the contemplative traditions are systematic about the discipline of attention over years, and they generally aim at a change in the person rather than at the resolution of a particular situation.
