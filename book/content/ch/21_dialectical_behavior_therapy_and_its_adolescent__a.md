# Dialectical Behavior Therapy and Its Adolescent Adaptations

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the origin of dialectical behaviour therapy in the treatment of chronic self-harm and suicidality, and explain the clinical problem it was designed to address.
- Explain the biosocial theory of the development of emotional vulnerability, and state the principal constructs.
- State the core dialectical strategies, the validation strategies, and the problem-solving strategies, and describe how they operate together.
- Describe the four skills modules — mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness — and the four levels of each.
- Describe the structure of a standard dialectical behaviour therapy programme and the consultation team model.
- Describe the adolescent adaptations of dialectical behaviour therapy and their empirical status.
- Apply dialectical behaviour therapy thinking to a case involving a young person.
- Evaluate the evidence for dialectical behaviour therapy and for its adolescent adaptations.

## Chapter Overview

Marsha Linehan developed dialectical behaviour therapy in the late 1980s, in a clinic in Washington State, for a population that the mental health system had largely given up on: women who cut themselves, who had attempted suicide, and who were being discharged from hospital after every admission. Linehan's own description of the difficulty — that the usual therapies, and the usual hospital responses, had made things worse for these patients — is the founding observation of the theory, and it is a critique of the profession that a student should read carefully, because it applies far beyond the population in which it was developed.

The therapy's central technical insight is that the patients who self-harm are in a state of **emotional dysregulation** in which the problem-solving system is offline, and that the clinical task is to move the young person from an emotional, reactive state into a problem-solving state before anything else is attempted. Everything else in the therapy follows from that: the emphasis on the dialectic of acceptance and change, the validation of the young person's experience, the skills training in four modules, the use of a "line" team that speaks with a single voice, and the deliberate assumption that the young person will continue to self-harm and that the work is therefore to make the self-harm less lethal until it stops.

The adolescent adaptations are among the most significant developments in the field, and the DBT for adolescents programme is one of the most widely implemented treatments for adolescent self-harm in the world. Their evidence is good but not as good as the adult evidence, and a student should be able to say so precisely.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The problem Linehan was solving

Linehan's account of the problem, as she set it out in the early 1990s, is worth stating in her own structure because it identifies a failure that has general application.

Patients who self-harm were typically treated with a combination of supportive therapy, medication, and hospitalisation, and the outcomes were poor: high rates of re-attempt, high rates of discharge followed by re-admission, and a pattern in which the patients were regarded as manipulative and as having brought their difficulties on themselves. The system, in effect, was responding to the behaviour rather than to the distress, and the behaviour was being reinforced by the response.

The alternative treatment, Linehan observed, was not better, because it treated the self-harm as a symptom to be eliminated and ignored the emotional state in which the self-harm occurred. The patients felt that the treatment was a judgement on them, and the therapeutic alliance deteriorated, and the patients left.

The treatment that Linehan built was designed around three observations: that the patients were ambivalent about their own self-harm and wanted to stop but could not; that the self-harm functioned to regulate intolerable emotion; and that a patient who was in an emotional state could not learn, and that the treatment had to address the emotional state first.

### 1.2 The suicide risk assessment and the "nonsuicidal self-injury" distinction

Two developments accompanied the therapy and are clinically important.

The first is the ** Suicide Risk Assessment (SRA)**, developed by Linehan and refined with colleagues, which is a clinical assessment of suicide risk that is conducted with the patient rather than about them, that explores the reasons for living and the reasons for dying, and that produces a collaborative safety plan. It has become one of the most widely used and most carefully evaluated risk assessment tools in the world, and Appendix H provides the documentation framework for it.

The second is the **distinction between suicidal self-injury and nonsuicidal self-injury**, which the treatment made explicit and which has become a standard distinction in the field. The distinction matters because the two are associated with different outcomes, the assessment differs, and the young person who engages in nonsuicidal self-injury is frequently not at imminent risk of suicide but is frequently at high risk of later suicidal behaviour, and it is frequently dismissed by adults as "attention-seeking" when it is in fact a regulation strategy that works in the short term.

### 1.3 The development of the programme

The first trial of the standard dialectical behaviour therapy programme was published in 1991 and reported a large effect on the frequency of suicidal behaviours and on the frequency of nonsuicidal self-injury, in a population of women who had been hospitalised specifically for these behaviours. The programme was a year-long, with individual therapy approximately once a week, a skills group once a week, a consultation team meeting once a week, and telephone coaching between sessions. The treatment was the first in the field to be delivered in this structured form, and the breadth of the programme is one of its defining features.

## 2. The Biosocial Theory

### 2.1 The two factors

The theory holds that a young person who develops a chronic pattern of suicidal or self-injurious behaviour has, typically, a **vulnerability** factor and an **environmental** factor, and that the behaviour emerges from their transaction.

**The vulnerability factor** is a biological predisposition, typically involving emotional sensitivity, high reactivity, high intensity, and a slow or inconsistent return to baseline after an emotional activation. Linehan's account is that this is a temperament, that it is substantially inherited, and that it is not a disorder.

**The environmental factor** is a pattern of invalidating environments. The original formulation specified three: a family that is emotionally chaotic and that does not model effective emotional regulation; a family that teaches the young person to inhibit expression of emotion; and a family in which communication of distress is met with escalating responses.

### 2.2 The problem of invalidation

The account of **invalidation** is the heart of the theory and the part that is most often misunderstood.

Invalidation is a set of assumptions about a person that are inconsistent with external reality. The classic examples: "You can't feel that bad, other people have it worse"; "You're being dramatic"; "You don't have to feel that way"; "You're making it up so people will pay attention." Invalidation is not the same as criticism, and it is not the same as a lack of support. It is the attempt to impose on a person a description of their experience that does not fit their experience.

The consequence of a chronic invalidating environment is that the young person does not learn to identify, to label, and to modulate their emotions, because the labelling of their emotional experience has been systematically undermined. The young person becomes **emotionally dysregulated**: intense, slow to return to baseline, and prone to acting from the emotion rather than from a problem-solving state.

### 2.3 The Dialectic

The **dialectic** is the central philosophical and clinical idea, and it is worth stating carefully because it is usually reduced to a slogan about two opposites.

The therapeutic stance is the synthesis of acceptance and change. The therapy accepts the young person's behaviour as it is, without requiring it to change in this moment and without implying that it is acceptable in the long term; and it requires change, in the form of the development of skills that the young person does not currently have and that they need in order to have a life they are willing to live. The therapist does not choose between acceptance and change; the therapist does both at the same time, and holds them together.

Linehan's formulation of the therapeutic relationship in this context is distinctive: the therapist is a **dialectical** stance, and the dialectic is of "both/and" rather than "either/or." The therapist is warm and firm, and the two are not in tension, because the warmth is what makes the firmness possible.

### 2.4 The development of the problem

The theory's account of how the problem develops is worth following, because it is a developmental account and it connects the theory to the material in Chapter 9.

The young person has the vulnerability factor. The environment invalidates. The young person's emotions are therefore not validated and are not developed, and the young person's emotional regulation capacity is impaired. The young person is therefore, in the presence of an emotional trigger, in a state of emotional dysregulation: intense emotion, no access to problem-solving, and a very high probability of an impulsive, harmful action. The self-injury functions to modulate the intolerable emotion, and it works, in the short term, which is precisely why it persists. The consequences of the self-injury — shame, the need to hide it, the escalation of the family's response, the increased invalidation — produce a further increase in the emotional vulnerability, and the cycle continues.

## 3. The Strategy Set

### 3.1 The three strategy families

The therapy's clinical repertoire is organised into three families, and a student should be able to name them and state the function of each.

**Dialectical strategies** maximise both acceptance and change simultaneously. The most important of them is the dialectical strategy of **validation and invalidation**, which is the direct counter to the invalidating environment that produced the problem. The therapist validates the young person's emotion and their reasoning, and then, if necessary, points out where the reasoning has led to a problem and what the young person might do differently. The classic formulation is: "It makes sense that you want to hit him; it makes sense that you're angry; and it might also make sense to walk away, and here's what that would look like." Both statements are true, and the young person is not asked to abandon the first in order to accept the second.

Other dialectical strategies include the dialectical strategy of **problem-solving first** (which addresses the practical problems before the emotional ones, in order to reduce the emotional pressure) and the dialectical strategy of **devil's advocate** (in which the therapist argues against the young person's position, in order to make the young person argue for it and thereby examine it).

**Validation strategies** make the young person feel understood. The strategies are the accurate reflection of the emotion, the accurate reflection of the reasoning, the communication of understanding of the young person's difficulties as being reasonable given their circumstances, and the engagement in problem-solving as a demonstration of respect for the young person's intelligence.

**Problem-solving strategies** teach the young person to change their behaviour, their thoughts, and their physiological state. They are drawn from the cognitive-behavioural, the behavioural, the exposure, and the mindfulness traditions, and they are applied in the problem-solving state, which is the state in which the young person is not emotionally overwhelmed.

### 3.2 Validation in practice

The validation strategies deserve more attention than they usually receive, because they are the part of the therapy that reverses the developmental damage, and because they are the part that a trainee finds hardest to do well.

**Validating the emotion.** "That makes sense. Of course you're furious. He told everyone, and you trusted him." The statement is about the emotional response, and it is not a statement about the behaviour, and the two must be kept apart.

**Validating the reasoning.** "It makes sense that you think it's not fair. He did promise." The statement is about the logic, and it is given even when the conclusion drawn from the logic is not acceptable, and this is the move that students find hardest: the reasoning is validated while the behaviour that follows from it is not.

**Validating the difficulty.** "It makes sense that you're finding this hard. You've been doing this for years and it's not working, and people have been telling you it's not good enough."

**Validating the person.** "You're doing something really difficult right now, and you haven't done it before."

**Invalidating.** The use of invalidation in the therapy is deliberate and limited, and a student should understand the rule: the therapist invalidates the *behaviour* and never the emotion, the reasoning, or the person. "Hitting him isn't going to help you here and it's not going to be allowed" invalidates the behaviour. "You're not allowed to feel angry" invalidates the emotion and is a serious error.

## 4. The Skills Training

### 4.1 The four modules

**Mindfulness.** The module on "what" rather than "how" — attending to the present moment with curiosity and non-judgement, and "how" — engaging in the current moment in a way that is more effective than the automatic patterns the young person has developed. The mindfulness skills in the therapy are the specific "observe," "describe," and "participate" skills, and the practice of them is directly relevant to the young person's tendency to act from the emotion.

**Distress tolerance.** The module on surviving a crisis without making it worse. The skills include the TIPP procedures (temperature change, intense exercise, paced breathing, and progressive muscle relaxation), the "STOP" skill, the "pros and cons" analysis, the "improve the moment" skills, and the acceptance and willingness skills. The rationale is that the young person needs a way of getting through the next ten minutes, and that the first step in changing a behaviour is surviving it.

**Emotion regulation** is the module on understanding and managing the emotions themselves: identifying and labelling the emotion, understanding its function and its message, reducing the vulnerability, increasing the positive reinforcement, and opposite action, and the problem-solving and relationship skills that support all of these. The emotion regulation skills are the direct answer to the invalidating environment, because they teach the young person the labelling that was never developed.

**Interpersonal effectiveness** is the module on the other people: being able to ask for what is needed, being able to say no, being able to maintain a sense of self in a relationship, and being able to solve problems in relationships. It is the module most directly responsive to the developmental task of adolescence, and the module in which the developmental adaptations are most needed.

### 4.2 The four levels

Each skill is taught at four levels, and the four-level structure is one of the therapy's most useful teaching devices.

**Level one: the crisis skills.** The skills that are used in the moment of crisis — for example, the TIPP procedures, or the STOP skill.

**Level two: the main skills.** The fuller version of the skill, taught in the group, with practice and homework.

**Level three: the perspective-taking skills.** "What did I do that made this harder?" "How would you have seen this if you were in their position?" "What is the other possible thing that this situation might mean?" The level-three questions develop the young person's capacity for the perspective-taking that the invalidating environment has prevented, and they are directly relevant to the developmental literature on the adolescent's emerging capacity for moral reasoning and for mentalising.

**Level four: the radical acceptance skills.** "This is just the way it is right now. This is the moment I am in. I am doing my best, and so are you, and I am going to be here with you." The level-four skills are the most profound and the most difficult, and they are taught in the context of the young person's own behaviour, in which the goal is to accept reality as it is while changing it.

### 4.3 The homework and the skills sheets

The therapy is heavily reliant on the skills sheets and on the homework, and the homework is the mechanism by which the skills become available in the moment of crisis. The skills sheets are short, and the young person writes on them, and the practice is deliberate and repeated.

## 5. The Programme Structure

### 5.1 The standard programme

The standard programme is a year-long, with four components: individual therapy approximately once a week; a skills group once a week, in which the four modules are taught in sequence; a **consultation team** meeting approximately once a week, comprising the individual therapist, the skills trainer, and the psychiatrist, with others as needed; and telephone coaching between sessions, with the skills trainer available.

The consultation team is one of the therapy's most distinctive features and is a direct answer to the "splitting" problem: patients who self-harm typically divide the staff team, and the consultation team ensures that the team speaks with one voice, and that no member of the team is drawn into a covert alliance with the patient against another member.

### 5.2 The suicide risk assessment and the safety plan

The formal suicide risk assessment is conducted with the patient, it is repeated whenever the risk changes, and it is documented. The **safety plan**, which replaces the traditional no-harm contract in this therapy, is a collaboratively constructed document that specifies the warning signs, the internal coping strategies, the people and places that provide distraction, the people who can help with a problem, the professionals or services to be contacted, and the making of the environment safe. Appendix H provides the templates and the documentation requirements.

## 6. Dialectical Behaviour Therapy with Adolescents

### 6.1 The adaptations

The adolescent adaptations of the therapy were developed by Linehan and colleagues, principally for the treatment of adolescent self-harm and of the range of difficulties that accompany it — depression, anxiety, substance use, disordered eating, and the family and school difficulties. The randomised trial of the adolescent-adapted programme was published in 2015 and was a large, well-conducted study, and the programme has since been widely implemented.

The principal adaptations are: the parent involvement, with the parents participating in a skills group of their own and in individual sessions, which is the single most important difference from the adult programme; the developmentally adjusted language and content of the skills; the reduction in the length of the treatment from a year to approximately six months, with a planned continuation phase; the increased emphasis on the interpersonal effectiveness module, which is where the developmental changes are most extensive; the use of the family and school as the primary targets of the interpersonal work; and the developmental emphasis on the "one thing at a time" formulation of the change process.

### 6.2 The parent component

The parent component is what makes the adolescent adaptation a family intervention rather than an individual one, and it is the component with the most important developmental logic.

The parents of a self-harming adolescent are, typically, in a state of acute distress, with a well-developed belief that the self-harm is manipulative, that it is attention-seeking, and that the solution is to remove the attention. Many parents are themselves emotionally dysregulated, and many are themselves survivors of the same pattern in their own families, which is the intergenerational transmission that the biosocial theory predicts.

The parent work therefore involves: the parents learning the same skills; the parents learning the validation strategies, which is the direct counter to the pattern in which the young person's distress was met with escalating responses; the parents working on their own emotional regulation, which is the developmental task of the parent's own "adolescent" phase of life, which the parents are frequently still in; and the parents being helped to distinguish between the self-harm and the underlying distress, which is a distinction that many parents cannot make and which is at the heart of the family pattern.

### 6.3 The developmental rationale

The developmental rationale for the parent component is worth stating, because it is the point at which the theory connects to the developmental literature in Chapter 9.

A self-harming adolescent is almost always in a state of developmental arrest: the developmental tasks of the second decade — the establishment of a peer position, the renegotiation of the parent relationship, the development of an identity, the increase in autonomy — have been arrested by the emotional dysregulation and by the family's response to it. The adolescent who is in a state of constant emotional crisis is not working on any of these tasks. The work of the therapy is to restore the developmental process, and the parent component is essential because the parents are the people who have the most control over the conditions in which the developmental process can resume.

### 6.4 The other adaptations and their evidence

The other adaptations include the use of the "middle path" and the "walking and talking" skills for the young person, the specific adaptation of the interpersonal effectiveness module to address peer relationships, the reduction of the treatment length, and the emphasis on the involvement of the school.

The evidence for the adolescent programme is good but smaller than the adult evidence. The 2015 trial was large and well-conducted and found substantial effects on the frequency of self-harm and on the frequency of suicidal ideation, with some erosion of the effect at follow-up. The implementation literature, which is now substantial, shows that the programme is widely used and that fidelity is variable, and that outcomes in routine services are less favourable than in the trial. The systematic reviews of the adolescent evidence base are generally positive, with the standard caveats.
