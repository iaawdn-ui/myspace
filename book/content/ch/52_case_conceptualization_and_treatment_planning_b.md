
## 7. From Formulation to Goals

Goals should be meaningful, observable enough to review, achievable in sequence, and owned by the young person and family where appropriate. SMART criteria are useful if not applied mechanically. “Reduce anxiety” is vague. “Attend first two lessons on four of five days for two weeks, using agreed entry route and break card” is reviewable. But human goals may also include feeling safer with mother, having one friend to eat with, or being able to sleep without checking locks. These can be scaled and described.

Distinguish:

- **Safety goals:** non-negotiable protection and risk reduction.
- **Stabilisation goals:** sleep, routine, regulation, attendance, basic functioning.
- **Change goals:** skills, relationships, beliefs, family patterns, academic participation.
- **Developmental goals:** autonomy, friendships, identity exploration, qualifications, transitions.
- **System goals:** school adjustments, coordinated care, parent support, practical resources.

A plan overloaded with goals becomes another experience of failure. Three to five active priorities are usually enough.

## 8. Treatment Planning

A complete plan states:

1. formulation summary;
2. goals and rationale;
3. interventions and who delivers them;
4. client, family, school, and professional responsibilities;
5. frequency, setting, and expected duration;
6. consent, assent, confidentiality, and information sharing;
7. risk and crisis arrangements;
8. measures and review dates;
9. criteria for intensifying, changing, referring, or ending;
10. practical barriers and solutions.

Sequencing is a clinical decision. Safety and engagement come before demanding processing. Parent work may precede joint sessions. Sleep and medical assessment may precede exposure. Skills may precede trauma narration. School changes may need to occur while individual therapy is still early.

## 9. Worked Example

**Presentation.** Amara, fifteen, has panic symptoms, school absence, falling grades, and nightly arguments.

**Formulation.** Predisposing: high sensitivity, perfectionism, previous bullying. Precipitating: school transition and public panic attack in assembly. Perpetuating: avoidance, reassurance seeking, mother leaving work to collect her, sleep reversal, academic backlog, teachers questioning absence in front of peers. Protective: strong writing ability, one trusted friend, committed grandmother, mother willing to change responses, school counsellor available.

**Plan.** Medical review; shared formulation; sleep routine; parent session on reassurance and collection thresholds; graded school return beginning with counsellor office and two lessons; interoceptive and situational exposure with trained practitioner; private teacher check-ins; academic triage so backlog does not become a second threat; weekly attendance and panic ratings; review after four weeks.

Notice that the plan is neither “CBT” as a slogan nor a collection of unrelated supports. Each item maps to a perpetuating factor.

## 10. Risk and Safeguarding in Plans

Risk formulation should identify static and dynamic factors, triggers, warning signs, protective factors, likely scenarios, and actions. Risk is not a score alone. Plans must name who does what, when, and how the young person can seek help.

Safeguarding concerns require action under local law and policy. Treatment planning cannot delay protection. Where parents are part of risk, information sharing and session structure change accordingly.

Plans should also consider risk created by treatment itself: exposure distress, family conflict after sessions, confidentiality breaches, transport home while dysregulated, online contact boundaries, and deterioration between appointments.

## 11. Review and Reformulation

Review asks:

- What improved, worsened, or stayed the same?
- Which formulation predictions were supported or disconfirmed?
- Was the intervention delivered as planned?
- Did the environment change?
- What does the young person say helped or harmed?
- Should intensity, focus, participants, or provider change?

Lack of progress is not automatically resistance. It may indicate wrong target, insufficient dose, poor alliance, unaddressed practical barriers, medical issues, family overload, or a formulation that flatters the professional's preferred theory.

Endings should be considered from the beginning. A plan that creates indefinite dependency without developmental purpose fails even if sessions feel warm.

## 12. Documentation Quality

Records should be accurate, respectful, and written as if the young person may read them—because in many systems they can. Avoid labels without evidence, speculative motives stated as fact, and copying forward outdated risk information. Date formulations, note whose views they represent, and record disagreements.

Treatment plans shared across agencies should include the minimum necessary information. Consent and legal basis for sharing must be clear.

## 13. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

Formulation can reproduce bias when culture is treated as pathology, poverty as parenting failure, or institutional harm as individual disorder. Include discrimination and material context explicitly, and ask the young person and family how they explain the problem.

Power matters in goal setting. A school may want compliance, a parent obedience, a service discharge, and the young person safety or freedom. The plan should identify whose goal each target serves and negotiate conflicts transparently.

Consent is ongoing. As formulation changes, the rationale for intervention should be re-explained. Young people should know what records exist, who can see summaries, and how to correct factual errors.

## 14. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Formulation** — A collaborative, testable explanation of how difficulties developed and are maintained.
- **Diagnosis** — Classification of a pattern according to recognised criteria.
- **Perpetuating factors** — Processes that maintain current difficulties.
- **Protective factors** — Relationships, capacities, and resources that buffer adversity.
- **Leverage point** — A changeable factor likely to influence the wider system.
- **Risk formulation** — Contextual explanation of risk scenarios, triggers, safeguards, and responses.
:::

## 15. Chapter Summary

Case conceptualization turns information into judgement and judgement into a plan. Its quality is measured not by theoretical elegance but by whether it helps a real young person, family, and service decide what to do next, in what order, with what consent, and how they will know whether it worked.

## 16. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Distinguish description, diagnosis, formulation, and plan.
2. Why is “avoidance because of anxiety” potentially circular?
3. Construct a five-part formulation for examination panic.
4. Convert three vague goals into reviewable goals without making them mechanistic.
5. What should happen when outcome data disconfirm a formulation?
6. How can school and parent goals conflict with the adolescent's goals?
7. Write a record entry that separates observation, report, and inference.
:::

## 18. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For formulation.** Johnstone and Dallos offer cross-theoretical depth; Kuyken and colleagues emphasise collaboration.

**For treatment planning.** Pair formulation texts with local safeguarding, consent, record-keeping, and service documentation standards.

**For practice.** Judge every formulation by whether it changes a decision. If it does not, it may be biography rather than formulation.
