
## 9. Behaviour Therapy with Children and Adolescents

The behavioural approach has a specific relationship with young people, and it is not simply the adult approach with a smaller person. Three features of the developmental context change the practice considerably: the fact that the antecedents of behaviour are usually controlled by adults rather than by the young person; the fact that the behaviour itself is usually still developing; and the fact that the consequences available to a young person are largely determined by the adults in the setting.

### 9.1 The contingencies are not the young person's to change

An adult can usually arrange to run for the bus. A fifteen-year-old cannot; the decision is made by a parent, a school, a transport authority. In behavioural terms, the young person's environment is largely a **fixed or semi-fixed contingency structure** in which they have very little control, and the clinical implication is significant. Behavioural interventions with young people therefore have to work *through* the adults who control the setting, and the first task in most behavioural work with a young person is to establish what the adults are currently reinforcing, often inadvertently.

The question the counsellor asks in the first session is therefore often more behavioural than it appears: *What happens immediately after this happens, and who does it?* The answer is frequently surprising, and the discovery that the behaviour which is generating so much concern is the behaviour that is currently being reinforced by a well-intentioned adult is a common and constructive turning point in the work.

### 9.2 The young person as a learner, not a performer

Developmental psychology has established beyond reasonable dispute that the young person is an active constructor of learning rather than a passive recipient of it. Bandura's observational learning account, which is the behavioural tradition's own contribution here, showed that a child acquires behaviour by watching models and by anticipating the consequences of their own actions. The developmental literature adds that the child is also constructing working models of other people, and is testing them against reality.

This has three practical implications.

**Learning by observation means that the adults in the setting are teaching continuously.** A child who watches a peer get attention for a disruptive behaviour is learning something, whether or not the peer intended to teach it. A child who watches a peer manage a conflict calmly is learning something else. Modelling as a technique (Chapter 25) formalises a mechanism that is operating in every group of young people at all times.

**Expectancy matters as much as experience.** A young person who expects that asking for help will produce ridicule will not ask for help, whatever the actual contingencies are. This is a cognitive construct, and its presence in the behavioural tradition is one of the ways the modern framework differs from the original.

**Learning is a slow and highly variable process.** Behavioural expectations must be calibrated to the developmental level. A young person who has been out of school for four months will not re-engage in two sessions, and a plan built on a twelve-week horizon may be entirely reasonable.

### 9.3 Developmentally appropriate targets

Behavioural objectives must be developmentally appropriate, and the specification of the objective is a technical act. Several principles guide it.

**Operationalise the behaviour precisely.** "Aggression" is not a behaviour. "Hitting a peer with an open hand" is. The precision is not pedantry: it is what makes the objective measurable, and it also forces the clinician to look at what the young person is actually doing, which frequently reveals that the behaviour is not what the referral described.

**Target the function, not the form.** The question is not "how do we stop the behaviour?" but "what does the behaviour accomplish, and what else could accomplish it?" If the behaviour functions to end an unbearable peer situation, then the target is the peer situation, and the replacement behaviour is a request for help that actually works.

**Begin at the level of success.** Shaping and the "first-day" design of exposure work both follow from the observation that a young person who is failing cannot learn.

**Include a generalisation plan.** Behaviour learned in the therapy room is not behaviour learned in the corridor. The generalisation plan specifies who needs to use the new behaviour, where, and with what support, and it is the component most often omitted and most often the reason for a disappointing outcome.

### 9.4 Token economies in the classroom and the clinic

The token economy is a system in which desired behaviour is reinforced with tokens exchangeable for backup reinforcers, and it is the technique through which many young people first encounter the logic of contingency management. Chapter 28 develops it in detail. The developmental points that matter here are that tokens must be immediate, salient, and age-appropriate; that the response cost of a token must be low; that the system must be scrupulously consistent; and that a token system introduced and then withdrawn can produce a worse outcome than none, because the young person has learned that adult promises about rewards are unreliable.

### 9.5 The limits with young people

Three cautions belong in this section rather than in a list at the end.

**A reduction in a counted behaviour is not automatically a good outcome.** If a young person has stopped running away from school but has begun to comply with a more controlling regime, the outcome has been achieved by a change in the power relation rather than in the young person's functioning, and the clinical goal has not been met.

**Compliance is not the same as development.** The behavioural tradition's legitimate concern is with the selection of behaviour; the developmental concern is with the acquisition of capacity. The two can diverge, and a plan that produces durable compliance by external control has solved the easier problem.

**Reinforcement can be withdrawn at the worst moment.** The most common failure in behavioural work with young people is not a technical error in the analysis but a discontinuity in the environment: a change of teacher, a change of shift, a family crisis, a move of school. A behaviour that has been carefully built over three months is fragile to such a disruption, and the plan must anticipate it, which means the adults who will carry the plan must be identified, briefed, and prepared to continue it.

## 10. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Was Cleared Every Day

**Presentation.** Daniel is fourteen. He is referred by his school because of "disruptive behaviour" and by his mother because he "won't do anything he's asked." The school's log records eleven exclusions in the past term. His mother reports that he ignores instructions, does not do homework, and stays out late.

**A behaviourally framed assessment.** The counsellor begins by asking the school for the incident log, which records date, time, location, and the stated reason for each exclusion. Reading the log, a pattern appears immediately: eleven of the eleven exclusions occurred in a single forty-minute period — the unsupervised break between the last and the second lesson — and every one of them occurred within four minutes of the start of the break. The stated reasons vary: "disruption," "non-compliance," "abusive language," "fighting." The actual behaviour in each case is the same: Daniel is in the corridor with a group of older pupils, and he is refusing to return to class when the bell rings.

The behavioural questions follow.

*What happens immediately before?* The bell rings. Daniel is with four older pupils in the corridor. There is no supervision.

*What happens immediately after?* He is excluded, goes home, and spends the rest of the day unsupervised. He is not in any trouble at home when he arrives; his mother is at work until five.

*What does the exclusion produce?* Escape from a classroom he finds aversive, time with the older pupils, and a day with no demands on him.

**The hypothesis.** The behaviour functions to produce removal from an aversive peer situation and from academic demands. It is not a conduct problem. It is an escape-maintained behaviour that has been inadvertently reinforced by a well-intentioned consequence, and it has been shaped into its present form by the exclusion itself.

**The intervention.** The exclusion policy is not, in this instance, within the counsellor's gift, and this is a frequent feature of behavioural work in schools. What is within the counsellor's gift is a restructuring of the break.

The plan has four components. First, a supervision arrangement: an adult presence in the corridor during the break, with a specific, named adult, and a smaller, structured activity available. Second, a differential reinforcement plan in which Daniel's return to class at the bell is reinforced with something he has been asked for and that has been established as valuable by his own report — in this case, access to the music room, which is a genuine preference and one that the school can grant. Third, a functional replacement: Daniel is taught to ask the on-duty adult for a five-minute pass to the music room, with the adult then escorting him, which delivers the same consequence through a behaviour the school can approve. Fourth, a withdrawal plan: the pass is available initially every time, and the availability is reduced over a defined period as the alternative behaviour consolidates, with the criteria for reduction written down in advance.

**The parents' involvement.** The mother is asked what happens at home when Daniel arrives after an exclusion, and the answer is that nothing happens; he goes to his room and plays on his games. She is asked whether she would be willing to make the consequence at home consistent with the school plan — which in this case means that a day home without school is also a day without the gaming console, and that the day home *with* school is a day with it. She is hesitant, because the current arrangement is the only time she has to herself. The counsellor treats this as a resource problem rather than a parenting problem and negotiates a specific arrangement: her break, agreed with another parent in the building, on the days her son is excluded.

**The outcome and the interpretation.** The exclusions fall from eleven to one over the first six weeks, and the one is on a day when the corridor supervision was not in place, which is itself informative. Daniel's grades do not change dramatically. The more significant change is in his report: he says the corridor is where the work gets done, and he begins to use the pass deliberately rather than as a route out of class.

The behavioural reading of this case is straightforward. But it is worth naming what the behavioural reading leaves out. Daniel's insistence on the corridor with the older pupils is also a peer-relation preference, and the fact that the school had provided no structured activity for the break meant that the unstructured break was the only part of the day in which his social needs were served. A purely contingencies-based intervention that removed the corridor entirely would have been behaviourally effective and would have removed something the young person valued. The formulation was correct, and the intervention needed to be broader than the analysis strictly required. This is the general lesson of the behavioural tradition applied carefully: the analysis identifies what maintains the behaviour, and the intervention must be adequate to the function, and where the function includes a legitimate need, the intervention should serve the need rather than only extinguish the behaviour.

## 11. Evidence and Research Support

### 11.1 The status of the evidence

The behavioural tradition holds an unusual position in counselling: it is the tradition with the longest history of controlled experimental investigation, and the tradition with the least tolerance for claims that outrun the evidence. A student evaluating behavioural methods should hold two facts together. The laboratory findings about conditioning and reinforcement are among the most thoroughly replicated results in psychology. The clinical evidence for behavioural treatments of specific conditions is strong for some conditions, moderate for others, and weak for the broader claim that behavioural principles are a general framework for counselling.

### 11.2 The single-case experimental tradition

Applied behaviour analysis contributed a methodological development that is arguably more influential outside the behavioural tradition than inside it: the **single-case experimental design**. The logic is straightforward. Because an individual case is the unit of concern, the design establishes a baseline, introduces the intervention, and measures whether the target behaviour changes at the point of introduction and then maintains. Designs are described by letters: **ABAB** (a withdrawal design) and **A-B-A-B** are the standard reversal and withdrawal patterns; **ABAB** is used where withdrawal is ethically impermissible.

The advantage is that the design can establish causal relations in a single individual with no requirement for a large sample, and the requirement of repeated measurement across conditions effectively rules out placebo effects and much of the history threat. The limitations are equally clear: the designs are only as good as the operational definition of the target behaviour, they are vulnerable to the reactivity of the participants to the measurement itself, and they do not generalise to populations.

### 11.3 Evidence for specific applications

The evidence picture differs sharply by application.

**Phobias and anxiety.** The behavioural treatment of specific phobias — systematic desensitisation and, more importantly, exposure-based treatments — has a strong evidence base, and exposure is the best-supported psychological treatment for anxiety disorders in adults. Chapter 25 addresses the developmental modifications required with young people.

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder.** Exposure and response prevention has an established and substantial evidence base with children and adolescents, and is a first-line treatment in most international guidelines.

**Parent training.** Parent training programmes based on operant principles have a long and strong evidence base for oppositional behaviour, conduct disorder, and disruptive behaviour in the school setting, and the Cochrane reviews have found the evidence to be robust.

**Autism spectrum disorder.** Applied behaviour analysis has been the dominant evidence base for behavioural intervention in autism for four decades, and the evidence for the *core* early intensive behavioural interventions has been considerably weakened by methodological problems in the early studies. Chapter 45 addresses this in detail.

**Depression in adolescents.** Behavioural activation has good support, and the evidence for the treatment of adolescent depression is generally stronger for behavioural and cognitive-behavioural approaches than for most alternatives. Chapter 27 develops this.

**Substance use.** The contingency-management literature in substance use is one of the strongest in the behavioural tradition, and it is unusual in reporting very large effects. Chapter 28 and Chapter 47 address it.

### 11.4 Evidence for the general framework

The broader claim — that the principles of learning provide an adequate framework for understanding and treating human psychological difficulty — is not supported by the evidence, and the field's own sophisticated methods of evaluation are the reason. Behavioural researchers are unusually rigorous about their own results, and the result of that rigour is a literature full of small, well-controlled, highly specific studies of individual techniques, which is not the same as a body of evidence for a general framework.

### 11.5 The place of practice-based evidence

The behavioural tradition is also the origin of the practice-based evidence movement, and the reason is instructive. The field was built on the analysis of individual cases, and it was therefore the first to notice that the randomised trial is not the only way to learn, and that a carefully conducted single-case design with a strong measurement system can support a meaningful claim about a change in a specific individual. This insight later generalised into the broader framework of common factors and practice-based research, and it is one of the behavioural tradition's most durable contributions to the discipline.

## 12. Strengths and Limitations of the Behavioural Framework

### 12.1 Strengths

**Clarity of method.** The framework specifies what will be done, what will be measured, and what result would count as improvement. Very few therapeutic traditions are this explicit.

**Transportability.** The techniques transfer across conditions, across settings, and across problems. A behaviour analyst working with a child who has severe self-injury and a behaviour analyst working with a child who will not complete homework use the same core technology.

**A basis for team and system work.** Because the analysis is stated in terms of contingencies, it can be written down and handed to a teacher, a parent, or a ward staff member. Almost no other tradition produces materials that a non-specialist can implement.

**Compatibility with measurement-based practice.** The framework fits naturally with routine outcome monitoring and with the documentation standards that the profession increasingly requires.

**Low risk in many applications.** Many behavioural methods are benign. Graded task assignment, differential reinforcement of appropriate behaviour, activity scheduling, and the teaching of social skills carry very low risk of harm.

### 12.2 Limitations

**The reduction of meaning.** The framework's epistemology, discussed in Section 2, is the source of its most persistent and most damaging professional criticism. A practice that is organised entirely around contingencies risks treating a young person as a set of responses to stimuli.

**The under-specification of internal states.** Where a behaviour is maintained by an internal state — a compulsion, a craving, a shame response, a traumatic re-experiencing — the framework provides less guidance than it appears to. A young person who is driven to self-harm by an unbearable internal sensation cannot be treated by manipulating the contingencies around the self-harm, because the self-harm is not maintained by its consequences in the way the framework requires.

**The technology can outrun the relationship.** A behavioural plan delivered without attention to the therapeutic relationship often fails, and the reason is not that the analysis was wrong but that the young person did not have a reason to use it. This is the point at which the common-factors literature of Chapter 6 and the behavioural literature converge.

**The diffusion of the vocabulary without the discipline.** "Positive reinforcement" has entered everyday and educational language, where it is often used loosely and sometimes incorrectly, and a student should be able to distinguish a technically specified programme of differential reinforcement from the encouragement of a child.

**The historical association with the conditioning of children.** The applied behaviour analysis movement in the 1960s included some ethically troubling practices, and the field's response — the emphasis on the least restrictive procedures, on assent, on the participant's right to withdraw, and on the measurement of the participant's own welfare as an outcome — is part of the ethical discussion in Section 14.

## 13. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 13.1 Culture, learning, and the assumption of a shared environment

The behavioural framework assumes, usually without saying so, that there is a shared environment in which learning occurs and that the young person and the clinician are oriented to the same contingencies. This assumption is not culturally neutral. In settings where the family and the community hold collective rather than individual goals, where a young person's behaviour is understood as an expression of family or group identity rather than as an individual choice, and where the authority of the professional is not a given, the analysis of contingencies may be technically accurate and practically irrelevant.

The practical implication for the practitioner is that the functional analysis must extend to the family's and the community's own systems of reinforcement and meaning. In many settings the most potent reinforcer in a young person's life is approval from a peer group or a family member whose value to the young person exceeds anything the school can offer, and a contingency plan that ignores this will fail for reasons that are not technical.

### 13.2 Culture and the interpretation of behaviour

The behavioural interpretation of a behaviour is always a hypothesis about function, and the hypotheses a practitioner generates are shaped by their own frame of reference. Behaviour that is read as defiance in one frame may be read as a refusal to participate in something the young person regards as unjust; behaviour read as non-compliance may be read as an expression of cultural or religious practice; behaviour read as withdrawal may be read as a culturally appropriate response to shame. These are not exotic considerations, and the practitioner who does not notice the interpretive step will not notice when their interpretation is wrong.

The contextual behavioural tradition (Bowers and colleagues) developed specifically out of this concern, and its central technique is the analysis of the client's own words for the words the therapist would use, which is a practical way of making the interpretive step visible.

### 13.3 The ethics of reinforcement

Reinforcement is, in the end, control. The ethical question is not whether it is control — every therapy influences the young person — but whether the influence is proportional, transparent, and in the young person's interests, and whether the young person and those with authority over the young person have a genuine say in it.

Six principles follow, and they are the ethics of the behavioural approach stated in the language of the discipline.

**Proportionality.** The intensity of the intervention should match the severity of the problem. A response cost for lateness is a different moral act from an aversive stimulus for aggression.

**The least restrictive effective procedure.** The first question is always whether a less intrusive method would work, and this question should be recorded and revisited.

**Transparency.** The young person should know what the system is, what they will get, and what will happen if they do not. A system that operates by surprise is a system the young person cannot co-operate with, and non-co-operation will be read as a symptom.

**Assent and the right to withdraw.** For young people of sufficient age and capacity, participation requires assent, and assent requires the capacity to decline. This is the point at which the behavioural tradition's commitment to the observable has to be supplemented by attention to the young person's own account of their experience, and the two are not in tension: a young person who is given a genuine choice to decline is more likely to remain in treatment and more likely to benefit from it.

**Attention to the function of the system.** A token economy that increases on-task behaviour while simultaneously increasing the young person's distress has not succeeded. The welfare of the young person is an outcome variable, not a secondary consideration, and it belongs in the data alongside the behaviour.

**Avoiding the instrumentalisation of the young person.** The most serious ethical failure in behavioural work is the treatment of the young person as a means to an end — as a source of data, a demonstration case, or a training subject. The professional version of this failure is subtle and common: the case is written up because it is a good example, and the writing up is not in the young person's interests.

### 13.4 Ethical practice with children and families

Three further considerations are specific to working with young people.

**Parental authority and confidentiality.** A young person may disclose something in therapy that the parent would wish to know and that the clinician has been asked to keep. The standard answer is that the limits of confidentiality should be established explicitly at the outset, and that the young person should be told what the limits are before they disclose. In many jurisdictions, the limits are set by the law rather than by the clinician, and the clinician should know what they are.

**The relationship between the family's contingencies and the clinical plan.** A behavioural plan that requires a parent's consistency is a plan that puts the parent in a position of possible failure, and the practitioner should be prepared to support the parent as well as the young person. The parent in the case illustration above was not a non-compliant caregiver; she was a woman with an impossible timetable.

**The child welfare principle where behaviour change is dramatic.** Any intervention that produces a marked change in a young person's behaviour must be reviewed by someone who is not the person who designed it, with particular attention to the possibility that the behaviour was serving a protective function. A young person who is aggressive in school and compliant at home is often a young person with a home that is not safe.

## 14. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Behaviour** — In the behavioural tradition, behaviour includes observable action and, where it can be specified functionally, private events; the term means "that which the organism does," not only what an observer can see.
- **Classical conditioning** — The process by which a neutral stimulus acquires the capacity to elicit a response through repeated pairing with a stimulus that already elicits it.
- **Unconditioned stimulus (US)** — A stimulus that naturally produces a response without prior learning.
- **Unconditioned response (UR)** — The reflexive response that the unconditioned stimulus produces.
- **Conditioned stimulus (CS)** — A previously neutral stimulus that, after pairing with the unconditioned stimulus, comes to elicit a conditioned response.
- **Conditioned response (CR)** — The learned response elicited by the conditioned stimulus alone.
- **Acquisition** — The phase in which the conditioned response is being learned.
- **Extinction** — The phase in which the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus, producing a decline in the conditioned response.
- **Spontaneous recovery** — The reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest interval, without further pairing.
- **Reinstatement** — The restoration of an extinguished conditioned response to full strength by the presentation of the unconditioned stimulus once again.
- **Generalisation** — The tendency for a conditioned response to be elicited by stimuli similar to the original conditioned stimulus.
- **Discrimination** — The learned differentiation between stimuli, so that a response occurs to one and not to others.
- **Higher-order (second-order) conditioning** — The conditioning of a new neutral stimulus by pairing it with an already-established conditioned stimulus.
- **Conditioned inhibition** — A stimulus that, through being repeatedly paired with the absence of the unconditioned stimulus, comes to signal safety.
- **Overshadowing** — The phenomenon in which a more informative stimulus in a compound captures the learning, preventing the less informative stimulus from acquiring associative strength.
- **Blocking** — The phenomenon in which a previously learned stimulus prevents a new stimulus in the same compound from acquiring associative strength.
- **Counterconditioning** — The procedure of pairing a conditioned stimulus with an incompatible response so that it comes to elicit the new response.
- **Systematic desensitisation** — The graded pairing of anxiety-provoking images with relaxation, developed by Wolpe.
- **Operant behaviour** — Behaviour that is emitted rather than elicited and that is selected by its consequences.
- **Reinforcement** — Any consequence that increases the future frequency of the behaviour it follows; the term is defined by effect and not by valence.
- **Positive reinforcement** — The addition of a stimulus after a behaviour, with the effect of increasing that behaviour.
- **Negative reinforcement** — The removal of a stimulus after a behaviour, with the effect of increasing that behaviour.
- **Positive punishment** — The addition of a stimulus after a behaviour, with the effect of decreasing that behaviour.
- **Negative punishment** — The removal of a stimulus after a behaviour, with the effect of decreasing that behaviour; also called response cost or time-out from reinforcement.
- **Law of effect** — Thorndike's principle that responses accompanied by satisfying consequences become more firmly connected with the situation, and those accompanied by discomfort become less so.
- **Shaping** — The systematic reinforcement of successive approximations to a target behaviour.
- **Chaining** — The construction of a complex behaviour by reinforcing a sequence of component behaviours in forward, backward, or middle order.
- **Prompting** — The provision of an assisting stimulus to initiate or perform a behaviour, with a planned process of fading the prompt.
- **Fading** — The systematic withdrawal of a prompt across successive trials, with reinforcement transferred to the unprompted behaviour.
- **Motivating operation** — An event that establishes or abolishes the reinforcing effectiveness of a stimulus; a change in the value of a reinforcer.
- **Extinction burst** — A temporary increase in the frequency or intensity of behaviour during the early period of extinction, before the behaviour declines.
- **Resurgence** — The reappearance of extinguished operant behaviour when the organism is returned to a context in which the behaviour was previously reinforced.
- **Differential reinforcement** — The reinforcement of a specific behaviour, or of a class of behaviour, in a way that distinguishes it from other behaviour.
- **Shaping schedule** — A written plan specifying the target behaviour, the successive approximations, the reinforcer for each, and the rules for advancing and for stepping back.
- **ABC model** — The observational framework recording the Antecedent, the Behaviour, and the Consequence of a behaviour; a description of a sequence rather than a hypothesis about function.
- **Functional analysis** — An experimental procedure in which the consequences are systematically manipulated to determine which consequence maintains a behaviour.
- **Behavioural function** — The consequence that a behaviour produces and that thereby accounts for its occurrence and maintenance.
- **Single-case experimental design** — A design in which behaviour is repeatedly measured across conditions in order to establish a causal relation in a single case.
- **Interobserver agreement** — The extent to which two independent observers code the same behaviour identically; the basic reliability check of behavioural measurement.
- **Radical behaviourism** — Skinner's position that a functional account of behaviour requires no appeal to private causes, and that private events are behaviour and are defined by their discriminative stimuli and consequences.
- **Functional relation** — A statement of covariation between behaviour and specified environmental conditions, established under experimental control.
- **Descriptive functional analysis** — A narrative account of the antecedents and consequences of a behaviour, based on observation and interview, used to generate hypotheses.
- **Contingency** — The relation between a behaviour and the consequences that follow it, specified in terms of the probability and immediacy of the consequence.
- **Response cost** — A form of negative punishment in which a specified amount of a reinforcing stimulus is removed following a behaviour.
- **Time-out from reinforcement** — A form of negative punishment in which the individual is removed from the source of positive reinforcement for a specified period.
- **Least restrictive effective procedure** — The ethical and technical principle that the least intrusive intervention that can produce the required result should be used.
- **Practitioner- and participant-defined outcomes** — The requirement that outcome measurement include the young person's own priorities alongside the practitioner's targets, and the family's and the school's.
- **Social learning theory** — Bandura's account of learning through observation, imitation, and expectancy, which made the behavioural framework usable with young people.
:::

## 15. Chapter Summary

Behaviour therapy is a frame rather than a single technique, and the frame is built on two claims: that behaviour is a function of specified conditions, and that the environment selects behaviour by its consequences. The claims come from a specific intellectual history — Pavlov's conditioned reflex, Thorndike's law of effect, Watson's methodological manifesto, and Skinner's extension to voluntary behaviour — and each contributed a specific element that survives in current practice.

The two conditioning paradigms were described in this chapter in their technical form because the clinical techniques of the following chapters cannot be understood without them. Classical conditioning established acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, reinstatement, generalisation, discrimination, higher-order conditioning, and inhibition, and it established the procedure — counterconditioning — from which exposure work derives. Operant conditioning established the four contingencies, the concepts of reinforcer value and motivating operations, and the technologies of shaping, chaining, prompting, and fading, together with the schedule typology developed in Chapter 26.

Two distinctions should be carried forward as a matter of professional safety. Negative reinforcement increases behaviour and punishment decreases it; "negative" refers only to the removal of a stimulus, and nothing in the terminology refers to anything being good or bad. And the behavioural analysis of a behaviour is a *hypothesis about function* that must be tested, not a description that can simply be read off the observations.

The epistemological position is the tradition's genuine contribution and its standing critical vulnerability. Treating inner events as behaviour defined by their discriminative stimuli and consequences made inner events available for prediction and control, and it did so at the cost of a reductive account of meaning. A practitioner who can hold the power of the framework and the limits of its position at the same time is doing something the discipline itself has found difficult.

With young people, three features of the developmental context change the practice: the contingencies are usually controlled by adults rather than by the young person; the young person is an active learner who acquires behaviour by observation and expectancy as much as by direct experience; and the developmental level sets a realistic horizon. Behavioural work with young people therefore runs through the adults in the setting, targets the function rather than the form of a behaviour, begins at the level of success, and requires an explicit generalisation plan.

The evidence is strong for specific applications — exposure-based treatments of specific phobias, exposure and response prevention for obsessive-compulsive disorder, parent training for oppositional and conduct problems, contingency management for substance use — and much weaker for the general claim that the framework explains human psychological difficulty. The single-case experimental design remains one of the profession's most durable methodological contributions, and the practice-based evidence movement grew out of it.

The ethical analysis is an analysis of control. The principles that follow from it are proportionality, the least restrictive effective procedure, transparency, assent and the right to withdraw, the young person's welfare as an outcome variable in its own right, and the refusal to instrumentalise the young person. In a field with a documented history of troubling practice with children, these principles are the field's own answer to its worst episodes.

## 17. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 23.1 — ABC Data Sheet for a Real Behaviour (90 minutes, individual, field-based)

Choose a behaviour you have actually observed in a placement, a workplace, or a family, and one that is not your own. Define it operationally in observable terms, then record it for one hour across three or more occasions using an ABC sheet with the columns Antecedent, Behaviour, and Consequence. Do not interpret anything while you are recording. At the end of the hour, write a single sentence stating the consequence you believe maintains the behaviour, and mark how confident you are. Then state what evidence would change your mind. The purpose of the activity is the discipline of separating observation from hypothesis, which is the discipline the whole framework rests on.

### Activity 23.2 — The Reinforcer Audit (75 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, take a real behaviour in a school or clinic that a young person and the adults around them find difficult — lateness, refusal of homework, disruption, non-attendance — and run a reinforcer audit on it. For each behaviour, list: what the young person gets when it occurs; what the adults get when it occurs; what the young person gets when it does not occur; and what the adults get when it does not occur. Compare the two columns for the behaviour and for the alternative. Most groups discover that the alternative behaviour is less well reinforced than the problem behaviour, and the discovery is usually more useful than any technique the group can suggest.

### Activity 23.3 — Shaping Plan with Fade Criteria (60 minutes, pairs)

Choose a target behaviour for a fictional fourteen-year-old who will not complete homework. Write a shaping plan with at least five successive approximations, from the first thing the young person will reliably do to the full behaviour. For each approximation specify the reinforcer and where it comes from. Then write the advance criterion and the step-back rule in explicit terms. Finally, write what happens to the plan if the young person's teacher changes. Practising the fade criteria and the continuity plan in advance is the difference between a plan that is implemented and a plan that is abandoned.

### Activity 23.4 — The Negative Reinforcement Audit (45 minutes, individual, reflective)

Write down the last three times you were annoyed by a young person's behaviour. For each, identify what the behaviour actually produced, and classify it as positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement, positive punishment, or negative punishment. Then answer the question you will probably find uncomfortable: in how many of the three was the behaviour maintained by something the adult wanted to stop, and in how many was it maintained by escape from something the adult had inadvertently made unbearable? Most practitioners find at least one case where their own frustration was the maintaining contingency.

## 18. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the principles.** Read Skinner (1953) for the systematic statement, and read Bandura (1977) for the mediational and observational account that made the framework usable with young people. Then read Iversen (1991) for the modern reassessment of the epistemological position, which is the fairest available account of the tradition's philosophy.

**For the laboratory basis.** Read Pavlov (1927) and Thorndike (1913) in the excerpts, and then read a modern textbook chapter on conditioning, because the original texts are slow and the phenomena are the same. Ferster and Skinner (1957) is worth sampling for the schedule typology that Chapter 26 develops.

**For the clinical craft.** Read Milner (1953) for the laboratory reference that sits surprisingly well with clinical work, and read Kazdin (1978) for the applied transition. Then read the applied behaviour analysis literature on functional assessment, because Chapter 24 depends on it.

**For the ethics and the critique.** Read the sections on assent, least restrictive procedures, and participant welfare in any applied behaviour analysis handbook, and read the critical literature on the history of applied behaviour analysis with children. The combination is the preparation for the ethical work in Part VIII and for the appendix documentation forms.

**For the evidence.** Read the Cochrane reviews on parent training and on the behavioural treatment of specific phobias, and read one single-case design handbook in full. The single-case design is the part of the behavioural tradition that most informs the rest of the discipline, and it is the part most often skipped.
