# A Genealogy of Counseling Theories

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain what a genealogical account of theory does, and distinguish it from a chronological one.
- Trace the principal lines of descent from the late nineteenth-century movements that produced counseling theory to the contemporary field.
- Identify the specific problem or crisis to which each major theoretical movement was a response.
- Explain the mechanism by which a new theoretical movement displaces or incorporates an older one.
- Describe the significance of the "third wave" and the contemporary developments that followed it.
- Draw four general lessons from the history of the field about the development of theory.
- Locate the origins of the ideas and techniques used in the contemporary practice of counseling with children and adolescents.

## Chapter Overview

Theories in counseling have a genealogy, and knowing it is not a scholarly ornament. It is the fastest way to understand why a theory has the shape it has, why it retains the assumptions it does, why it is more comfortable with some problems than with others, and why a technique that its founder developed for one purpose seems to work for another. It also explains the persistence of arguments that would otherwise look irrational, because a great many of the field's quarrels are the continuation, in a new key, of a dispute that was settled — provisionally, and with costs — eighty years ago.

A chronological account lists theories in the order they appeared. A genealogical account does something different and more useful: it shows that each major theory is a response to a specific crisis in the theory before it, and that the residue of that response remains in the theory, visible and often embarrassing, long after the crisis has been resolved. The cognitive therapies, for example, were invented in part to escape the limitations of behaviourism, and they retain the behaviourist emphasis on measurement, on written records, and on the treatment of the client as a rational agent whose errors can be specified. The humanistic therapies were invented to escape the limitations of psychoanalysis, and they retain a conception of the practitioner as a warm, healthy, autonomous person whose own development is the precondition of the other's. The family therapies were invented partly to escape the individual focus of the intrapsychic schools, and they retain a conception of the symptom as a communication.

This chapter traces the field's descent in six periods and draws four general lessons from it.

## 1. What a Genealogical Account Does

A genealogy, in the Foucauldian sense, asks a question that sounds trivial and is not: *what is the specific problem to which this practice, discourse, or theory is a response?* The point of the question is that ideas do not arise from nothing. They arise because something else became untenable, and the shape of the new idea is determined by the shape of the difficulty it solves.

Applied to counseling theory, this question immediately produces several observations that a purely chronological account misses.

**Theoretical succession is not cumulative.** The history of counseling theory is not a story of steady accumulation, in which each theory adds a piece to a growing edifice. It is a story of repeated displacement, in which a new theory takes over the central questions of the field by re-describing them, and the previous theory survives as a specialism or as a set of techniques. Cognitive therapy did not incorporate behaviour therapy; it colonised the territory and left behaviour therapy in the clinic. The third wave did not replace the second; it split the second wave into a wave that embraced acceptance and a wave that embraced mindfulness, and it created an active and continuing dispute about the value of symptom reduction as an outcome criterion.

**Successions are driven by unmet needs, not by refutation.** A theory rarely displaces another because it has been shown to be false. It displaces it because it answers a question that the incumbent has left unanswered — most often a question about a population the incumbent handles badly, a question about the limits of a method in a particular context, or a question about the acceptability of an intervention to the people receiving it. The emergence of the family therapies in the 1960s was substantially a response to the perceived and actual failure of individual therapy with children whose difficulties lay in their families, and of the psy chodynamic assumption that the individual was the proper unit of treatment. The emergence of the trauma-informed and somatic approaches in the 1990s and 2000s was substantially a response to the inadequacy of talk-based therapies with people whose difficulties were organised at the level of the body and of autonomic arousal.

**Every theory carries its prehistory in its techniques.** The techniques of a mature theory usually encode the assumptions of its predecessors, and they are therefore sometimes inconsistent with the theory's own philosophy. The cognitive therapist's thought record requires written, sequential, propositional thought, and it is a profoundly rationalistic instrument; the practice of it, in the presence of a young person who does not think in propositions, is the site of a persistent and unresolved friction. The behavioural therapist's attention to reinforcement schedules is an extraordinarily refined instrument for a person who is not a machine, and the entire history of applied behaviour analysis has been an argument about whether the person is more or less than a machine.

**The genealogy is a resource for ethical reflection.** Because each theory was a response to a problem, the problem remains available as a standard against which to judge the theory's contemporary use. A technique used in a population or a setting for which it was not developed is easier to identify as such when one knows what problem it was developed to solve.

## 2. Precursors, 1880–1920

The theory of counseling has no single ancestor, but it has a small number of convergent nineteenth-century sources.

**The clinical psychology of light.** Pierre Janet's work on automaticity, on the hierarchical organisation of behaviour, and on the phenomenon of psychogenic deterioration established the first systematic attempt to explain psychological difficulty in behavioural and physiological terms, and introduced the concept of the trauma. Janet's work was rediscovered in the 1980s by the trauma researchers who needed a precursor for the idea of the traumatic memory as an unintegrated bodily event.

**The psychology of individual differences.** The work of Francis Galton, Alfred Binet, and James McKeen Cattell established the measurement of individual differences and the notion of the trait, and supplied the technical apparatus — the test, the norm, the statistical comparison — that counseling still uses in assessment, career guidance, and programme evaluation. Cattell's factor-analytic programme in particular shaped the trait and factor models that underlie the modern instruments used with young people.

**The first laboratory child.** Lightner Witmer established the first psychological clinic in 1896, in the United States, and coined the term "psychological clinic" for the purpose. The clinic is the institutional ancestor of every subsequent setting in which counseling is delivered, and Witmer's method — the study of an individual child, longitudinally, in relation to the problems brought to the clinic — is essentially the method of a modern case formulation.

**The pragmatist philosophy.** John Dewey, William James, and Charles Cooley supplied a philosophy that treated the person as an organism-in-environment, the self as a product of social interaction, and the examination of experience as the primary method of inquiry. Cooley's concept of the looking-glass self is the direct ancestor of the constructivist and social-cognitive emphasis on the social origin of the self; Dewey's concept of the continuity of experience is the direct ancestor of the developmental approaches in Chapter 9.

**The child study movement.** The child study movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries produced the systematic observation of children's development, the normative milestone approach, and the "normality" concept. It also produced its characteristic methodological weakness, a reliance on small, unselected samples of children whose representativeness was assumed, and it produced the norm-versus-pathology framework that still organises developmental assessment.

**Psychoanalysis.** Freud's *Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) and the *Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality* (1905) created the first comprehensive theory of the human mind and of psychological difficulty, and the first theory in which the symptom had a meaning. The clinical technique of free association and the method of interpretation produced the foundational observation that the person resists the interpretation as well as seeking it, which became resistance, defence, and later the whole account of the therapeutic relationship in the relational and humanistic traditions.

## 3. The Clinical Movement, 1920–1940

Between the two wars, a generation of clinicians produced the theories that the mid-century counselling profession would inherit.

**Alfred Adler's individual psychology.** Adler relocated the centre of personality from the intrapsychic to the social, insisting that the person is a striving for superiority and that the person's fundamental problem is a feeling of inferiority against which a compensatory lifestyle is constructed. Adler's work supplied the first coherent account of the child as a striving creature with a subjective goal, and the first therapeutic method centred on the reconstruction of the person's style of life through the analysis of early memories. It supplied the developmental interest in the significance of birth order and of sibling position, which appears, in modified form, in the family therapies of the 1960s and in the modern literature on sibling relationships.

**Otto Rank's developmental will.** Rank emphasised the will, the creative force, and the separation anxiety of the child, and his developmental theory of the first three years influenced attachment research and object relations theory. Rank's notion of the "trauma of birth" was wrong in its specifics and enormously productive in its general form: the idea that the developmental process itself is a source of anxiety that must be negotiated.

**The object relations school.** Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicott, and, in the English context, John Bowlby and John Bowlby's colleague Ronald Fairbairn developed a view of the child in which the central issue is the management of dependency on a caregiver and the internalisation of the caregiver as an object. Winnicott's contributions — the holding environment, the transitional object, the true and false self, the facilitation environment — are among the most cited and most practically useful concepts in the psychotherapy literature, and the concepts of the good enough mother and of the true self are quoted by practitioners who have no use for the theory that produced them.

**The interwar behavioural and learning tradition.** Watson's 1913 manifesto asserted that psychology was a purely objective branch of natural science whose goal was prediction and control, and the Law of Effect (Thorndike, 1905) and the acquisition of conditioned responses (Pavlov) supplied the learning theory on which the later behaviour therapies were built. The 1930s saw the work of Skinner on operant conditioning, Mowrer on the two-factor theory of avoidance, and the clinical application of conditioning to phobias by Mary Cover Jones, whose 1924 treatment of Little Peter is the ancestor of every exposure-based therapy in the modern field.

**The child guidance movement.** The child guidance clinics of the 1920s and 1930s, and the publications of the influential Chicago clinic, established the multidisciplinary team, the case conference, the clinical notebook, and the assessment of the child in the family. The "child guidance" model of the 1930s is the ancestor of the contemporary school-based, family-based, and consultation models of service delivery, and the practice of a written case formulation that begins with a family and a school is a direct inheritance.

**Karen Horney and the neo-Freudians.** Horney's account of the neurotic need for safety and of the three neurotic orientations, and the work of Erich Fromm and Harry Stack Sullivan, moved the field toward an interpersonal and sociocultural account of personality, supplied the concept of the "basic anxiety" and of the "selective neglect" of reality, and produced the clinical observation that became the basis of the cognitive therapy of Albert Ellis: that the neurotic's disturbance lies in a demanding and self-defeating style of thinking rather than in the events themselves.

## 4. The Professional Turn, 1940–1960

The 1940s and 1950s are the decade in which counseling became a profession and in which the central split in the field was made visible.

**The conditions.** The returning veterans, the National Mental Health Act of 1946, the establishment of the National Institute of Mental Health in 1949, the founding of the American Personnel and Guidance Association's predecessor organisations, and the 1950s expansion of the school guidance movement all created the demand, the funding, and the institutional infrastructure for a profession.

**The professional bodies.** The American Association of Counseling and Development (founded in 1917 as the National vocational guidance society, and renamed through several iterations) brought the four traditions — school counseling, mental health, vocational, and rehabilitation — into one organisation. The rival body, the American Psychological Association, and the National Association of School Psychologists and the American Orthopsychiatric Association each claimed a portion of the territory, and the jurisdictional disputes of the present day are the direct descendants of the professional arrangements of this decade.

**Client-centred therapy.** Carl Rogers' *On Becoming a Person* (1961) and his 1950s research on the counselling relationship produced the intervention that the field's most important empirical literature would later study. The theoretical commitments were: that the person has an innate drive toward growth; that the self-concept is a structure that may be incongruent with experience; that the conditions of growth are the psychological conditions of the therapeutic relationship — congruence, unconditional positive regard, and empathic understanding; and that the practitioner's role is a facilitative, non-directive one. The theory's distinctive claim, and the claim that the subsequent forty years of common-factors research would examine, is that the conditions of the relationship are themselves the therapeutic conditions.

**Maslow and the humanistic theory of motivation.** Maslow's 1943 paper on the hierarchy of needs and his later work on self-actualisation supplied the humanistic domain with its developmental account and its rejection of the drive-reduction model of motivation, which held that behaviour is a function of biological need and the reduction of that need. Maslow's claim that there is a higher motivational level at which growth, creativity, and the pursuit of meaning become the primary drivers of behaviour is a claim with real clinical consequences, and it is the ground of the humanistic domain's scepticism about the behavioural account of motivation.

**Behaviour analysis.** Skinner's 1953 *Science and Human Behaviour* and 1957 *Verbal Behaviour* gave behaviourism its mature statement and its most ambitious application, and the applied analysis of behaviour that followed in the 1950s and 1960s produced the token economy, the shaping procedures, the functional analysis, and the systems of behavioural classroom management that Chapter 28 addresses. The 1950s also saw the rise of behaviour modification in the treatment of severe behaviour problems in institutional settings, with the ethical and methodological controversies that Chapter 24 examines.

**The first randomised psychotherapy trial.** The 1961 work comparing client-centred and psychoanalytic therapy, and the 1959 work on neurotic depression treated with drug and placebo conditions, established the methodological template for the psychotherapy research literature: the comparison of two credible treatments, and the demonstration that a placebo condition produced substantial improvement in the absence of the specific ingredient. Chapter 6 treats this literature in detail.

**The stress reaction.** The work of Selye and of the wartime psychiatrists on combat fatigue produced the concept of the stress response, which was to become, forty years later, the physiological foundation of the somatic and trauma-informed approaches of Part VI.

## 5. The Cognitive Turn, 1950–1975

The cognitive revolution in psychology and the parallel development of the cognitive therapies in clinical practice were driven by two problems with behaviourism that could no longer be ignored.

**The first problem was the treatment of cognition itself.** The behaviourist account of language as behaviour under stimulus control could not accommodate the observation that a thought, once available, changes what a person does. Cognitive psychology solved this by treating information processing as the mechanism of behaviour, and clinical practice solved it by treating the content and structure of thought as the appropriate target of intervention. Albert Ellis' rational-emotive behaviour therapy, published in 1962, made the connection explicitly: it applied the ABC model of rational-emotive emotion to the interpretation of events and insisted that the event is not the cause of the emotion, the client's belief about the event is.

**The second problem was the explanatory poverty of behaviourism about common phenomena.** Behaviourism could explain acquisition and extinction with great precision and had little to say about the phenomenon of insight, the phenomenon of expectancy, the phenomenon of the placebo effect, or the enormous variance in the response to a given treatment. George Kelly's 1955 *Personal Constructs* supplied the most ambitious of the alternative accounts, proposing that each person constructs a personal theory of the world and that psychological difficulty is a consequence of a non-adaptive or incoherent personal construct system. Aaron Beck's work, beginning in the 1950s with depressed outpatients at the Philadelphia VA hospital and consolidated in 1976, translated Kelly's constructs into a clinically usable method.

**The social learning revolution.** Bandura's 1969 *Principles of Behavior* and 1977 *Social Learning Theory* supplied the mechanism that the cognitive therapists needed: the acquisition and maintenance of behaviour is a matter of modelling, observation, and self-efficacy rather than of direct reinforcement alone. Bandura's demonstration that people learn behaviour by observing models without any reinforcement to the observer was a serious challenge to the behaviourist account, and his self-efficacy theory became a mechanism of change in its own right, later revised in the 1990s into the "self-efficacy and coping" model, which Chapter 45 develops.

**Meichenbaum and the cognitive-behavioural integration.** Donald Meichenbaum's 1975 cognitive-behavioural therapy integrated the behavioural procedures with the cognitive restructuring, and it is from this development that the contemporary cognitive-behavioural therapies emerged, including the treatments for anxiety with children and adolescents that are among the most extensively evaluated interventions in the field.

**The status of the cognitive turn.** By the mid-1970s the cognitive therapies had a coherent theory, a manualised method, a testable model of psychopathology, and a research programme that generated the majority of the field's outcome literature. The "cognitive revolution" in psychology, in the sense of the shift of psychology's centre of gravity from behaviour to internal mental representation, had by then become the field's dominant framework, and the cognitive therapies became its clinical expression.
