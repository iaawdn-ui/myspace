# Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why a theory of counseling developed with adults cannot be transferred to children and adolescents without developmental modification, and identify the modifications in question.
- Describe the major developmental frameworks relevant to practice with young people and the specific clinical contribution each makes.
- Explain the cognitive-developmental changes that occur across the second decade and their implications for the use of cognitive techniques.
- Describe the adolescent identity task, the psychosocial tasks of the school-age years, and the clinical significance of each.
- Explain the developmental course of attachment, the attachment classifications, and the clinical implications for work with adolescents.
- Summarise the neurodevelopmental evidence on the adolescent brain and distinguish the defensible claims from the popular ones.
- Apply the developmental perspective to a case, and identify the points at which a therapy has been misapplied to a developmental stage.
- Construct a developmentally appropriate formulation for a young person at a specified stage.

## Chapter Overview

Every theory in this book was developed with adult clients, and the majority were developed with adult clients in outpatient settings in the United States and Western Europe. The student who applies a cognitive therapy developed for a thirty-year-old's depressive appraisal to a nine-year-old's refusal to speak will produce a specific and predictable failure, and it is important to be able to specify that failure rather than merely to note that "developmentally inappropriate" is a phrase.

This chapter supplies the developmental knowledge required to make those modifications. It covers the frameworks that a counselor working with young people needs — the cognitive-developmental, the psychosocial, the attachment, the neurodevelopmental, the family, the moral, and the social — and it organises them around a single clinical question: *what is developmentally expected at this stage, and what is the meaning of the young person's deviation from it?*

The organising argument of the chapter is that the developmental perspective is not a set of facts to be recalled but a discipline of interpretation. A ten-year-old's concrete operational thinking is not a deficit against an adult standard; it is the expected form of thought at that stage, and what it means depends entirely on what the task demands. A fifteen-year-old's risk-taking is not a failure of parenting; it is a probabilistic decision made by an organism with an asymmetry between the reward and the penalty systems. A thirteen-year-old's belief that the world is fundamentally unfair may be a stage-appropriate response to evidence rather than a cognitive distortion. Developmental knowledge, used well, replaces moral judgement with interpretation, and it is the foundation on which the developmental adaptations in every subsequent part of this book rest.

## 1. Why Development Is a Condition of Theory

A developmental perspective has four distinct functions in counseling with young people, and they are frequently confused.

**Interpretation.** The developmental perspective provides the reference point against which behaviour is interpreted. A young person's behaviour is only interpretable against what is expected, and "expected" is a developmental judgement. Without it, practitioners default to moral and cultural judgements, and those judgements are usually the least informed and most biased judgements available.

**Selection.** Development determines which interventions are viable. A child cannot participate in a thought record that requires abstract propositional thought; a school-age child cannot reliably do interoceptive exposure; a young person cannot consent to a procedure whose rationale depends on a concept they do not have. The developmental perspective constrains the treatment plan directly.

**Dosage and timing.** Development determines the pace. The same technique delivered to a nine-year-old and a seventeen-year-old requires a different schedule, a different session length, a different degree of parental involvement, and a different horizon over which change is expected.

**Meaning.** Development determines what the young person is doing. Adolescence is a period in which enormous change is occurring, and much of what presents as pathology in a young person is a normal developmental process that has become stuck or has become pathological in its own right. The diagnostic question is never "is this abnormal?" but "is this developmentally appropriate, and if not, in what way?"

## 2. The History of Developmental Psychology in Counseling

The relationship between developmental psychology and counseling has a notable history, in which the two disciplines were repeatedly connected and repeatedly separated.

The child study movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries established the normative approach: the description of what typical children do at typical ages, and the clinical use of the deviation from the norm. Its limitations were substantial — small samples, unselected, and largely of the privileged — and they have never been fully resolved.

The developmental psychology of the 1930s and 1940s, in the form of the work of Jean Piaget, the normative and clinical work of Arnold Gesell, and the psychoanalytic contributions of Anna Freud and of Erik Erikson, supplied the conceptual framework for the child guidance clinics, and the child guidance clinic is the institutional ancestor of modern child practice.

The 1950s and 1960s brought the "developmental psychopathology" perspective, associated principally with the work of DSM-II and the first DSM-III, which located the origins of adult disorder in childhood and made the developmental course of a disorder a central concern of classification. This work was the most intellectually consequential in the field's history and it is also the work that has been most distorted by subsequent use: the reification of categories in the third edition of the diagnostic manual, and the resulting practice of diagnosing a child with a disorder of unknown course.

The 1980s and 1990s brought the developmental systems framework, associated with the clinical applications of Vygotsky's work and with the developmental psychopathology of the Rochester group, and the field's contemporary attention to the transactional model — in which the child is an active agent in her own development and in which the direction of influence between the child and the environment is reciprocal.

The contemporary position is that developmental psychology and counseling theory must be integrated at the level of the case, and that the integration is a professional skill rather than a theoretical given.

## 3. The Cognitive-Developmental Framework

### 3.1 Piaget's stages and their clinical relevance

Piaget's account of cognitive development has four stages, and the clinical relevance of each is different.

**The sensorimotor stage (birth to approximately two years)** is of limited direct relevance to the counseling practitioner, though it is foundational for the attachment literature and for the understanding of early adversity.

**The preoperational stage (approximately two to seven years)** is characterised by symbolic thought, egocentrism, centration, and the absence of conservation and of reversible operations. The clinical significance is considerable: the child at this stage cannot hold two perspectives at once, cannot reason backwards from an outcome to a cause, cannot understand that the amount of liquid is unchanged when the shape of the container changes, and cannot reliably distinguish appearance from reality. A young child who is told that a clinician will not tell their mother what they say will not be able to reason about the future consequences of the disclosure, which is one reason why confidentiality with very young children must be explained in concrete, immediate terms rather than in principle.

**The concrete operational stage (approximately seven to eleven years)** brings logical operations on concrete material, the ability to conserve, the ability to take others' perspectives, and the beginning of decentration. The clinical significance is that the child at this stage can think logically about concrete situations and can be engaged in problem-solving, rehearsal, and behavioural experiments, but cannot reliably work with abstract propositions, hypothetical situations, or generalisations. This is the stage at which cognitive techniques first become usable, and they must be concrete, visual, and tied to a specific recent event.

**The formal operational stage (approximately eleven and onwards)** brings abstract reasoning, the ability to hypothesise, the ability to consider possibilities, and the capacity for metacognition. The clinical significance is that the young person can now examine evidence, generate alternatives, and evaluate their own thinking — but the capacity is new, uneven, and highly sensitive to context. The adolescent who can reason formally about a hypothetical in a classroom may be entirely unable to do so about their own panic, which is the case because the formal operations are applied to material the young person can control and not to material that is emotionally charged.

**The crucial clinical principle.** The stages are not a ladder to be climbed and not a rigid sequence. A young person's capacity is context-dependent, stress-dependent, and topic-dependent, and a practitioner who concludes "she is too young for cognitive work" from her age alone is as likely to be wrong as one who concludes "she is old enough" from the same data. The developmental question is not "what stage is she in?" but "what can she do with this particular material, today, in this state?"

### 3.2 Vygotsky and the zone of proximal development

Vygotsky's contributions to counseling are less familiar than Piaget's and considerably more useful. The **zone of proximal development** is the distance between what a child can do alone and what she can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other. Its clinical significance is that it identifies the optimal difficulty of a task: a task within the zone can be completed with help, and the help is the mechanism of learning. A task below the zone is trivial and produces boredom; a task above the zone produces helplessness and withdrawal. This is the developmental justification for the graded task hierarchies in behavioural approaches, and it generalises far beyond them.

The related concept of **scaffolding** — the provision of temporary support that is progressively withdrawn as the young person becomes capable — describes the pedagogical relationship in a way that maps directly onto the counselling relationship in work with young people, and it is one of the few places where the developmental literature and the therapy research literature converge cleanly.

The third contribution is the **cultural-historical** account of development, in which higher mental functions originate in social interaction and become internalised. Its clinical significance is that a young person's "intrapsychic" state is partly a record of prior interactions, and that the developmental tasks of adolescence are themselves culturally organised. Chapter 48 draws out the implication.

### 3.3 Information processing and executive function

The third contribution of the developmental cognitive psychology is the account of executive function: working memory, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and planning. The clinical relevance is substantial and often overlooked. A young person with a neurodevelopmental condition may have a working memory that cannot hold a thought record in mind while they examine it, an inhibitory control that cannot suppress an intrusive worry, or a flexibility that cannot generate an alternative appraisal. The cognitive therapist's failure to understand this will lead to a misformulation of what is a motivational problem as a belief problem, and to the delivery of a technique that the young person is developmentally unable to execute.

The clinical implication for technique design is specific: reduce the load on working memory, externalise the material, and structure the task so that each step has one demand on it.

## 4. Psychosocial Development: Erikson and the Contemporary Extensions

Erikson's eight stages remain the most widely used organising framework for psychosocial development in counseling practice, in part because they are memorable and in part because the field has not fully absorbed the more rigorous alternatives. Their clinical value lies in the specification of the developmental *task* and the *virtue* whose failure or distortion characterises the stage, which gives a practitioner a way of describing a young person's difficulty that is developmental rather than moral.

| Stage | Age | Task | Distortion when unresolved | Clinical presentation in counseling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Infancy | 0–1.5 | Trust versus mistrust | Mistrust | Difficulty trusting adults; guardedness; expectation of abandonment |
| Early childhood | 1.5–3 | Autonomy versus shame and doubt | Shame and doubt | Excessive shame; self-doubt; fear of asserting; toilet and autonomy struggles |
| Play age | 3–6 | Initiative versus guilt | Guilt | Guilt-driven perfectionism; difficulty with unstructured play; inhibition of assertion |
| School age | 6–12 | Industry versus inferiority | Inferiority | Academic and social inadequacy; competence doubts; withdrawal from challenge |
| Adolescence | 12–18 | Identity versus role confusion | Role confusion | Diffusion; instability; inability to commit; identity foreclosure |
| Young adulthood | 18–40 | Intimacy versus isolation | Isolation | Disconnection; difficulty with committed relationships; shallow connection |
| Adulthood | 40–65 | Generativity versus stagnation | Stagnation | Meaninglessness; unproductive contribution; mid-life disengagement |
| Later adulthood | 65+ | Integrity versus despair | Despair | Regret; bitterness; fear of death; loss of meaning |

Erikson's framework is used in this book as a heuristic and not as a diagnostic instrument, and the reasons are worth stating: the stages are not empirically derived, the age boundaries are culturally variable, the resolution of a stage is not a binary event but a lifelong process, and the framework was developed in a particular cultural and historical context. The contemporary developments that have extended it are more useful.

**The adolescent identity task** has been substantially revised since Erikson. Current understanding holds that identity in adolescence is not a single achievement but a set of ongoing commitments, explored in multiple domains, that are revised in the light of experience and are not expected to be permanent. The clinical significance is that a young person who is uncertain about their vocational direction, their sexual orientation, their peer group, or their political commitments is not pathological and does not require intervention. What requires intervention is *foreclosure* — a premature and rigid commitment, often made under pressure from family or community, which forecloses further exploration. The clinical task is often to make the possibility of exploration available rather than to resolve the uncertainty.

**The increase in autonomy with age.** The developmental literature on the second decade describes a consistent increase in the young person's autonomy, in the sense of their capacity to make and carry out decisions, and a concurrent increase in conflict with parents. The widely replicated finding of a "mismatch" between the adolescent's and the parent's eveningness chronotype is a small and charming illustration of a general truth: the adolescent is developmentally out of step with the adults around them, and this is not a problem to be solved but a phase to be negotiated. Parent–adolescent conflict that is low in intensity and high in frequency is normal, and a practitioner who treats all conflict as pathological will either pathologise ordinary development or fail to detect the conflict that is genuinely dangerous.

**The self and the "possible self."** The developmental research on the possible self — the version of oneself one is trying to become, along with the fear of becoming it — has direct clinical relevance. Young people's anxiety and depression are strongly associated with the discrepancy between their present and possible selves, and the therapeutic work that follows from this is a values-and-identity work rather than a symptom work. Chapter 37 on emotion-focused therapy and Chapter 51 on integration both draw on it.

## 5. Attachment and Its Developmental Course

### 5.1 The theory

The attachment theory originated in John Bowlby's work on the effects of separation and on the motivational systems underlying proximity-seeking, and it was developed empirically through Mary Ainsworth's observations of infant–mother interaction in the "Strange Situation," a procedure that classifies the quality of infant attachment into four categories.

**Secure attachment.** The child uses the caregiver as a secure base, explores freely when the caregiver is present, and is readily comforted. The caregiver is responsive and available.

**Insecure-avoidant attachment.** The child does not seek proximity and does not show distress on separation, and is self-soothing in the caregiver's presence. In the literature this pattern is associated with a history of unresponsive or rejecting caregiving, and the child's behaviour is understood as a suppression of the attachment system in the presence of a caregiver whose availability is unpredictable or whose response to distress is unhelpful.

**Insecure-resistant (or anxious-ambivalent) attachment.** The child seeks proximity but is not readily soothed, and is preoccupied with the availability of the caregiver. This pattern is associated with caregiving that is inconsistently responsive.

**Disorganized attachment.** The child displays contradictory, confused, or frightened behaviour in the presence of the caregiver, including approach and avoidance simultaneous with freezing. This classification was added by Mary Main and her colleagues and is associated with trauma, with maltreatment, and with unresolved loss or trauma in the caregiver's own history.

### 5.2 The developmental extensions

Three developments have extended attachment theory in ways that matter for adolescent practice.

**Internal working models.** Bowlby's proposal that the child constructs internal working models of the self and of others from the early attachment relationship has been developed into the most influential account of the adolescent's relational expectations. The adolescent who expects that others are unavailable will behave in ways that produce unavailability, and the practitioner who reads the adolescent's pushing away as evidence of a lack of investment is making exactly the error the theory predicts.

**The developmental transition at adolescence.** The developmental literature has established that adolescence involves a renegotiation of the attachment system in which the young person moves from the childhood state of dependence on caregivers toward a position of increasing autonomy, and that the process is observable in the changing quality of the parent–adolescent relationship, in the increasing salience of peers as attachment figures, and in the adolescent's increasing capacity to manage the attachment system without the caregiver present. The clinical implication is that parent–adolescent conflict is a normal feature of this renegotiation, and that the question is whether the renegotiation is proceeding or has stalled.

**The peer attachment and the capacity for intimacy.** The developmental literature on peer relationships in the second decade establishes the increasing significance of peers as attachment figures, the developmental importance of the capacity to sustain a mutual, reciprocal relationship, and the association of the failure to develop that capacity with a range of later difficulties. The clinical relevance is that the peer relationships of an adolescent are a legitimate target of the work and not merely a distraction from it, and that a young person who has no friends is a young person with a developmental task uncompleted rather than with a social skill deficit.

### 5.3 The clinical use of attachment

The attachment literature is used in three ways in counseling practice.

**As a lens on the relationship.** A practitioner who understands attachment can interpret the young person's behaviour in the consulting room with much greater precision: the young person who cannot tolerate the practitioner's leaving the room, the one who is extremely compliant, the one who is testing and aggressive, the one who is charming and evasive, and the one who is indifferent are each displaying a particular attachment strategy, and each strategy is an attempt to manage a specific kind of expectation.

**As a framework for the assessment of caregiving.** The developmental and empirical literature on the parent–adolescent relationship has identified the features of caregiving that are associated with secure attachment in the adolescent years: warmth and responsiveness, a degree of autonomy-support, consistency, and a specific set of processes that have been called "child-centred" or "attachment-focused" parenting. Chapter 33 applies this to the practical task of working with parents.

**As a caution about causal claims.** The attachment literature contains a substantial number of correlational findings that are frequently reported as though they were causal, and a student should be cautious. The longitudinal findings are more robust than the cross-sectional ones, and the intervention studies — the video-feedback interventions with parents of infants and toddlers, and the growing body of work with parents of older children — are the appropriate basis for causal claims.

## 6. The Neurodevelopment of the Adolescent Brain

The adolescent brain is the most over-claimed topic in popular developmental psychology, and the student must be able to separate the evidence from the folklore.

### 6.1 What the evidence supports

**Continued myelination of the prefrontal cortex into the mid-twenties.** The prefrontal cortex, and particularly the regions responsible for planning, inhibition, and the evaluation of consequences, continues to myelinate into the early-to-mid twenties, and the grey matter in these regions peaks in early adolescence and is then pruned. The popular image of the adolescent as a "rational person with an un-rational accelerator" is a caricature, but it is a caricature of a real asymmetry.

**A relative acceleration of the socioemotional system.** The limbic and socioemotional regions of the brain mature earlier than the prefrontal regions, and the adolescent is consequently more responsive to social and emotional information, particularly to the emotional responses of others. This supports the observation that the presence of peers escalates both risk-taking and emotional arousal, and that peer relationships are developmentally significant rather than incidental.

**The function of the adolescent's risk-taking.** The probabilistic reasoning research of the 1980s and 1990s, and the subsequent developmental neuroscience, indicate that adolescents do not differ from adults in their valuation of risk so much as in their perception of the uncertainty of outcomes, and in the increased influence of the potential for social reward. The adolescent's risk-taking is, on this account, a rational response to a rational assessment of a different environment, and the adult's prohibition of it is often an unexamined cultural preference rather than a response to a developmental fact.

**Sleep.** Adolescent circadian rhythms shift later during puberty, and the combination of a later phase with early school start times produces chronic sleep restriction in many adolescents, with measurable effects on mood, attention, and behaviour. This is a concrete and modifiable factor in the assessment of an adolescent with attentional or mood problems, and it is routinely overlooked.

### 6.2 What the folklore gets wrong

**"The adolescent brain is damaged"** — there is no evidence of damage, and the popular account of a rewired or malfunctioning brain is a misinterpretation of the normal developmental processes of myelination and synaptic pruning.

**"Teenagers cannot think about the future"** — the research shows a specific and interesting asymmetry. Adolescents are capable of sophisticated reasoning about the future in the abstract, and of long-term planning, and their deficit is specifically in the domain of the future that is uncertain, that is emotionally charged, and that is not continuously present. The practical implication for practice is specific and useful: the adolescent can engage with a long-term goal in the abstract, and cannot reliably engage with the goal in the presence of an immediate emotional pull. This is why a young person will agree enthusiastically with a plan on Tuesday and abandon it by Thursday, and it is why the plan's immediate steps matter more than its stated horizon.

**"Brain development explains behaviour"** — the neurodevelopmental account is used in the popular press and in some clinical writing as a determinative explanation, and it is not. Brain development describes a propensity; it does not explain a particular young person's decisions, and it is not a useful basis for declining to hold a young person responsible.

### 6.3 The clinical implications

Three implications are worth extracting, and they are the basis of the developmental modifications throughout this book.

**The environment must be structured rather than the intention strengthened.** Because the adolescent's capacity for self-regulation is genuinely immature in the presence of emotional arousal, the effective intervention is environmental: earlier bedtimes, a predictable timetable, planned responses, a parent who manages the transition home from school. The "just try harder" approach fails for reasons that are developmental, not motivational.

**Emotion precedes cognition in the moment of difficulty.** When an adolescent is emotionally aroused, the prefrontal capacity for reflective appraisal is reduced, and the cognitive techniques that depend on it are unavailable. The clinical consequence is that the cognitive work must be done when the young person is not in the middle of the difficulty, and the practice of pre-agreed coping strategies for the moment of difficulty is not a supplement to the cognitive work but a precondition for it.

**The peer context is developmentally significant.** Whatever the adolescent does in the presence of peers, they will do more of. The clinical implication is that working with a young person's peers, where this is possible and safe, is not a distraction from the individual work.
