
## 7. Assignment Four: Observed Skills Practice

**Task.** In a structured practice with a fictional adolescent or family scenario, demonstrate a theory-informed intervention segment and submit a written rationale and reflection.

Assessment focuses on:

- contracting, consent, and safety;
- relationship and listening;
- accurate use of theory-linked skills;
- developmental and cultural responsiveness;
- pacing, boundaries, and response to emotion;
- avoiding harm, coercion, and inappropriate disclosure;
- recognising what did not work;
- identifying next steps.

Students are not assessed on producing a miraculous outcome in fifteen minutes. They are assessed on judgement, process, ethics, and learning. Alternative formats should be available where disability, language, or other access needs require, while preserving the capability being assessed.

## 8. Assignment Five: Professional Learning Portfolio

The portfolio may include:

- a revised formulation showing how feedback changed thinking;
- a cultural humility reflection linked to concrete practice decisions;
- an evidence summary used in teaching or placement;
- a supervision or peer feedback response;
- an ethics analysis;
- a personal development plan with limits of competence.

Reflection should not be graded by amount of personal disclosure. Students may analyse fictional or anonymised material. The criterion is quality of reasoning, connection to evidence and professional standards, and specificity of future action.

## 9. Rubric for Case Conceptualisation

| Criterion | Excellent | Proficient | Developing | Insufficient |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formulation | Integrated, developmental, cultural, concise, and testable | Coherent with most relevant factors | Descriptive or partly list-like | Retells case or relies on diagnosis alone |
| Theory use | Theories are compared, sequenced, and linked to mechanisms | Theories are applied accurately | Theory named but weakly linked | Techniques are listed without rationale |
| Planning | Prioritised, feasible, collaborative, and safety-aware | Clear goals and interventions | Generic or overloaded plan | Unsafe, impractical, or missing plan |
| Evidence | Current, critically appraised, youth-relevant | Relevant evidence used accurately | Evidence mentioned superficially | Unsupported claims or adult evidence assumed transferable |
| Ethics and culture | Power, consent, equity, and context shape decisions | Main ethical and cultural issues addressed | Generic statements | Issues ignored or stereotyped |
| Evaluation | Measures, review points, and change criteria are explicit | Outcomes identified | Vague intention to review | No evaluation |
| Communication | Clear, respectful, professional, well organised | Generally clear | Difficult to follow in places | Incoherent or disrespectful language |

Rubrics guide judgement; they do not replace it. Borderline and failing work should receive marker comments tied to criteria and, where institutional rules require, second marking or moderation.

## 10. Rubric for Observed Practice

| Criterion | Indicators of strong performance |
|---|---|
| Contracting | Purpose, confidentiality limits, consent, time, and choices are clear |
| Relationship | Warmth, attention, pacing, and respect are sustained without over-familiarity |
| Theory application | Interventions follow from a stated model and are adapted in real time |
| Developmental fit | Language, autonomy, family context, and capacity are appropriately addressed |
| Cultural responsiveness | Assumptions are checked; identity and power are handled respectfully |
| Safety | Emotion, risk cues, boundaries, and stopping points are managed responsibly |
| Reflection | Student identifies strengths, errors, alternatives, and supervision questions accurately |

A serious ethical breach—such as coercing disclosure, inventing confidentiality promises, or ignoring expressed withdrawal of assent—cannot be offset by fluent technique and should be handled under institutional fitness-to-practise procedures where applicable.

## 11. Feedback, Moderation, and Integrity

Feedback should identify what met the standard, what did not, why it matters, and what to do next. It should arrive while students can still use it. Audio or annotated feedback may help, provided accessibility and records are managed.

Moderation includes calibration meetings, sample second marking, review of grade distributions, external examining where applicable, and analysis of differential outcomes. Markers should discuss anonymised examples before assessing high-stakes work.

Integrity rules must address plagiarism, contract cheating, unauthorised collaboration, fabricated practice evidence, misuse of client data, and generative AI. Policies should distinguish permitted study support from submitting generated work as one's own analysis. Students should declare assistance where required and remain accountable for accuracy, confidentiality, and reasoning.

No identifiable client material may be placed into external AI systems or public platforms. Even anonymised cases can be identifiable in small communities; institutional guidance governs.

## 12. Accessibility and Fairness

Assessment design should provide clear instructions, examples of structure without model answers that invite copying, reading lists in accessible formats, and reasonable adjustments. Extra time, assistive technology, alternative oral or written formats, and adjusted practice settings should preserve the assessed capability while removing irrelevant barriers.

Group work requires individual accountability and processes for conflict, unequal labour, caring responsibilities, and accessibility. Peer assessment should be formative unless carefully moderated.

Students should have access to criteria before submission, formative opportunities, academic skills support, and transparent late submission, extension, resubmission, and appeals procedures.

## 13. Using Assessment Data

Course teams should review:

- outcome-level performance patterns;
- marker consistency;
- differential attainment and access;
- student feedback and workload;
- skills that remain weak across cohorts;
- whether cases and examples reflect diverse youth contexts;
- whether graduates can transfer theory into practice.

Changes should be documented in course review. If students perform well on essays but poorly in formulation, teaching needs more worked examples and formative case practice. If skills are technically fluent but ethically weak, laboratory supervision and criteria require attention.

## 14. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Assessment blueprint** — A map linking outcomes, tasks, weighting, criteria, and quality assurance.
- **Validity** — The extent to which assessment supports intended interpretations and decisions.
- **Reliability** — Consistency of assessment judgements.
- **Moderation** — Processes that calibrate and verify marking standards.
- **Constructive alignment** — Coherence among outcomes, learning activities, and assessment.
- **Fitness to practise** — Professional suitability requirements concerning safety, conduct, and competence.
:::

## 15. Chapter Summary

The assessment system for CAC. 701 should model the counselling it teaches: clear contracting, developmental sensitivity, cultural fairness, evidence-informed judgement, useful feedback, ethical boundaries, and review. Students pass not by sounding like theorists, but by demonstrating that they can use theory to help a young person safely, coherently, and accountably.

## 18. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For assessment design.** Biggs and Tang provide constructive alignment; Sadler and Hattie explain feedback that changes learning.

**For rubrics.** Use rubrics with exemplars, calibration, and narrative comments. A grid without marker judgement creates false precision.

**For integrity and accessibility.** Follow current institutional policy and professional standards, especially where practice material, disability adjustments, and digital tools are involved.
