# Termination, Evaluation, and the Ethics of Endings

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why endings are active therapeutic events rather than administrative afterthoughts.
- Distinguish planned termination, transfer, interruption, dropout, and service-imposed ending.
- Prepare adolescents and families for endings from the beginning of treatment.
- Evaluate progress, consolidate learning, and plan relapse prevention.
- Manage practitioner transitions and endings caused by placement, funding, school leaving, or risk escalation.
- Address abandonment, dependency, gratitude, anger, and unfinished business ethically.
- Write closing summaries and referrals that protect continuity and dignity.

## Chapter Overview

Every counselling relationship ends. It may end because goals are achieved, time expires, school terms turn, a practitioner leaves, a family moves, risk requires another service, or the young person stops attending. Endings can consolidate autonomy or repeat experiences of rejection and disappearance. Ethical practice therefore treats termination as part of treatment from the first session.

## 1. Why Endings Matter Developmentally

Adolescents are already negotiating separations: changing schools, shifting friendships, leaving home, losing relatives, moving between care placements, and approaching adult services. Counselling endings can echo these transitions. A young person with abandonment sensitivity may test the ending by missing sessions, devaluing progress, becoming suddenly worse, or leaving first. These responses are not manipulation by default; they are information about attachment, control, and grief.

A planned ending offers a different experience: the date is known, feelings can be named, progress is reviewed, anger and sadness are permitted, and help-seeking in the future is framed as competence rather than failure.

## 2. Types of Ending

**Planned mutual termination** follows goal review and relapse planning.

**Time-limited ending** is built into a brief model and should be explicit from the outset.

**Transfer** occurs when needs exceed competence or setting, practitioner leaves, or a specialist service is indicated.

**Interruption** may result from illness, placement move, family crisis, transport failure, or school exclusion. It calls for active follow-up within policy.

**Dropout or unilateral ending** may signal dissatisfaction, improvement, practical barriers, shame, family conflict, or avoidance. Services should attempt respectful contact without harassment.

**Service-imposed ending** due to funding, age limits, non-attendance policies, or risk thresholds requires transparency, appeal routes where available, and transition planning. Calling it “client disengagement” does not erase institutional responsibility.

## 3. Beginning with the End in Mind

At intake, explain likely duration, review points, and what may trigger referral or ending. For time-limited work, name the number of sessions when known. For open-ended work, schedule periodic reviews.

Questions include:

- How will we know this has helped enough?
- What would you like to be different by our review?
- What concerns you about ending or changing counsellor?
- Who else should be involved in planning next steps?

This is not pessimism. A visible horizon helps clients use time and reduces dependence on the counsellor as the sole container of progress.

## 4. Evaluating Whether to End

Evaluation draws on symptoms, functioning, goals, relationships, participation, safety, client experience, and developmental trajectory. A young person may still have symptoms yet possess skills, supports, and a plan that make counselling no longer the right intensity. Another may score below a clinical threshold but remain isolated and unsafe.

Consider:

1. Have agreed goals been met or sufficiently progressed?
2. Can gains be maintained with ordinary supports?
3. Are risks understood and managed by a wider plan?
4. Has the young person internalised skills and help-seeking routes?
5. Are family and school responses supporting change?
6. Would continued sessions add value or create dependency?
7. Is another service better matched to remaining needs?

Ending from counselling does not mean ending all support. Step-down may involve mentoring, school check-ins, family support, peer groups, primary care, or planned booster sessions.

## 5. The Termination Phase

The final phase usually includes reviewing the original concerns, identifying changes and how they occurred, naming unresolved issues, rehearsing future challenges, updating safety and relapse plans, and deciding what records or summaries will be shared.

Young people may benefit from concrete artefacts: a coping card, letter to future self, timeline of progress, list of trusted adults, or summary in their own words. Such artefacts require consent and should not disclose sensitive material if lost or shared.

Booster sessions can be ethical when planned with purpose and limits. Open-ended “come back any time” promises should not be made where service rules do not permit them.
