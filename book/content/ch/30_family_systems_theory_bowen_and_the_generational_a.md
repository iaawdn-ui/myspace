# Family Systems Theory: Bowen and the Generational Project

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of the family systems tradition, from the early family therapy movement to Bowen's work and its descendants.
- State the eight core concepts of Bowen's family systems theory and explain the clinical significance of each.
- Explain the concept of differentiation of self, and distinguish it from individuation, autonomy, and emotional detachment.
- Describe the formation and function of triangles, and explain why almost all clinical material with a young person is triadic.
- Describe the nuclear family emotional process, including the concept of the emotional cutoff and the generation of the family projection process.
- Construct a genogram, and explain its uses in assessment and in intervention.
- Apply the Bowen framework to a case involving an adolescent and a family, and evaluate the limits of the framework.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of generational family systems work.

## Chapter Overview

Bowen's family systems theory is unusual in the counselling field in two respects that a student should appreciate before reading the theory.

The first is that it is a **theory of the whole of a person's life in a small number of concentric circles**. Bowen did not treat the family as one system among many, nor as a context for an individual problem. He treated the family as the fundamental unit of emotional life, and he described the person's functioning as a series of nested systems — the two-person dyads, the triangles, the family subsystems, the family, the extended family, and the wider community and culture — each of which is governed by the same processes at a different scale. A young person's symptom is therefore not a disorder located in the individual but a symptom of a disturbance in the process of the family, and it can be read at every level simultaneously.

The second is that it is a **theory with a clinical method attached to the assessment**. Bowen developed the genogram — the family diagram — as a clinical instrument, and his use of it to trace the patterns of relationship across three or four generations is the most distinctive contribution of the tradition to the practice of counselling. The genogram does something that no other clinical tool does: it makes the intergenerational dimension of a young person's difficulty visible, and it makes it visible in a form that a family can look at together.

The theory's weaknesses are as well known as its strengths, and they should be understood before the theory is applied. Bowen did not conduct controlled outcome research, and the evidence base for the theory as a whole is weak, with the evidence concentrated on specific applications such as family-based treatment of eating disorders and of substance use, where the family component has a demonstrable effect. The theory's assumption that the family is the fundamental unit of emotional life does not hold in the same way for every young person, and the account of the family as a system of emotional processes has been criticised for a tendency to locate the problem in the family when the problem has been located in the wider social structure.

This chapter presents the theory in its technical form, the genogram as a clinical method, and the adaptations that the work with young people requires.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The origins of family therapy

Family therapy emerged in the United States in the 1950s, and it emerged because of a clinical problem that the individual model could not solve: a substantial number of patients improved in individual therapy and then relapsed as soon as they returned to their families. The observation was so consistent that it forced a reorientation, and the reorientation took the form of a simple and radical proposition — that the family, not the individual, is the unit of treatment, and that the symptom is a function of the family's structure and its patterns of interaction.

The early work was conducted in two settings that were quite unlike any contemporary mental health service. The first was the **child guidance clinics**, where the child was understood to be the identified patient and the family was understood to be the context; the second was the **institutions**, where the deinstitutionalisation movement exposed the extent to which chronic disturbance was maintained by the institutional structure itself.

The field's founding figures were diverse in background and in method, and the diversity is instructive. **Murray Bowen**, a psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, developed the family systems theory and the genogram. **Virginia Satir**, a social worker, developed a humanistic and skills-oriented family therapy. **Salvador Minuchin**, a psychiatrist, developed the structural model. **Jay Haley**, a communications specialist, developed the strategic and communication approaches. **Carl Whitaker**, an experiential therapist, developed a distinctive and influential model of the family as a developmental context.

The division that emerged between the "structural" and "strategic" camps, and later between Bowen and the others, is partly a matter of theory and partly a matter of professional temperament, and a student should know that the family therapy field has never been a unified discipline.

### 1.2 Bowen and the generation of the idea

Bowen's training was in psychoanalysis, and he was in the unusual position of being able to see the limits of the individual model from within it. His central clinical observation was that his analysands' families reproduced their symptoms in the session: a patient who was withdrawn in the consulting room had a family that was warm and intrusive in the family room, and a patient who was anxiously enmeshed had a family that was cool and distant at home.

The implication was that the anxiety was not located in the individual but was a *product of the relationships*, and that the individual patient's symptom was a consequence of the position he occupied in the family system. This is the shift from intrapsychic to interpersonal causation, and it is the shift that the entire family systems tradition rests on.

Bowen's theoretical contribution was to make the interpersonal shift *quantitative* and *generational*. The family was not a single system; it was a nested set of systems governed by the same processes at different scales, and the processes had a lineage that ran through the generations.

### 1.3 The development of the tradition

Bowen's own practice was unusual. He worked with families of varying composition, he used the genogram as a routine part of assessment, he was interested in the transmission of emotional processes across generations, and he formulated in a language that was intended to be shared with the family rather than concealed from it. He was also, characteristically, cautious about the ambition of the systemic work: he described his approach as a way of *thinking about* a family rather than a technique for *treating* one.

The descendants of the tradition are numerous. **Murray Bowen, Jr.** and the group at the Family Institute in Washington developed Bowenian family therapy and the clinical training that carries his work forward. **Carl Whitaker's** experiential family therapy, developed in parallel, is distinctive in its attention to the emotional experience of the family members and in its refusal to take the identified patient's account at face value. **Murray Bowen's** articulation also informed the **structural** and **strategic** schools, which are the subjects of Chapters 31 and 32.

### 1.4 The reception

Two distinct lines of reception are important for a student.

**The clinical reception.** Bowenian family therapy is widely used in academic training and in a minority of clinical settings, and it is influential well beyond its direct practice, because the genogram is now a standard tool in assessment in family therapy, in safeguarding, in school counselling, and in adoption and fostering practice.

**The critical reception.** The theory has been criticised on several grounds, and each is worth stating because each identifies a real limitation.

**It is empirically thin.** Bowen did not conduct controlled outcome research, and the research literature on Bowenian family therapy is small. Where family-based approaches have been shown to work, the demonstrations are usually of components — family-based treatment of adolescent eating disorders, family therapy for substance use, behavioural family therapy for conduct problems — rather than of Bowen's theory as a whole.

**It risks a circularity.** If a young person's difficulty is understood as a product of the family, and the family's patterns are understood as the products of earlier generations, and the earlier generations are understood in the same terms, then the young person can end up as the tip of a lineage rather than as a person, and the clinical work can end up directed at the ancestors.

**It under-accounts for the structural.** The theory treats the family as a system of emotional processes, and it can under-specify the material and political realities within which the family lives: poverty, racism, migration, unemployment, housing, and the effects of state decisions on the family's capacity to function. A formulation that attributes a family's difficulties to its internal emotional process risks locating the problem in the family when the problem has been located in the conditions.

**It assumes a family.** The theory presupposes a coherent and continuing family system. For a young person in care, for a young person separated from parents, for a young person in a family that has been dissolved by migration, conflict, or violence, the presupposition does not hold, and the practitioner must adapt the theory substantially or work with a different one.

## 2. The Systems Perspective

### 2.1 The eight core concepts

Bowen's family systems theory is conventionally described in terms of eight core concepts, and a student should be able to state all eight and give an example of each in a family with a young person.

1. **Two-person systems**, in which the relationship between two individuals is the primary unit of emotional life.
2. **Differentiation of self**, the degree to which a person can maintain a clear sense of self within an emotional relationship.
3. **Triangles**, the three-person systems that form when the tension between two people is relieved by the involvement of a third.
4. **Nuclear family emotional process**, the transfer of emotional process from one generation to the next, transmitted through the parental subsystem.
5. **Emotional cutoff**, the process by which a family member distances from the family in order to avoid anxiety, and the transmission of that cutoff across generations.
6. **The multigenerational transmission process**, the description of how emotional processes are transmitted across generations.
7. **The family projection process**, the mechanism by which the parental subsystem transmits its own emotional process to a child.
8. **Sibling position**, the way in which a child's position in the birth order shapes the family's emotional process.

Two further concepts, the **sociocultural emotional process** and the **family emotional system**, complete the framework. The sociocultural process describes the way in which the emotional processes of a family are shaped by the wider culture, and it is the concept through which the theory can be brought to bear on the structural questions that the theory otherwise under-specifies.

### 2.2 The nested systems

The defining feature of the framework is that it is **scale-invariant**: the same processes operate at every level of the system.

![Bowen's nested systems, showing the concentric levels from the two-person dyad through the triangle and the family subsystems to the wider family, the community, and the culture.](fig50_bowen_systems.png)

At the level of the **two-person system**, the fundamental emotional reality of the human being is the capacity to relate to another. Bowen was explicit that this is a level of *emotional* relationship and not of *rational* contract, and the distinction matters: a person can be entirely clear about the terms of a relationship and entirely unable to maintain a defined self within it.

At the level of the **triangle**, tension between two people is relieved by the involvement of a third, and the two sides of the triangle are in a state of tension with each other while the third is in a state of tension with both. Almost all emotional conflict in families involves triangles, and the practitioner who cannot identify the triangle will not understand the case.

At the level of the **family**, the sub-systems — the parental, the sibling, and the individual — are pulled towards a lower level of differentiation under stress, and the symptom is the mechanism by which the tension is held in the system.

At the level of the **extended family and the community**, the processes continue, and the community and the culture become the wider contexts in which the family negotiates.

### 2.3 The governing laws

The framework is described by two governing laws, and they are the most quoted and least understood part of it.

**Law one: the preservation of self.** Bowen drew this from William James and from the philosophical literature, and it states that the organism is a whole and that the function of the whole is to preserve the whole — that is, the individual. The "self" in the law is a functional term: it refers to the capacity of the organism to maintain its integrity and to function as a whole.

**Law two: the preservation of the family** — or, in its common formulation, the emotional system of the family is more important than any individual member of it.

The two laws are in tension, and the tension is the source of the clinical problems the theory describes. Under stress, the family system pulls the individual towards the family (law two), and the individual resists (law one), and the visible result is a symptom in one of the members.

A student should be able to explain the laws and their tension, and should resist the interpretation — which is widespread in the popular literature — that the theory counsels self-sacrifice or family loyalty. It does not. It says that the pull towards the family and the push towards the self must be balanced in a way that permits both, and that the balance is called differentiation.

## 3. Differentiation of Self

### 3.1 The concept

**Differentiation of self** is the degree to which a person can maintain a sense of self within an emotional relationship — the capacity to think, to act, and to feel in a way that is determined by one's own convictions rather than by the emotional pressure of the relationship, while remaining fully engaged in the relationship.

Three elements are involved: the **thoughts**, which are not contaminated by the emotional pressure of the situation; the **feelings**, which are not overwhelmed by the pressure of the situation; and the **actions**, which are not determined by the pressure of the situation. The degree of differentiation is the degree to which these three are congruent with each other and with the person's own position.

The key phrase is *within an emotional relationship*. Differentiation is not the same as independence, and a person who leaves the family, or who refuses to feel anything, or who maintains a position at the cost of every relationship, is not differentiated. And it is not the same as emotional detachment, which is the avoidance of emotional involvement in order to protect the self.

### 3.2 The two extremes

The two poles of the continuum describe the two characteristic family positions.

**The undifferentiated position** is characterised by the flooding of thought, feeling, and action by the emotional process of the relationship. The person cannot think clearly in the presence of the conflict, cannot modulate the feeling, and acts impulsively or rigidly. In the family this position is often occupied by the person who is the most involved in the family's emotional process.

**The cut-off position** is characterised by the reduction of emotional involvement in order to preserve the self. The person thinks and acts clearly, but at the cost of feeling, and the feeling is often displaced into physical symptoms, into a narrow pursuit, or into the body. The cut-off is a *process* and not a position, and it is a process in motion, which is why the families of cut-off young people often describe an abrupt disconnection — a departure, a marriage, a collapse in contact.

Between the two poles lies the differentiated position, in which a person can be close and can be autonomous, can feel deeply and can think clearly, and can disagree and can remain in the relationship.

### 3.3 The clinical significance

Differentiation is the central clinical concept, and it operates at two levels: the intrapersonal and the interpersonal.

**Intrapersonal differentiation** is the difference between the person's various states. A differentiated person can recognise that the anxiety she feels about her mother's phone call is not the same thing as the anger she feels about the last thing her mother actually said, and can distinguish between the fact and the interpretation, and can act on the fact.

**Interpersonal differentiation** is the difference between the people in the relationship. A differentiated parent can hold two different views — "my daughter is suffering" and "my daughter is also refusing to see me" — without collapsing the one into the other, and can express both to the daughter at the same time.

The clinical targets follow directly. A family with a symptomatic young person typically contains at least one undifferentiated member, whose flooding is transmitted to the others, and typically contains at least one cut-off member, whose disconnection leaves the family to manage its own anxiety. The work is to increase the differentiation of the family, and the symptom is what will reduce as a by-product.

![The two poles of differentiation, showing the movement of thought, feeling, and action from the undifferentiated position through the differentiated position to the cut-off position.](fig51_emotional_process.png)

### 3.4 Emotional process in the body

Bowen's clinical observation that the emotional process is transmitted through the body is one of the most immediately useful and least theoretical parts of the tradition, and it has a direct place in a book for practitioners who work with young people.

The observation is that family members' bodies synchronise in the presence of tension. Posture, breathing, heart rate, and muscle tension change together, and the change is observable in a session within minutes, and the practitioner can use it as a data source. Bowen wrote about the "body copy" of the family emotional process, and the observation is the ancestor of the somatic and interpersonal neurobiological work in Part VI.

For a practitioner, three practical consequences follow. The young person's somatic symptoms may be a direct expression of the family process, and the treatment of a recurrent unexplained physical complaint should include a look at the family system. The practitioner's own body is a diagnostic instrument, and the practitioner can learn to notice their own somatic response to a family member. And a family that is being asked to talk about its emotional process should be prepared for the somatic experience of doing so, and the practitioner should expect an increase in somatic presentation in the early sessions.

## 4. Triangles

### 4.1 The formation of a triangle

A **triangle** forms when the tension between two people is relieved by the involvement of a third, and it has two sides. In a **comfortable triangle**, the two sides are in tension with each other and the third person is in tension with both. In a **conflictful triangle**, the two sides are in tension with each other and the third person is in tension with one side and aligned with the other.

The transfer of tension into a triangle is a universal human process and it happens in the consulting room as well as in the home. A young person who is anxious about a parent's marriage will frequently become the focus of the parents' conflict, and the parental conflict will frequently stop when the young person becomes symptomatic, because the symptom has provided a third position that both parents can occupy.

### 4.2 The clinical importance of triangles

**Almost all emotional conflict is triadic.** A practitioner who works with the identified patient alone is working with one vertex of a triangle and has no information about the other two. This is a practical argument for including the family, and it is an argument the theory makes without any appeal to family loyalty.

**The symptomatic member is usually the most involved.** In a family with a symptomatic young person, the young person is frequently the most enmeshed, and the parents are frequently the more differentiated, and the pull towards the family is what produces the symptom.

**Every triangle must be tracked.** The practitioner working with a family learns to ask, of every conflict between two people, "who is the third?" — and the answer, when it is found, explains the conflict that would otherwise be puzzling.

**The symptom stabilises the triangle.** This is the crucial clinical point, and it explains the family's resistance to change. The symptom is what holds the tension in the family at a manageable level, and removing it without substituting another structure releases the tension, and the released tension is experienced by the family as a catastrophe. A practitioner who removes a symptom quickly and does not replace the triangle structure has produced a worse outcome, and the family will describe the change as an escalation.

The clinical implication is that a plan for a young person whose symptom is doing structural work must include a plan for the family.

### 4.3 The "just" relationship

A specific and useful clinical distinction is between the **intense** and the **"just" relationship**. In the just relationship, the people involved maintain contact and mutual support while keeping the emotional intensity low. A young person who is in a just relationship with a parent continues to see the parent, continues to talk to the parent, and does so without the fusion and the conflict that characterise the intense relationship.

The just relationship is the target of most systemic work with a young person: not the resolution of the conflict but the reduction of the intensity, and the establishment of a workable low-intensity contact.

## 5. The Nuclear Family Emotional Process

### 5.1 The transmission

**Nuclear family emotional process** is the description of the way in which the emotional process of the parental subsystem is transmitted to the children, and it is the mechanism by which the intergenerational transmission occurs.

The transmission is not of content but of **process**. What is transmitted is the *way of handling emotional tension* — the level of differentiation, the capacity to sustain anxiety, the pattern of triangulation, the response to conflict. A parent who cannot tolerate anxiety and who resolves it by triangulation transmits the capacity to tolerate anxiety to the children, and the children develop the same intolerance, and the intolerance is expressed in the next generation as a symptom.

### 5.2 Emotional cutoff

**Emotional cutoff** is the process by which a family member reduces emotional involvement with the family in order to reduce anxiety, and it is the most common response to a family system that is too intense for the individual to bear.

It is worth distinguishing three forms, because they have different clinical consequences. The **healthy cutoff** is a psychological separation that permits the individual to function in a family that cannot be changed; the young person who goes to boarding school and maintains a warm relationship with the parents has made a healthy cutoff. The **internal cutoff** occurs within the family, in which the person remains in contact but ceases to be emotionally available; this is the young person who is physically present and psychologically elsewhere. The **external cutoff** is a physical or social severance; this is the young person who is removed from the family or who removes themself.

### 5.3 The family projection process

The **family projection process** is the mechanism by which the parental subsystem transmits its own emotional process to a child, and it is the specific mechanism that makes the transmission intelligible.

The process has two linked components.

**The projection** is the transmission of the parents' own unresolved emotional issues, attitudes, and emotional needs to the child. The parent projects onto the child a part of their own emotional world, and the child is shaped by the projection.

**The introjection** is the child's absorption of the projected material, which becomes part of the child's own emotional structure. A child who has been the object of a parent's projection of their own anxiety and their own unmet needs will internalise that anxiety and those needs, and will grow up with the sense of being responsible for the regulation of someone else's emotional state.

The clinical significance is the explanation it provides for the young person who is over-responsible, who is the family's emotional caretaker, who cannot let anyone else worry, and who finds it impossible to be in need. This is one of the most recognisable presentations in the family literature, and it is very often mistaken for a young person's character trait.

### 5.4 Sibling position

**Sibling position** — the order, the age gaps, the sex composition, and the number of children in the family — shapes the family emotional process. Bowen described the "sibling position" in terms of the child's place in the birth order and the emotional characteristics associated with the position, and the descriptions are provocative and should be treated as hypotheses rather than as findings.

The useful clinical proposition is the one about the family projections: a family with two children tends to focus its projection on one of them, and the choice is often determined by the compatibility of the child's temperament with the parents' unresolved issues, and the child who is the focus of the projection is frequently the one who presents with the symptom, and frequently the one who is described as the "good" one.

The other useful proposition concerns the **family projection of a sibling position into the parental system**: a child who occupies the position of a parent's own sibling, and who resembles that parent, will receive a particular intensity of projection.

## 6. The Genogram

### 6.1 The instrument

The **genogram** is a family diagram, and it is Bowen's most widely used and most widely borrowed clinical contribution. It extends the pedigree — the standard family tree that traces biological descent — into a map of the *relationships* in the family across three or more generations.

The genogram records: the biological and the marital structure of the family; the names and the ages of the members; and, at a series of concentric levels, the social, psychological, medical, and emotional information about each member.

![A three-generation genogram showing the standard symbols for marital and descent lines, close and conflicted relationships, cut-off, and the triangle.](fig87_genogram.png)


The levels are the key to the instrument. The **social** level records education, occupation, ethnicity, migration, and socio-economic status. The **psychological** level records the significant emotional events — the losses, the abuse, the mental illness, the substance use, the suicide attempts, the relationship ruptures — and the quality of the relationships between the members. The **medical** level records the illnesses, the genetic conditions, and the causes of death.

![The three concentric levels of the genogram, showing the social, psychological, and medical data recorded for each family member.](fig88_genogram_levels.png)

### 6.2 The procedure

The construction of a genogram is a clinical conversation, and it should be done with the family and not about them.

**The first session** establishes the structure: the marriages, the separations, the divorces, the children, the adoptions, the step-relations, the losses, the migration, and the family contacts that have been lost. The practitioner draws the genogram as the family speaks, because the drawing is itself a communication.

**The second session** adds the emotional level: the relationships, the alliances, the conflicts, the estrangements, the closeness. The symbols for these are the ones the family recognises immediately, and the genogram becomes a shared object that the family can see.

**The third session** adds the emotional process: the triangles, the cutoffs, the projections, the areas of intensity and of low intensity.

**The fourth session** addresses the family projection process, and the fifth the multigenerational transmission process.

### 6.3 The uses

The genogram is used for three distinct purposes, and it is worth separating them because they have different status.

**As an assessment instrument**, the genogram is of value in its own right. It is the most efficient way to discover the presence of a pattern that has been operating for three generations, and the discovery of such a pattern often reorganises the family's understanding of its own difficulty. A family that has always understood its difficulty as the fault of one member frequently re-describes it, after the genogram, as a long pattern.

**As a therapeutic instrument**, the genogram is used directly in the work. Bowen regarded the genogram as a way of placing the family in the context of its history, so that the current problem is seen as a legacy rather than as a personal failing, and the process of drawing it — with the family — is itself a form of intervention.

**As a supervision and consultation tool**, the genogram is used to make a case comprehensible to a team, and it is a far more efficient communication format than a narrative formulation.

### 6.4 The limits

Three limits should be named.

**It is a retrospective construction, and memory is unreliable.** The genogram is a family's account of its own history, and families' accounts are shaped by the current relationships. A grandmother's role in the transmission of the pattern may be remembered very differently by the grandmother and by the mother, and the disagreement is itself clinically interesting but it does not resolve the question.

**It can be a burden.** A young person who is shown a genogram that documents three generations of illness, loss, and conflict has been given a substantial amount to carry, and a young person who is handed the family pattern and told that it explains them may experience a loss of agency. The genogram should be used with the young person and not presented to them.

**It is not a research instrument.** Its reliability and validity for research purposes are poor, and a practitioner should not present a genogram as evidence.
