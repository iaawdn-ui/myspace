
## 4. The Phase-Oriented Approach

### 4.1 The rationale

The phase-oriented approach is the practical response to a problem that is specific to this population. The specific trauma-focused protocols are effective and are the best-evidenced treatments available for the specific phobias and the discrete traumas of childhood, and they are not straightforwardly applicable to the developmental presentation, in which there is no discrete target, the affect is chronic rather than situational, and the young person's symptoms are pervasive rather than circumscribed.

The phase-oriented approach therefore divides the work by the state of the young person rather than by the type of trauma, and it is a management framework rather than a treatment protocol: it determines what kind of work is indicated now, and it specifies the conditions under which a different kind of work becomes indicated.

### 4.2 The phases in practice

**Phase one, the stabilisation and the safety.** The work described in Section 2, and the practical and material work: the housing, the income, the school placement, the legal status, the relationships. The phase continues until the criteria for phase two are met, and the criteria are the ones given in Chapter 38.

**Phase two, the processing.** The specific protocol, and the choice of protocol is determined by the presentation: the trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy or the EMDR for a discrete event; the narrative work of Chapter 36 for a young person whose difficulty is organised around a dominant story; the emotion-focused work of Chapter 37 for a young person whose difficulty is organised around an emotion; the attachment-based work of Chapter 34 for a young person whose difficulty is organised around the relationship.

**Phase three, the integration and the rehabilitation.** The rebuilding of the young person's life: the education, the relationships, the futures, the identity. The phase is the most neglected in practice and the most important for the outcome, and the developmental literature is clear that a young person whose trauma has been processed and who has not been helped to build a life is not going to stay well.

### 4.3 The phase transitions as clinical decisions

The phase transitions should be made explicitly and recorded, and the criteria are as given in Chapter 38.

**The transition into phase two is a decision about readiness, and it should be made collaboratively with the young person and, where relevant, with the caregiver.** A young person who does not want to process is not ready, whatever the criteria say, and the work at that point is the work of the phase one, and the practitioner should say so.

**The transition backwards is a decision that requires a formulation.** A young person who deteriorates in phase two is not a failure of the technique; the deterioration is information, and the usual findings are that the young person was not stabilised, or that the caregiver's behaviour has changed, or that a new crisis has occurred, or that the work has gone too fast.

**The transition into phase three is often made too early and is often not made at all**, and the most common reason is that the young person's symptoms have reduced, and the reduction of the symptoms is not the same as the restoration of the life.

### 4.4 The management framework

The phase-oriented approach is usually delivered as a **framework for the whole service** rather than as a treatment for one young person, and in this form it has a number of features that a practitioner should know.

**Phase-specific pathways.** Each phase has its own procedures, its own documentation, and its own review criteria, and the young person's record shows clearly which phase they are in.

**Phase-specific staff.** The practitioners who do the stabilisation work and the practitioners who do the processing work may be different, and this is often a good arrangement, because the demands of the two are different.

**The measurement at each phase.** The measures that are collected at each phase, and the review that compares the phase's outcomes against the criteria for the next.

**The avoidance of the false dichotomy.** The framework avoids the two failure modes: the service that does only stabilisation and never processes, and the service that processes everybody regardless of their readiness.

## 5. Stabilisation with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The developmental modifications

**The skills are taught to the young person, not to the practitioner.** A common error in this work is for the practitioner to be the one who does the grounding — and to be the one who does it for the young person, in the session, every time, which means the young person never acquires the skill and the practitioner never becomes unnecessary.

**The skills are taught in the language of the young person's life.** A twelve-year-old's safe place is a specific and sensory place, and a fifteen-year-old's grounding practice may be a song on a playlist, and the practitioner should ask what the young person already does to feel better and build the programme from that.

**The parent is taught the same skills.** The parent's own regulation capacity is the mediator of the young person's regulation, and the parenting work of Chapter 33 and the parental emotion coaching of Chapter 37 are the vehicles. A parent who is dysregulated cannot co-regulate a child, and the practitioner who teaches the young person the skills and does not teach the parent has done half the work.

**The skills are generalised deliberately.** A skill that is used only in the therapy room is not a skill; the practitioner should think in terms of the contexts in which the young person will need them — the corridor, the bus, the playground, the dinner table — and should plan the practice accordingly.

**The developmental order matters.** A young child is taught the sensory grounding and the safe place; an adolescent is taught the traffic light, the interpersonal skills, and the cognitive reframing of the state.

### 5.2 The presentations

**The young person with dissociation.** The dorsal vagal state is the clinical fact, and the intervention is the orienting and the physical, and the movement-based grounding, and the work on the safety, and the practitioner should not attempt to work with the content.

**The young person with hyperarousal.** The sympathetic state, and the intervention is the reduction and the containment, and the work on the sleep, on the caffeine, on the noise, on the schedule, and the physical work.

**The young person with the persistent somatic presentation.** The somatic work of Chapter 43 is the entry point, and the somatic work is frequently the stabilisation work, and a young person who has been told for years that there is nothing wrong with them will be more willing to engage with a somatic intervention than with a psychological one.

**The young person in an unstable placement.** The stability of the placement is the intervention, and the practitioner who is working on the young person's skills while the placement is in flux is working on the wrong level.

### 5.3 The limits

**Stabilisation does not resolve the trauma.** A young person who is perfectly stabilised and whose trauma is unprocessed will remain vulnerable, and a practitioner who is content with the reduction in the symptoms has substituted symptom management for treatment.

**The skills can be used to avoid.** A young person who has learned the grounding thoroughly can use it to avoid the work, and the practitioner who is pleased by the young person's mastery has been outmanoeuvred, and the question to ask is whether the young person is using the skill in the moment of distress or in the moment of the invitation to the difficult work.

**The phase one can become a waiting room.** The practitioner's anxiety about the processing work is the most common reason for the phase one continuing, and the supervisory question — "what would have to be different for us to move to phase two?" — is the most useful.

**The young person who cannot be stabilised.** Some young people cannot be, and the reasons are usually a continued threat, a caregiver who cannot provide safety, or a neurodevelopmental condition that the stabilisation programme has not been adapted for.

## 6. Case Illustration: The Young Woman Who Was Not in the Room

**Presentation.** Nadia is sixteen, and the referral is from a child and adolescent mental health service that has assessed her and diagnosed a complex post-traumatic stress disorder following a year of sexual abuse by a stepfather. The service has referred her for a trauma-focused protocol. The previous two attempts at therapy have ended: one because she did not attend after the first session, and one because the therapist's approach had made her "worse".

**The assessment.** The practitioner meets Nadia for the assessment, and the first thing they do is not to take a history. The first thing they do is to ask Nadia what happened in the previous therapy. Nadia says: "She kept asking me about it. And I'd go somewhere else. And then she'd get more upset than I had, and I'd be the one apologising." The practitioner says: "That's a specific thing. What would you have needed instead?" And Nadia says: "Somebody to ask me what I needed."

**The phase placement.** The practitioner places Nadia in phase one, and the criteria are explicit in the record: no consistent, safe adult in her life; a mother who is not able to be present with Nadia's distress because her own distress is overwhelming her; a persistent somatic presentation with a pain presentation that has not been medically explained; dissociation in the first session; and no capacity to report the SUD on a memory, which is a prerequisite of the protocol she has been referred for.

**The first three sessions.** The practitioner does not ask about the abuse in the first three sessions. The work is as follows.

*Session one.* The practitioner explains the phase model in plain language: "There are two kinds of work here. One is working out what happened and what it did to you. The other is making sure you can survive the ordinary week and sleep and be in a room with people. Most people I've worked with who had a bad experience find the second one first, because the first one needs you to be able to be in a room. So that's what we're doing first." Nadia says: "How long?" and the practitioner says: "I don't know. It depends how fast you get the skills. And we'll be checking." The practitioner then does the orienting exercise with her, in the room, and Nadia notices that she has been looking at the door the entire time, and the practitioner names this and asks about it, and Nadia says: "I'm waiting for him to come in." The practitioner says: "He's not going to come in here. I want to be clear about that. This room is yours for fifty minutes and he is not in it and he is not going to be in it."

*Session two.* The skills. Nadia chooses the grounding that she finds most tolerable, which is not the sensory one — she says the sensory one makes her think of the specifics — and it is the movement-based one and the time travel to a safe memory. Her safe memory is specific: her grandmother's kitchen, the smell of the bread, the sound of the radio. She practises it with the practitioner, and the practitioner practises with her, and Nadia's shoulders come down, and she notices it, and the noticing is the teaching.

*Session three.* The mother. The practitioner sees the mother alone, and the mother's account is that she did not know, that she is "destroyed", that she cannot sleep, that she is frightened of her own daughter because the daughter screams at her, and that she has been told by a service that Nadia "needs to process" and that she has been told by another service that she "needs to be the parent". The practitioner asks the mother the coping question — "How have you been managing?" — and the mother says: "I've been lying on the floor of the bathroom. That's where I go." The practitioner stays with that, and the mother cries, and the practitioner does not move on, and the mother says: "I've been so frightened of her. And she was the one who was hurt."

**The work with the mother.** The practitioner teaches the mother three things, and the first of them is the traffic light, which the mother learns to apply to her own arousal, and which she initially uses to describe Nadia's. The second is the orienting exercise, which the mother practises in the bathroom when she is at the point at which she would usually go and stand outside Nadia's door. The third is the specific change in the relationship: the mother stops going to Nadia's door when Nadia is in distress, and the practitioner says why: "Every time you go to that door, she learns that the way to get you is to scream, and every time she learns that, she has to scream louder, and she is spending all of her energy on it." The mother says: "But she needs me." And the practitioner says: "She needs you. She doesn't need you at the door while she's screaming. She needs you in the kitchen with a cup of tea and ten minutes of not needing anything from her."

**The assessment of the somatic presentation.** The practitioner arranges a medical review of the abdominal pain and arranges the screening for the physical consequences of the abuse, and the review finds nothing unaccounted for, and the practitioner says to Nadia: "Your body has been through something and it has kept the record. The pain is not imaginary and it is not you being weak. What we are going to do is work with the body directly, and there is a person here who is very good at that, and I would like you to meet her." This is the referral to the somatic work of Chapter 43, and it is done in the first month, and it is the intervention that changes the trajectory of the case.

**The outcome of phase one.** At four months, Nadia is sleeping, is not dissociating in the sessions, has a safe adult in the school — the pastoral support worker, who has been trained in the grounding skills and who checks in with her daily by a specific agreed method — and the mother is no longer lying on the bathroom floor. The somatic work is running. The phase two work has not begun, and the practitioner says so to the referrer, in writing, with the criteria and the current status against each of them.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the central point of the chapter. The referral was for a protocol, and the protocol was the right protocol for a young person who was ready, and Nadia was not ready, and the failure of the previous therapies was a failure of the phase. The work of the first four months was not preparation in the sense of a preliminary; it was the treatment of a young woman whose nervous system was organised for survival and who had no capacity to be in a room. The mother was the decisive intervention, and the decision about the door was the specific act of clinical reasoning that made the rest possible.

## 7. Evidence and Research Support

### 7.1 The status of the evidence

**There is good evidence for the general principle** that the reduction of arousal precedes the cognitive work, and that the reduction is achieved through the body, and that a young person who is dysregulated cannot use the cognitive and the narrative techniques. This principle is well supported.

**The evidence for the individual grounding skills is growing and is positive.** The systematic reviews of the grounding, the stabilisation, and the self-regulation skills report positive effects on the symptoms of distress, on the physiological measures, and on the functional measures, and the most consistent findings are for the paced breathing, the sensory grounding, and the orienting.

**The evidence for the parent training element is the strongest part.** The parental component is the element with the best support, and the evidence for the parenting programmes in this population is good, and this is consistent with the developmental finding that the caregiver's regulation is the mediator of the young person's regulation.

**The evidence for the phase model as an integrated framework is thin**, and the most useful support is indirect: the comparative trials of the specific protocols, and the trials of the stabilisation-only treatments, which do show meaningful improvement in functioning.

**The clinical consensus considerably exceeds the empirical support**, and a student should be able to state this without embarrassment, because it is a true and important feature of the field.

### 7.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The trials are small and heterogeneous**, and the measures are frequently the self-reported distress measures.

**The fidelity of the stabilisation work is difficult to measure**, and the skill is delivered in different forms by different practitioners.

**The long-term outcomes are not well documented**, and the developmental question is unanswered.

**The studies are dominated by the adults**, and the paediatric evidence is smaller.

**The comparative evidence on the phase model against the direct provision of a specific protocol is absent**, and this is the most important missing study in the field.

## 8. Strengths and Limitations of the Stabilisation Work

### 8.1 Strengths

**It is the work that makes the rest possible.** This is its central and sufficient justification.

**It is teachable and it is portable.** A young person who has four or five grounding skills and a traffic light has something that works in the corridor, on the bus, and in the dining hall, and it does not require a therapy appointment.

**It is honest about the timeline.** The skills take weeks to months, and the young person can be told that, and this is a considerable relief to a young person who has been offered a cure in three sessions.

**It gives the young person control.** The traffic light and the self-monitoring move the regulation from the practitioner to the young person, and this is the objective of the phase.

**It addresses the ordinary functioning.** School, sleep, eating, and the relationships are the goals that the young person and the family want, and they are not the goals that the phase model privileges.

**It is the entry point for the somatic work.** The body-based work of Chapter 43 and the stabilisation work are the same work approached from two directions.

### 8.2 Limitations

**It can become a way of avoiding the work**, and this is the most serious limitation and the most common.

**It can be delivered in a way that is custodial.** A young person whose every action is monitored and whose every dysregulation triggers a response has not been given a skill; they have been given a more sophisticated supervision, and this is a real and documented failure mode in services that use the language of safety.

**The evidence for the individual skills is weaker than the practice assumes.**

**It does not address the source of the dysregulation.** If the threat continues, the skills will be used to survive it, which is what they are for, and the young person will remain dysregulated, and this is the correct behaviour and it is not a recovery.

**It can be infantilising.** The techniques were developed for adults and for young children, and the adaptation for an adolescent is a genuine design task, and a practitioner who teaches a sixteen-year-old a "safe place" in a bubble with a rabbit will lose the young person.

## 9. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 Culture and the body

**The body is not a universal language.** The somatic vocabulary of the stabilisation work — the grounding through the senses, the body scan, the attention to the body's signals — assumes that the body is a legitimate object of attention and that the young person has permission to attend to it. In some cultures and in some families, the body is a private and a shameful object, and the young person will not want to be asked where they feel it.

**Some of the grounding techniques are culturally inappropriate.** The "safe place" in the form of an imagined serene location is one cultural form; in some traditions, the safe place is a specific person, or a specific practice, or a specific place of worship, and a practitioner who insists on the internalised version has missed the point.

**The physiological claims are not universally applicable.** The breathing practices are drawn from a specific and largely Western scientific account, and a young person who has been taught a different breathing practice in their own tradition should be helped to adapt it rather than to abandon it.

**The movement-based grounding is the most broadly acceptable**, and it is often the entry point for a young person for whom the internal, verbal, and cognitive forms do not work.

### 9.2 Ethical considerations

**The relationship between the skills and the care.** A young person who has learned the grounding skills and who finds that the skills are used by the adults to manage them has been taught a tool for surviving the adults' interventions, and the practitioner should be alert to this.

**The risk of the custody model.** Every safety plan and every traffic light in a service is a piece of documentation, and a young person whose every action is logged has been placed in a form of soft containment, and the practitioner should ask routinely whether the documentation is a clinical tool or a control mechanism.

**The consent to the skills.** The young person should be able to decline a technique, and a young person who declines the body scan should not be pressed, and the alternatives should be offered.

**The handling of the disclosure during stabilisation.** A young person who discloses in the middle of a grounding exercise has disclosed, and the practitioner must know the reporting obligations and must not be so preoccupied with the technique that the disclosure is mishandled.

**The meaning of "safe".** A practitioner who tells a young person that the room is safe is making a promise, and a young person who has been abused in a room with an adult has good reason to doubt it, and the promise should be made carefully and kept absolutely.

**The work with the parents and the confidentiality of the young person's own experience.** The parent is coached in the same skills, and the young person should know what the parent has been taught and what the parent has been told, and the young person should have some private time with the practitioner.

**The prohibition on the use of the stabilisation phase to defer a safeguarding decision.** A young person who is still at risk requires a safeguarding response, and the stabilisation work is not a substitute for it.

## 10. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Stabilisation** — The phase of work in which the young person's capacity to be present, to think, and to feel is restored, and in which the specific capacities required for the processing work are established.
- **The four purposes of stabilisation** — To widen the window of tolerance, to build the capacity to remain present, to establish the working relationship, and to restore ordinary functioning.
- **Orienting to the here and now** — The five-four-three-two-one sensory grounding exercise, and the variants that orient to the internal sensations and to the temporal.
- **Grounding through the senses** — The script-based use of the sensory environment to anchor the young person in the present, and its contraindication where sensory detail is activating.
- **Paced breathing** — The lengthening of the exhalation to increase the vagal tone and reduce the arousal, and the technique to use in the sympathetic state.
- **The contraindication of the breathing in the dorsal vagal state** — The deepening of the exhalation deepens the shutdown, and the orienting and movement are required instead.
- **The safe place** — A concrete, sensory, personally meaningful location, taught in detail and used as the resourcing intervention.
- **The container** — The imagined receptacle into which the young person places the overwhelming material, usually taught in the drawing.
- **The body scan** — The movement of attention through the body with the reporting of what is noticed, used both in the stabilisation work and in the completion of the autonomic component of a memory.
- **The contraindication of the body scan** — Its use with a young person with a history of bodily abuse, and the movement-based alternative.
- **Movement-based grounding** — The large, undemanding physical discharge and the orienting through movement, the most broadly acceptable form of the grounding work.
- **The orienting through movement** — The up-and-down eye movement exercise with a moving object, derived from the EMDR literature.
- **Time travel to a safe memory** — The recollection of a real and specifically pleasant memory as an orienting and regulating practice, and the requirement that the memory be real and checked.
- **Asking for help and saying no** — The two interpersonal skills taught in the stabilisation phase, and the requirement that the requests be met and the declines honoured.
- **The traffic light** — The three-colour self-monitoring scheme in which the young person locates their own arousal and knows what they need at each level.
- **The movement of the regulation to the young person** — The objective of the stabilisation phase, and the criterion by which its completion is judged.
- **The phase-oriented framework** — The management structure in which the work is determined by the young person's state rather than by the type of trauma, with the phases and the transition criteria.
- **The transition criteria** — The explicit conditions for moving between the phases, and the requirement that they be recorded.
- **The transition backwards** — The return to phase one on the deterioration of the young person's functioning, and the formulation that the deterioration requires.
- **The phase-specific pathways** — The separate procedures, documentation, and review criteria that characterise each phase in a service framework.
- **The false dichotomy** — The two failure modes of a service that does only stabilisation, and a service that processes regardless of readiness.
- **The four presentations for stabilisation** — The dissociating young person, the hyperaroused young person, the young person with a persistent somatic presentation, and the young person in an unstable placement.
- **The co-regulation principle** — The finding that the caregiver's regulation capacity mediates the young person's, and the reason the parent is taught the same skills.
- **The generalisation plan for the skills** — The deliberate practice of the skills in the contexts in which the young person will need them, and the reason the skills are portable.
:::

## 11. Chapter Summary

Stabilisation is the necessary and unglamorous work that precedes everything else, and its central proposition is that a young person's capacity to be in a relationship, to think, and to feel is regulated by the body, and that the body can be taught to be more regulated. The chapter set out the four purposes of stabilisation — widening the window of tolerance, building the capacity to remain present, establishing the working relationship, and restoring ordinary functioning — and distinguished stabilisation from the processing work, from supportive counselling, from symptom suppression, and from a prelude that can be skipped.

The evidence was presented accurately: good support for the general principle, a growing and positive evidence base for the individual skills, the strongest support for the parent training element, thin support for the phase model as a package, and a clinical consensus that considerably exceeds the empirical support.

The core skills were described in detail with their mechanisms, their teaching procedures, and their contraindications: the orienting skills to the here and now, to the internal sensations, and to the temporal; the sensory grounding, with its contraindication where sensory detail is activating; the paced breathing, with its physiological rationale and with the crucial caution that it must not be used in the dorsal vagal state; the safe place and the container; the body scan, with its two uses and its contraindication in the case of bodily abuse; the movement-based grounding, including the orienting through movement; the time travel to a safe memory, with the requirement that the memory be real and checked; the two interpersonal skills; and the traffic light, with the observation that its value is that it moves the responsibility for the regulation from the practitioner to the young person, which is the criterion of the phase's completion.

The phase-oriented approach was described with its rationale, its three phases in practice, the phase transitions as clinical decisions, and the management framework with its phase-specific pathways, its phase-specific staff, its measurement, and the avoidance of the false dichotomy.

The developmental modifications were described: the skills are taught to the young person and not to the practitioner, the language is the young person's own, the parent is taught the same skills, the generalisation is planned, and the developmental order matters. The four presentations were described and the four limits stated, with the risk that the skills become a way of avoiding the work identified as the most serious.

The case of Nadia demonstrated the chapter's central point. The referral was for a protocol and the protocol was appropriate for a young person who was ready, and Nadia was not ready, and the failure of the previous therapies was a failure of the phase. The work of the first four months was not preparation but the treatment of a young woman whose nervous system was organised for survival, and the decisive intervention was with the mother, and the specific decision about the door was the act of clinical reasoning that made the rest possible.

The evidence was summarised with its limits, and the strengths and limitations were set out, with the custodial failure mode and the infantilisation risk named.

The cultural analysis addressed the body as a culturally constructed object, the cultural forms of the safe place, the limits of the universality of the physiological claims, and the relative acceptability of the movement-based grounding. The ethical section addressed the relationship between the skills and the care, the risk of the custody model, the consent to the skills, the handling of a disclosure during a grounding exercise, the meaning of the promise of safety, the transparency of the parent work, and the prohibition on using the stabilisation phase to defer a safeguarding decision.

## 13. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 40.1 — The Personal Grounding Inventory (90 minutes, field-based)

In a placement, work with one young person to build their own grounding inventory, and record the following: their safe place, with sensory detail; their five preferred sensory anchors; their preferred movement; their best genuine safe memory; their early warning signs; their traffic light descriptions; and the two people they can ask for help. The inventory becomes a document they own, and the exercise teaches that the inventory is built from the young person's own material and not from the practitioner's.

### Activity 40.2 — The Skill Rehearsal (75 minutes, triadic)

One student plays a fifteen-year-old in a state of high arousal; one plays the practitioner; one observes. The practitioner must bring the arousal down without talking about its content, using only the body's language. The observer's task is to record every intervention that the practitioner makes and to mark the point at which the young person's state changes. The exercise teaches the most important thing in the chapter, which is that the intervention in the sympathetic state is not linguistic.

### Activity 40.3 — The Phase Criteria Audit (60 minutes, individual)

Take a case and write out the criteria for moving from phase one to phase two, marking each as met, partially met, or not met, with the evidence. Then write the three specific interventions that would move the two that are not met from "not met" to "partially met". The exercise is the discipline of the phase model, and it also reveals how easily a practitioner drifts into phase two because the pressure to do something is stronger than the discipline.

### Activity 40.4 — The Custody Audit (60 minutes, small group)

Take a real service's safety plan, traffic light, or behaviour chart and audit it from the young person's point of view. For each element, ask: does this give the young person a skill, or does it give the adults a record? Then rewrite the elements that fail the test, and identify what would have to change in the service for the rewrite to be possible. The second half of the exercise is the organisational work of Chapter 58 and it is the part that matters most.
:::

## 14. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the body.** Read van der Kolk (2014), Ogden's sensorimotor psychotherapy, and the trauma and the body literature. These are the theoretical basis of the body-based work and they are more convincing than the technique manuals.

**For the skills.** Read the stabilisation sections of the Child and Family Traumatic Stress Intervention manual and the Linehan skills repertoire, and read Cutting's work on the neuroscience of grounding, which is the most useful single paper on the specific techniques.

**For the phase model.** Read the phase model chapters in the standard clinical texts and the guidelines for the treatment of PTSD, and read the child and adolescent protocols of the specific approaches.

**For the parent work.** Read the parenting and co-regulation literature and the emotion coaching material from Chapter 37. The parent component is the evidence-supported core of the stabilisation work.

**For the critique.** Read the critical literature on the custodial drift of trauma services and on the over-use of safety plans. The field has been subjected to serious criticism on these grounds and the criticism has produced some of the better practice standards.
