
## 7. Evidence and Research Support

### 7.1 The status of the evidence

Behavioural activation has a larger evidence base than most psychotherapies, and the findings are unusually consistent. The principal results are as follows.

**Behavioural activation is effective for depression in adults.** The Cochrane and meta-analytic reviews report effect sizes in the large range, and the treatment is recommended in the major international guidelines.

**Behavioural activation is effective for depression in adolescents.** The adolescent literature is smaller but is consistent with the adult findings, and the reviews report moderate-to-large effects on both symptoms and functioning.

**Behavioural activation produces more rapid improvement in mood than cognitive therapy in the acute phase**, and this is the finding that established the treatment's viability as a standalone treatment and that motivated the combination with cognitive therapy.

**Behavioural activation is at least as effective as cognitive therapy on measures of depression severity**, and the two are not clearly different on the outcome measures, though the treatment acceptability data favour behavioural activation, particularly with young people and with people whose symptoms include a predominance of fatigue and anhedonia.

**The combination of behavioural activation and cognitive therapy is probably the most effective treatment for depression in adolescents**, and this is what most guidelines now recommend, with the behavioural component delivered first.

**Behavioural activation has good support in populations that are difficult to engage**, including young people who are reluctant to talk, and the treatment can be delivered by trained non-professionals with supervision, which is relevant to school-based and primary care services.

### 7.2 The component evidence

The treatment is composed of identifiable components, and the component studies are more useful for practice than the outcome studies.

**Activity monitoring** is the component with the weakest independent evidence, and this is a point of some controversy in the field. The counter-argument is that monitoring is not a treatment in itself but a mechanism that makes the discrepancy visible to the young person, and that its value is in what follows rather than in itself. The practical guidance from the evidence is to keep the monitoring brief and to do it collaboratively.

**Activity scheduling** has good support and is the component with the clearest mechanism.

**Graded task assignment** has good support, particularly with young people and in the treatment of depression with apathy, and the evidence for its use in activity scheduling for individuals with severe depression and low energy is reasonably strong.

**The discrepancy between the predicted and the actual quality of an activity** is a well-established finding, and the value of drawing it explicitly to the young person's attention is the therapeutic core of the treatment.

**The primacy of the behavioural component in the acute phase** is supported, and the treatment sequence is to activate first and to cognitively address the remaining difficulties once the behavioural cycle has been interrupted.

### 7.3 Limitations of the evidence

**The adolescent literature is smaller than the adult literature**, and the trials are heterogeneous in the age range, the severity, and the inclusion criteria.

**Long-term maintenance is less well studied than symptom reduction**, and a substantial proportion of young people relapse after the end of treatment, which is consistent with the developmental reality that the conditions that produced the depression may still be present.

**The trials have generally not included the most severely impaired young people**, particularly those with comorbid substance use, with trauma histories, or with neurodevelopmental conditions, and these are the young people most likely to be seen in a school or specialist service.

**The measures are largely self-report**, and the adolescent literature's reliance on self-report of mood is a limitation, particularly in a population whose reporting is affected by the very symptoms being measured.

**The allegiance effects are substantial** in this literature, as they are in the behavioural literature generally.

## 8. Strengths and Limitations of Behavioural Activation

### 8.1 Strengths

**It works quickly.** The behavioural component often produces a measurable improvement in mood within two weeks, and a rapid improvement is itself a therapeutic event, because it gives the young person evidence that the situation can change.

**It is concrete and accessible.** A young person who has been told that their thoughts are distorted usually responds poorly; a young person who is told that they will walk to the park and then come back and tell someone what they noticed is likely to go along with it.

**It is compatible with the developmental task.** The treatment works on the same processes that the adolescent's development requires, and this is a rare alignment.

**It is deliverable by non-specialists.** The treatment can be delivered by a school counsellor, a primary care nurse, or a trained support worker with supervision, and the evidence for the supervised delivery is good.

**It produces data.** The monitoring generates a record that the young person, the family, and the school can all see, and the record is itself a resource in the work with the school.

### 8.2 Limitations

**It is incomplete.** Behavioural activation addresses the behavioural withdrawal and its consequences. It does not address the reason the young person withdrew in the first place, except insofar as the withdrawal is maintained by a contingency, and the social anxiety in Nadia's case required work that the graded task alone would not have provided.

**It can be experienced as a productivity demand.** A young person who is told to "get up, get dressed, go to the park" may experience the schedule as another set of expectations, and the young person who is in the grip of a depressive episode may not be able to comply and will conclude that they have failed again. The clinical response is to make the first steps trivially small and to make the review of the log collaborative rather than evaluative.

**It does not reach the cognitions quickly enough in some cases.** A young person with a strong global negative self-belief may need the cognitive work sooner than the standard sequence allows, and the combination of behavioural activation and cognitive therapy addresses this.

**The maintenance is the problem.** A young person who leaves treatment with an improved mood and an unchanged set of conditions will relapse, and the relapse prevention work of the cognitive tradition should be built into the plan from the beginning.

**It can be misapplied as a compliance programme.** A graded task schedule in a school that is presented to the young person as a set of targets to be met, with consequences attached, is not behavioural activation; it is a compliance programme, and the evidence does not support it.

## 9. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 Culture, activity, and what counts as rewarding

The entire treatment depends on the assumption that the young person will find certain activities rewarding, and that assumption is culturally patterned in ways that require attention.

**What is rewarding is culturally specified.** Activities that are rewarding in one context are not in another: sport, music, religious or spiritual practice, family ceremony, food and cooking, language use, craft, and community activity all vary in their valuing. A clinician who proposes a graded task of "go to the library and read" to a young person for whom reading is associated with academic failure has proposed a task that is unlikely to be rewarding, and the failure will be read as motivation.

**Family obligation may be the available reward.** In some contexts the most reliably reinforcing activity available to a young person is the participation in family obligations and in the family's projects, and this may be the only socially legitimate way to build a schedule. A schedule built around helping at the family's shop, or caring for a grandparent, or the family's shared meals, may be both more effective and more acceptable than one built around the activities a clinician would nominate.

**Gender expectations shape the available activities**, and a schedule that puts a young woman into activities that expose her to unwanted attention in the community may be actively harmful, and the clinician should ask.

**The stigma of mental illness affects the schedule.** In some settings, a schedule that is visible to the family or the school and that names the young person's difficulties is harmful, and the schedule should be designed to be usable without being explanatory.

### 9.2 The relationship to the body and to appearance

Behavioural activation with adolescents inevitably touches the body, because the activities that relieve depression include physical activity, and because the young person with depression has often stopped eating, exercising, and attending to their appearance. Three cautions follow.

**The activation of physical activity can be experienced as a demand on the body**, particularly for a young person who has experienced body scrutiny, weight commentary, or disordered eating. The activities should be chosen with that in mind.

**Weight gain and appetite disturbance are features of the disorder**, and a young person who is told to eat more, or to exercise, may experience the instruction as an accusation. The clinician should name the symptom as a symptom.

**Anorexia nervosa is not treated by behavioural activation.** The graded task schedule is contraindicated in a young person with a diagnosed eating disorder, and the medical and specialist pathway in Chapter 46 takes precedence. This is an important exception and the practitioner should know it.

### 9.3 Ethical considerations

**Voluntariness.** The activities in a behavioural activation schedule are proposed, not imposed, and the young person chooses from a list. A schedule of activities chosen by the clinician is a different intervention with weaker support and a different ethical character.

**The monitoring and the surveillance boundary.** The daily log is a monitoring device, and the young person should know exactly what it is for, who will see it, and what will be done with it. A log that is shared with the school or the parents without the young person's knowledge is a breach of trust that will damage the alliance and is likely to end the treatment.

**Avoiding the allocation of tasks that should not be allocated.** A graded task hierarchy must not include tasks that the young person is not developmentally able to perform, must not include tasks that function as unpaid labour, and must not include the performance of household duties that other family members should carry.

**The graded task as a performance of recovery.** When the young person's graded task is shared with the family, it can become a public display of progress, which is a burden and can become a source of shame if the progress is slow. The clinician should keep the hierarchy private unless the young person wants it shared.

**The competence dimension and the risk of discouragement.** The graded task generates evidence of competence, and it also generates repeated evidence of failure if the steps are mis-set. The first step must be achievable, and the review must be framed so that a step that is not completed is information about the step rather than about the young person.

**Coordination with the school must be consented to.** A practitioner who shares a young person's monitoring data with the school without the young person's knowledge has used the data for a purpose other than the one for which they were collected.

## 10. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Behavioural activation** — A treatment for depression in which the reduction of reinforcing activity and the increase of aversive activity are treated as the maintaining cycle, and the intervention consists of scheduling and performing rewarding and mastery activities.
- **Activity record** — Jacobson's daily log of activities with ratings of pleasure and of mastery, used to establish the baseline and to make the discrepancy between predicted and actual experience visible.
- **Activity monitoring** — The modern, abbreviated form of the activity record, used as a baseline, as a measure of the relationship between activity and mood, and as the source of the schedule.
- **Activity scheduling** — The specification of what the young person will do, when, and with whom, on a written schedule in three columns.
- **Graded task assignment (GTA)** — A hierarchical list of tasks of increasing difficulty, worked through one or two steps beyond the young person's current position, with each completed step recorded and acknowledged.
- **Task hierarchy** — The ordered list of graded tasks in a graded task assignment, running from currently impossible to currently easy.
- **Advance criterion** — The specified condition for moving to the next step of a graded task hierarchy, usually a number of consecutive completions.
- **Mastery activity** — An activity selected for the sense of competence it produces rather than for its immediate pleasure.
- **Pleasure activity** — An activity selected for the immediate reward it produces.
- **The discrepancy** — The difference between the expected quality of an activity and its observed quality; the therapeutic target of the treatment.
- **Behavioural model of depression** — The account in which depression is maintained by the reduction of reinforcing activity, the increase of aversive activity, the avoidance of non-reward situations, and the disruption of physiological rhythms.
- **Immediacy of reward** — The property of an activity's consequence that determines its reinforcing strength, and the basis for restructuring delayed-reward activities.
- **Certainty of reward** — The predictability of an activity's outcome, which determines its reinforcing strength and the rationale for graded mastery tasks.
- **Social contact in the schedule** — The deliberate inclusion of scheduled contact with other people, which is the component most specific to adolescent depression.
- **First-day design for school return** — The practice of beginning a return to school with a single brief, structured, and rewarding visit, before any substantial assessment or homework.
- **Anhedonia** — The loss of the capacity to experience pleasure from activities that would ordinarily be rewarding.
- **Behavioural activation with cognitive therapy** — The combination in which the behavioural component is delivered first, to interrupt the cycle, and the cognitive component follows.
- **Control therapy** — The early behavioural approach to depression based on the restoration of control through graded mastery tasks.
- **Learned helplessness** — The account of the acquisition of a passivity response through exposure to uncontrollable aversive events, with limited application to adolescent depression.
- **Pacing** — The deliberate matching of the task difficulty to the young person's current capacity, with the expectation of variable performance.
- **Behavioural activation for apathy** — The application of activity scheduling and graded tasks to the predominance of fatigue and loss of interest in the depressive presentation.
- **Window of tolerance** — The range of arousal in which a person can function and process information, and outside which behaviour is dysregulated; the constraint within which an activity schedule must be built.
- **Contingency management of the depressive cycle** — The application of the operant analysis to the relationship between the young person's activity, their mood, and the contingencies that sustain the withdrawal.
- **Activation of the social self** — The reintroduction of contact with other people, graded, as the mechanism by which the depressive cycle is interrupted in the adolescent presentation.
:::

## 11. Chapter Summary

Behavioural activation is the treatment that the behavioural tradition contributed to the understanding and treatment of depression, and it rests on a single observation that is older than the cognitive model and, in some ways, more useful: the defining features of depression are not beliefs but withdrawal, and the withdrawal is self-maintaining.

The behavioural model describes depression as a cycle with four processes: the reduction of reinforcing activity, the increase of aversive and numbing activity, the avoidance of situations associated with non-reward, and the disruption of the physiological rhythms. The intervention interrupts the cycle by identifying activities that the person finds rewarding, scheduling them, and performing them, and by adding graded mastery tasks where the young person has stopped doing the difficult things.

The three components were described in detail. Activity monitoring establishes the baseline, makes the discrepancy visible, identifies the pattern, and supplies the data for the schedule, and the chapter noted the three practical responses to the predictable non-compliance with it. Activity scheduling requires the specification of what, when, and with whom, and it must include social contact, because the social component is the one that distinguishes the adolescent depression from the adult depression and the one that has been lost. Graded task assignment is the component most associated with the treatment, and its rules — achievable but not trivial, chosen by the young person, reviewed weekly, subdivided until the first step is clearly achievable — were set out in full.

The case of Nadia showed the treatment in operation and showed the three elements of the formulation that generalise: the maternal contingency, in which the daily attention of a loving parent was sustaining the withdrawal; the ordering of the work, with the graded task preceding the social contact and the social contact preceding the school return, because a premature return to school would have confirmed the young person's expectation; and the distinction between the depression, which was the presenting problem, and the social anxiety, which preceded it and maintained it.

The evidence is large and consistent for adults and substantial and growing for adolescents, and the findings that behavioural activation produces more rapid mood improvement than cognitive therapy in the acute phase, and that the combination is probably the best available treatment for adolescent depression, are the ones that changed practice. The component evidence favours activity scheduling and graded task assignment, and the chapter noted the controversy over the monitoring component honestly.

The limitations are that the treatment is incomplete, that it can be experienced as a productivity demand, and that maintenance is the central problem, since a young person who leaves treatment with an improved mood and unchanged conditions will relapse. It can also be misapplied as a compliance programme, which is not the same intervention and is not supported by the evidence.

The cultural analysis addressed the specification of what is rewarding, the role of family obligation as the most reliably available reward in some settings, gender expectations, and the stigma of mental illness in the design of a schedule. The ethical analysis addressed the voluntariness of the activity selection, the surveillance boundary around the monitoring, the body and appearance issues that are unavoidable with adolescents, the specific contraindication of graded task scheduling in a young person with a diagnosed eating disorder, and the ethical requirements of keeping the hierarchy private unless the young person wishes otherwise.

## 13. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 27.1 — Your Own Activity Record (three days, individual)

Complete a two-column activity record for three days, with a pleasure rating and a mastery rating against each activity. Do not complete it retrospectively. On the fourth day, plot the mood rating against the activity and identify the relationship. Then write two sentences describing a discrepancy you found — something you expected to be unpleasant that was not, or something you expected to be pleasant that was not. The exercise is the treatment in miniature, and every student finds at least one discrepancy they had not noticed.

### Activity 27.2 — Building a Graded Task Hierarchy (75 minutes, individual)

Choose a young person from placement who is withdrawn. Construct a graded task hierarchy of at least twelve steps, running from currently impossible to currently easy, with the steps specified in the units in which the young person would actually do them. Then check the first step against the criterion: is it something the young person is confident they could do today? If not, subdivide and repeat. Most hierarchies fail this test at the third or fourth step, and the exercise is designed to make that visible.

### Activity 27.3 — The Social Contact Ladder (60 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, design the social reintroduction component of a behavioural activation plan for a withdrawn sixteen-year-old. Specify a graded ladder of contacts, the people involved, the duration of each, the preparation for each, and what is done if the young person refuses. Then discuss what the group would do if the young person said she did not want to see any of the people the group had chosen. The last part is the most instructive, because the answer is almost always that the young person chooses a different person, and the reason she could not choose one initially is the point.

### Activity 27.4 — Auditing a Schedule for Coercion (45 minutes, individual)

Take any activity schedule you have seen or designed, and audit it against five criteria: was the activity chosen by the young person; is the monitoring private; is the graded task free of tasks that should be allocated to others; is the schedule free of tasks that are unsafe for the young person; and is the first step achievable today? Count the failures. The exercise is a short version of the ethical analysis, and the failures are usually found in the monitoring rather than in the schedule.
:::

## 14. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the treatment.** Read Jacobson (1971) for the original account and Jacobson and colleagues (2001) for the controlled comparison with cognitive therapy, and read Rehm (2011) for the modern synthesis. The Jacobson material is old and the concepts are current, which is a rare and useful combination.

**For the evidence.** Read the adolescent review literature on behavioural activation and read the meta-analyses of the adult literature, and then read a study with a component analysis, which is more useful for practice than the outcome trials.

**For the developmental adaptation.** Read the adolescent development literature on the role of competence and connection, and read the treatment adaptation papers for young people. The developmental rationale is what makes the modifications in Section 4 principled rather than ad hoc.

**For the combination with cognitive therapy.** Read the cognitive model chapter in this book alongside the activation material, and read the studies of the combination, which is the treatment most likely to be used in practice.

**For the cautions.** Read the eating disorder and adolescent depression literature, particularly on the contraindication of behavioural activation in anorexia nervosa, and read the literature on activity monitoring as a burden.
