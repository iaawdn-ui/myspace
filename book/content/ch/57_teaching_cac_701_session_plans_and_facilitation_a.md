# Teaching CAC. 701: Session Plans and Facilitation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the lecturer should be able to:

- Translate the CAC. 701 syllabus into a coherent sixteen-week teaching sequence.
- Construct session plans with outcomes, preparation, teaching activities, assessment, and resources.
- Facilitate discussion of counselling theory without reducing it to memorisation.
- Use case vignettes, role play, observation, writing, and digital resources safely.
- Support students with varied academic, linguistic, disability, and professional backgrounds.
- Assess participation and contribution without rewarding disclosure of personal trauma.
- Evaluate and revise teaching using student feedback and assessment evidence.

## Chapter Overview

Teaching counselling theories is not the delivery of a museum of famous names. Students must learn to think with theories: to notice what a theory sees, what it misses, how it explains change, what interventions follow, what evidence supports it, whom it fits, and where it may harm. A CAC. 701 session should therefore alternate explanation, demonstration, comparison, practice, critique, and reflection.

This chapter offers a teaching architecture rather than a script. Lecturers should adapt examples, pacing, readings, and assessments to institutional requirements and student needs while preserving constructive alignment: outcomes, activities, and assessment must point in the same direction.

## 1. Course Design Principles

**Start with clinical problems.** Students remember theories better when each is introduced through a human situation. Begin with a brief vignette, ask what needs explaining, then present the theory as one disciplined way of seeing.

**Compare early and often.** Comparison prevents brand loyalty. Ask how behavioural, cognitive, humanistic, family, trauma-informed, and cultural accounts differ in cause, change, role of counsellor, and evidence.

**Teach evidence with concepts.** A theory should not be presented first as timeless truth and later as research topic. Include evidence, limitations, and populations studied as part of the theory's identity.

**Practise safely.** Skills laboratories require consent, alternatives to personal disclosure, clear roles, stop rules, and debriefing. Students should practise on fictional or consented material, not be required to reveal trauma to pass.

**Make youth central.** Every adult-origin theory must be interrogated for developmental fit: cognition, language, autonomy, family dependence, school context, consent, and power.

**Model cultural humility.** Teaching examples should vary across family forms, languages, disability, class, migration, gender, sexuality, and community contexts without turning any group into a spectacle.

## 2. Sixteen-Week Sequence

A possible sequence is:

1. Why theory matters; course map; formulation as disciplined seeing.
2. Scientific, philosophical, and professional foundations.
3. Worldviews, common factors, alliance, and evidence appraisal.
4. Developmental and cultural foundations for youth counselling.
5. Person-centred, existential, Gestalt, and focusing approaches.
6. Transactional analysis, internal family systems, and parts language.
7. Cognitive model, REBT, schema concepts, and youth CBT.
8. ACT, DBT skills, mindfulness, and third-wave debates.
9. Behavioural principles, functional assessment, exposure, and contingencies.
10. Behavioural activation, school and clinic systems.
11. Family systems: Bowen, structural, and strategic approaches.
12. Attachment, solution-focused, and narrative practice.
13. Emotion-focused, trauma-informed, EMDR, stabilisation, and somatic practice.
14. IPT-A, mentalization, motivational interviewing, and social cognitive theory.
15. Adolescent development, psychodynamic, multicultural, feminist, intersectional, LGBTQ+ affirmative, resilience, and ecological practice.
16. Integration, case conceptualisation, assessment, ethics of endings, teletherapy, school consultation, and capstone formulation.

This sequence compresses substantial material in later weeks. Institutions with more time may divide weeks 14–16 into separate units. The logic is cumulative: foundations, relationship, major mechanisms, systems, development and culture, then integration and professional use.

## 3. Anatomy of a Three-Hour Session

A three-hour postgraduate session might use the following rhythm:

**Opening and retrieval (15 minutes).** A low-stakes question connecting to the previous week.

**Clinical problem (15 minutes).** A short case vignette read aloud or shown in text; students identify what needs explanation.

**Concept teaching (35 minutes).** Core theory, key terms, mechanisms, and a diagram.

**Demonstration (25 minutes).** Lecturer demonstration, recorded session excerpt with permission, transcript analysis, or live structured role play.

**Break (10 minutes).**

**Application laboratory (40 minutes).** Triads practise with fictional material; observer uses a structured feedback form.

**Evidence and critique (20 minutes).** Trial or review summary, limitations, cultural questions, populations missing.

**Formulation synthesis (15 minutes).** Students write what the theory changes in a treatment plan.

**Exit assessment (5 minutes).** One concept learned, one question remaining, one application to youth practice.

Not every week needs every element. Predictable rhythm reduces anxiety, while variation in tasks maintains attention.

## 4. Facilitation Moves

Useful lecturer moves include:

- asking for observations before interpretations;
- inviting competing theoretical readings of the same event;
- writing student language verbatim before translating into theory;
- pausing after questions long enough for thought;
- asking who benefits and who may be harmed by an intervention;
- distinguishing evidence, clinical tradition, and personal preference;
- summarising disagreement accurately;
- returning to the young person's voice.

Lecturers should avoid rewarding the most confident speaker as the most competent thinker. Written responses, pairs, anonymous digital polls, and small groups distribute participation.
