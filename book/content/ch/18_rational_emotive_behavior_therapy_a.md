# Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the development of rational-emotive behaviour therapy in Albert Ellis's work and the changes it has undergone, including the shift from rational-emotive psychotherapy to rational-emotive behaviour therapy.
- State the ABC model and apply it to a case.
- Explain the concepts of irrational beliefs, disputation, the alternative, and the effective philosophy, and use the language accurately.
- Distinguish the healthy and unhealthy emotions, and explain the clinical and ethical significance of the distinction.
- Describe the three forms of disputation and the five steps of the therapeutic process.
- Apply rational-emotive behaviour therapy to work with children and adolescents, and specify the developmental modifications.
- Evaluate the evidence for the therapy and for its distinctive components.
- Critically evaluate the theory, including its philosophical commitments, its account of emotion, and its cultural assumptions.

## Chapter Overview

Albert Ellis began rational-emotive therapy in the 1950s, after a decade of practice as a psychotherapist and after reading the Stoic philosophers, whose proposition that human distress is caused not by events but by judgments about events struck him as both obviously true and practically useful. He developed the ABC model, which is one of the most widely used explanatory frameworks in the profession, and he remained for forty years its most energetic advocate and its most persistent critic of everyone who took the cognitive model in a more moderate direction.

The therapy's distinctive features are the **dispute** — a structured, argumentative examination of the young person's irrational belief, conducted in a way that is explicitly non-blaming because the belief is understood as a self-defeating strategy that the young person has learned and can therefore unlearn — and the **effective philosophy**, the set of statements that the young person is helped to construct and to live by.

The theory is of particular interest to a student in a course on children and adolescents because of a tension at its centre that Ellis himself foregrounded. The therapy asserts that human beings are responsible for their emotional disturbances, and this is its great pedagogical strength, because it gives a young person genuine agency in the face of circumstances that are frequently outside their control. It is also, in the eyes of many critics, its great liability, because the assertion can be heard as an accusation, and because the therapy's account of what is outside a young person's control is narrower than it should be. This chapter presents the theory in its full strength, describes the modifications required for developmental work, and examines the criticism with the seriousness it deserves.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 Ellis's development

Albert Ellis's path to rational-emotive therapy was distinctive. He was, in succession, a business executive, a teacher, and a psychotherapist, and he had no formal clinical training until later in his career. He was, in his own frequently polemical account, a failed marriage and a mediocre therapist, and he turned to the Stoics, to Descartes, and to the sceptical philosophers in order to make sense of his own difficulty.

In 1955, at a meeting of the American Association for the Advancement of Psychotherapy, Ellis gave a paper that is generally taken as the founding of the therapy, and the paper — on the psychological techniques of getting a new idea and throwing away an old one — was poorly received. The title of the paper is itself a statement of method, and the reception was a fair indication of how radical the proposal was. Rational-emotive therapy was in effect an argument that a human being's characteristic psychological disturbance is not caused by external events, by a past, or by an unconscious, but by the person's own self-defeating beliefs, and that the therapeutic task is to argue the young person out of those beliefs.

### 1.2 The changes

The therapy has changed substantially in sixty years, and a student should know the trajectory, because the popular version is the 1950s version and the practice has moved a long way from it.

**From rational-emotive psychotherapy to rational-emotive behaviour therapy.** The renaming in the 1980s reflected the recognition that the therapy had always included behavioural and experimental methods and that the "rational" label had drawn attention away from them. The modern therapy emphasises the behavioural experiments, the homework, and the emotional and behavioural change, and it is more active and more directive than the popular image of a Socratic argument.

**The move from the exclusive to the inclusive philosophy.** Ellis's original formulation proposed a "philosophy of rational emotions" that was a demanding personal philosophy to be adopted wholesale. The contemporary formulation is the **effective philosophy**, which is more modest: a set of preferences rather than a set of truths, a way of living that is preferable rather than correct, and a set of statements that the young person is helped to construct for their own circumstances. The shift has been important, and it has reduced the theory's metaphysical exposure considerably.

**The development of the psychoeducational and popular forms.** The 1960s and 1970s saw the development of the popular rational-emotive pamphlets, the encounter with the marathon groups, and the formation of the Albert Ellis Institute. The popular form is still widely used in educational and workplace settings and a student should know it, because it is frequently the version with which a young person will have had contact.

**The influence on other theories.** The REBT account of the structure of the irrational belief, the ABC model, the Socratic disputation, the homework, and the concept of the self-disturbance have been absorbed into the cognitive therapies, and several of the most widely used cognitive techniques were developed independently by people working in the Ellis tradition.

## 2. The ABC Model

### 2.1 The structure

The ABC model is a simple sequence.

**A — the activating event.** Something happens. This is the crucial theoretical claim: the event is not the cause of the emotional disturbance, and the "if... then" propositions of the behavioural theorists, which made the event the cause and the behaviour the consequence, are precisely what the theory attacks.

**B — the belief.** The activating event is interpreted, and the interpretation generates the emotional and behavioural consequences. The critical point is that the belief is not the activating event but the person's *interpretation* of it, and that the interpretation is available to awareness and to examination.

**C — the emotional and behavioural consequence.** The emotional and behavioural consequences, which are unhealthy or irrational, follow from the belief rather than from the event.

**A, B, C — the dispute.** The therapeutic intervention, in which the practitioner and the young person examine the belief.

The model's power is its clarity, and the clarity is genuinely useful. A young person who can be helped to see that the "activating event" was a mark of 62 per cent and the belief was "I am a failure" has made a large step, and the step is one that can be made developmentally.

### 2.2 A worked example

Take a sixteen-year-old, Nadia, who has received a text from a friend saying that the friend cannot come to her birthday party. The activating event: the text. The irrational belief: "I am not important enough for people to want to come." The healthy emotion: disappointment, and a proportionate sadness. The unhealthy emotion: shame, and the belief that she is a failure. The behavioural consequence: she does not go to the party, does not tell anyone, and spends the evening in her room.

Ellis's own way of putting it was: "It is not the party that is the problem; it is your belief about the party." The therapeutic work is then to dispute the belief.

## 3. Irrational Beliefs

### 3.1 The definition

An **irrational belief** is, in the modern formulation, a belief that is **inflexible, absolutist, and self-defeating**. The three criteria are: it demands that something must be exactly a certain way; it is a demand, an should, a must, an utterly, an always, a never; and it produces emotional disturbance in the person who holds it, and typically damages the person's relationships and opportunities.

The formulation is important because it is behavioural and functional rather than moral. A belief is irrational not because its content is false but because holding it rigidly is bad for the person holding it. This is a considerably more defensible claim than the one that irrational beliefs are simply "wrong."

### 3.2 The ten or eleven unreasonable beliefs

Ellis's lists of unreasonable beliefs are the most widely known part of the theory, and they are the most frequently trivialised. The classic list is:

1. The misfortune of other people is my fault.
2. I am what my experiences have made me.
3. Something outside of my control must be the cause of my unhappiness.
4. I am what my experiences have made me.
5. My past is the cause of my present behaviour, so I cannot change.
6. It is easier to be sick than to take on the difficulties of change.
7. It is easier to avoid than to face.
8. Life has to be perfect, or it is awful.
9. I must be perfectly good or I will be condemned.
10. I must get what I want, or I will be miserable.
11. It is my fault if I am not happy.

Modern versions have modified the list considerably — the "musts" of the contemporary formulation are about the self ("I must be loved," "I must be competent"), and the emphasis has shifted from a catalogue to a structure. A student should learn the structure rather than the list.

### 3.3 The structure of the belief

The contemporary formulation identifies four elements in the structure of a self-defeating belief: the **triggering event** (the "A"), the **demand** ("I must," "I should," "I have to"), the **belief about the demand** ("If I must do this, then I am a failure" / "If I must not do this, then I am a bad person"), and the **self-disturbance** (the emotional and behavioural consequence).

The **self-disturbance** is the term Ellis used for the unhealthy emotion, and the word is chosen to make the point that the person is disturbing himself. The contemporary practice is to use it as the name for the point at which the intervention is directed.

### 3.4 The dispute

The **dispute** is the core of the therapy, and it is the element most often misdescribed as confrontational. Ellis was explicit that the dispute is not an argument, and the principles that govern it are:

**The dispute is aimed at the belief, not the person.** The practitioner does not say the young person is wrong; the practitioner says the belief is self-defeating.

**The dispute is Socratic.** The practitioner asks questions that expose the belief's inflexibility and its consequences, and does not deliver the alternative.

**The dispute is empirically grounded.** The practitioner asks what evidence the young person has for the belief, what evidence there is against it, and what the practical consequences of holding it are. Ellis insisted that the dispute should not be a philosophical debate about the nature of reality but an examination of the practical consequences of the belief.

**The dispute is unconditional and unconditional in its acceptance of the person.** The young person is accepted whatever the belief.

**The dispute is aimed at the demand.** The most common and most effective target is the demand within the belief — the "must" and the "should" — and the goal is to replace the demand with a preference.

### 3.5 The alternative

The **alternative** is the statement the young person constructs in the place of the irrational belief, and the discipline here is important: the alternative is not the positive opposite of the irrational belief, and it is not the objectively correct statement. The alternative is a **better**, more flexible, less self-defeating statement that the young person can believe and act on.

This is the point at which the therapy is most often misunderstood as a demand for positivity, and the practitioner should be able to give a clear example. The irrational belief "If I am not liked, I am worthless" does not have the alternative "If I am not liked, I am wonderful." The alternative is something like: "Not being liked by everyone is disappointing, and I can cope with that, and I still want to be with these people even if some of them are not available." The alternative is functional, not flattering.

## 4. The Healthy and Unhealthy Emotions

### 4.1 The distinction

The theory distinguishes the **unhealthy emotions**, which are generated by the self-defeating beliefs and are self-defeating in turn — the intense, blaming, absolutist states such as shame, guilt, depression, anxiety, and rage — from the **healthy emotions**, which are generated by the flexible beliefs and which are appropriate responses to a difficult situation. A young person who is disappointed because a friend has not come to a party has a healthy emotion; a young person who is crushed, humiliated, and convinced they are worthless has an unhealthy one.

The distinction is not a claim that some feelings are good and others bad. It is a claim about the *function* of the emotion: the healthy emotions are the appropriate responses to a reality that is genuinely difficult, and they are useful; the unhealthy emotions are the responses to a belief that is not supported by the reality and that damages the person.

### 4.2 The clinical and ethical significance

The significance is large, and it is the aspect of the theory that a student should most carefully understand.

**The clinical significance** is that the unhealthy emotion is the target and the healthy emotion is the goal, which means that the therapy does not aim to remove the young person's distress by reassuring them that everything is fine. It aims to remove the *irrational* component of the distress, which is the belief, and to leave the appropriate response to the actual difficulty. A young person who is disappointed about the party should be disappointed; the work is with the shame and the worthlessness.

**The ethical significance** is more complicated, and the complication is where the theory's central vulnerability lies. The theory asserts that human beings are responsible for their emotional disturbances. Applied to a young person, this assertion can become a claim that the young person is responsible for their depression, which is both false and damaging. The REBT literature has been comparatively attentive to this risk, and the modern formulation is more careful than the popular version, but the risk is real and a practitioner must guard against it.

The guard is the same one that guards the cognitive model: the dispute is conducted against the belief and never against the person, and the examination of the belief is always traced back to its origins and to its function in the young person's life. A dispute that ends with the young person feeling foolish has gone wrong, however technically correct the practitioner has been.

## 5. The Therapeutic Process

### 5.1 The five steps

**Step 1: The relationship.** The practitioner establishes a relationship in which the young person can consider a challenge to their belief without feeling unsafe. Ellis insisted that this is the most important step and the one most neglected, and he was right: the dispute does not work with a young person who does not trust the person doing it.

**Step 2: The diagnosis of the irrational beliefs.** The practitioner and the young person identify the specific irrational beliefs, the demands within them, and the self-disturbances they generate. The aim is to find the beliefs that are actually operating, which is often not what the young person presents.

**Step 3: The learning of the alternative.** The practitioner and the young person work to develop a more flexible, less self-defeating belief, and the young person is helped to see what difference it makes in practice. The "emotional homework" — deliberately provoking the self-disturbance in a controlled situation so that the young person can observe it — is the characteristic technique at this step.

**Step 4: The solution.** The young person is helped to identify the concrete problems in their life and to plan specific action. The theory is emphatically practical, and Ellis objected strongly to therapies in which the young person felt better but continued to behave in the same self-defeating ways.

**Step 5: the prevention of relapse.** The young person is helped to establish a pattern of self-disputing, so that they can catch the irrational beliefs in operation in future.

### 5.2 The emotional homework

The **emotional homework** is the technique most associated with the therapy and it is distinctive. The young person is asked to identify the situation in which the self-disturbance occurs, to deliberately provoke it — by imagining the worst, or by dwelling on the event, or by rehearsing the belief — and then to observe the emotional disturbance as it arises, and to observe it as it changes.

The mechanism is that the young person experiences the disturbance in a controlled setting, learns that it rises and falls, and discovers that it is not the catastrophe that was feared. It is a behavioural and physiological technique, and the cognitive and behavioural elements are both present. With a young person, the technique is modified to be shorter, more bounded, and to be concluded deliberately with a return to the effective philosophy, and it should never be used with a young person whose disturbance is a serious symptom of a mental health condition without a full assessment.

### 5.3 Other techniques

**Rational emotive imagery.** The young person imagines the worst that could happen and then the worst after that, and continues in a chain, and then — the key step — imagines how they could cope with it, and then how they could improve the situation. The technique is highly structured and is one of the more effective ways of working with catastrophising, and it is directly adaptable to a young person who is a catastrophic thinker, which is very common in anxiety.

**The decision-making and the preference.** The therapy works with the young person's decisions and their "shoulds" about others, and the contemporary work on decision-making focuses on the young person's tendency to assign blame and to build a self-image out of the blame.

**The philosophy of the self.** The contemporary formulation uses the "philosophy of the self" — a short personal statement of the young person's core beliefs about themselves, built in the session — and the work on shame, self-forgiveness, and unconditional positive regard for oneself.

## 6. Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The developmental fit

REBT has a specific and unusual claim to relevance with young people, and it is worth making the claim precisely: **the therapy gives a young person a way to be responsible for their own reactions without being responsible for their circumstances.** That is a developmental achievement of some importance, because the central problem of adolescence is precisely the collision between the young person's growing need for agency and the adult world's insistence on their powerlessness.

The therapy also provides a vocabulary that is usable developmentally, because the irrational belief and the demand are concrete and teachable, and because the emotional homework is a behavioural technique that does not require much verbal sophistication.

### 6.2 The developmental modifications

**The language.** The formulation must be translated into the young person's own terms. "The activating event" becomes "what happened"; "the demand" becomes "the rule you are living by" or "the thing you are telling yourself you have to do"; "the self-disturbance" becomes "the feeling that makes it worse." Ellis himself insisted on plain language, and the practitioner should follow the principle with more rigour than the theory's own literature does.

**The scope of the dispute.** With a young person, the dispute should be conducted with the "must" as the target and with the person protected. A useful developmental rule is: dispute the demand, never the person, and never the young person's right to feel what they feel. The dispute is about the inflexibility and the consequences of a rule, not about the appropriateness of the feeling.

**The social context.** The developmental modification with the greatest consequences is the requirement to examine whether the irrational belief is in fact irrational *in the young person's circumstances*. A young person who believes "I must be liked by everyone in order to be safe" may be living in an environment in which the threat of rejection is real and the stakes are high, and disputing that belief with the abstract proposition that "not everyone liking you is terrible" is a callous response to a real situation. The REBT framework itself provides the solution — the "healthy, real, logical, and philosophical" consideration of the actual situation — and a practitioner who omits it has misunderstood the theory.

**The emotional homework with young people.** The homework should be shorter, more bounded, and more frequent, and it should be done with a specific, concrete trigger. And it should never be used to provoke a young person's trauma response, and a practitioner must be alert to the possibility that the homework has triggered something that needs the protocols of Part VI.

**The parent.** The work with parents is often the most valuable component, because parents' own irrational beliefs about their young people are frequently at the origin of the young person's.

### 6.3 The specific risk

The specific risk with young people is the misapplication of the responsibility claim, and the practitioner should be able to describe how to avoid it.

**The avoidance.** The responsibility claim is most safely conveyed by a specific reframe: "Some of what is happening to you is not your fault, and some of how you are handling it is worth working on. Both of those are true, and neither cancels the other."

**The relational basis.** The dispute is most likely to be experienced as an accusation when the young person has a history of being blamed, and the practitioner should establish the relational conditions before the dispute. Ellis's own emphasis on the relationship as the first step was, in retrospect, ahead of its time.

**The pathology risk.** A young person with a diagnosis — of depression, of an eating disorder, of a psychotic condition, of ADHD — may have difficulties that the responsibility claim does not touch, and the dispute should be conducted with the diagnosis in view, not around it.

## 7. Modern Developments

**The emphasis on the self-disturbance and the emotion.** The contemporary therapy has moved closer to the emotion-focused and acceptance-based approaches, with a specific practical point: the distinction between the healthy and the unhealthy emotion is retained, but the theory's earlier tendency to treat the healthy emotion as a goal has given way to a more accurate position in which the goal is the reduction of the self-disturbance and the development of the young person's capacity to experience and to tolerate the healthy emotion.

**The integration with the cognitive-behavioural tradition.** The REBT and CBT traditions have converged substantially, and the differences are now largely matters of emphasis. REBT is more active and more disputational, more philosophical in its commitments, and more focused on the structure of the belief than on its content.

**The applications in education and organisations.** The popular and educational forms of REBT are widely used in schools, in universities, in the workplace, and in sport, and the "rational emotive behaviour education" programmes are among the more empirically supported school-based prevention and promotion programmes.
