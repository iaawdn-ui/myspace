
## 7. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Hurt Himself in the Bathroom

**Presentation.** Marcus is fifteen, and he lives in a residential placement. He has a diagnosis of a neurodevelopmental condition and a history of placement breakdown. He has a behaviour of biting his own forearm, most often in the bathroom, most often in the late afternoon, and it has been increasing over the last six weeks. The injury on the day of the referral was severe enough to need a hospital assessment, and the placement manager is asking whether the placement can continue.

This is the situation in which a practitioner must be extremely careful about the sequence of events, because the pressure is to produce a plan immediately and the plan that gets produced under this pressure is usually a containment plan that will not work.

**Step 1: safety.** The safety plan is written on the first day, before anything else, and it is short and it is specific. It names the early indicators — and these come from the staff, who have been the ones watching: a particular stillness, the sudden interest in the bathroom, the way he stops mid-sentence, a hand going to his pocket. It specifies who goes to him, what they say, and what they do. It specifies the response to the behaviour itself, which is the removal of the identified object — in this case, it is a modification of his clothing, because the biting has been found to occur when his forearm is bare and the arm is exposed, and the clothing change is a modification that costs him nothing in dignity. And it specifies the provision of the alternative: a weighted item that he takes with him and that he has already identified as calming, which is permitted and encouraged, and which is the thing that has been removed from him in the last placement.

**Step 2: medical.** The medical review is the second step, and it is not a formality. It establishes a full medication review, and it establishes that Marcus's last medication review was eleven months ago, which is itself a finding. It establishes that he has been having significant headaches, which his presentation team had not known, and which are being treated. It establishes that his sleep has been disrupted by the placement's night-time routine, and that he is not eating properly, and that both have changed in the last six weeks, which is the period over which the behaviour has increased. A change in the behaviour over a defined period always indicates a change in the conditions, and the medical review is where the change is found.

**Step 3: the functional assessment.** The descriptive assessment comes next, and it is done with the staff rather than in a clinic, because the behaviour occurs in the placement.

The ABC record is revealing. The antecedents are consistent: late afternoon, the bathroom, alone, and — in the last six weeks — the arrival of a specific member of staff who works the late shift and who has come from a different placement with a very different approach, and who is loud, and who rearranges the bathroom. The consequences are also consistent: the bathroom door is closed, and the young person is alone, and the physical sensation of the bite is a strong stimulus, and the bite is followed by a reduction in the emotional state that the staff describe as "settled" and that Marcus describes, much later, as "quieter".

The pattern is that the behaviour is most likely in the late afternoon, that it has increased since a change of staff, and that it is followed by a marked reduction in the young person's distress. That pattern is consistent with automatic reinforcement — the behaviour produces a sensory and affective consequence — and it is also consistent with escape, because being alone in the bathroom is a relief after a shift with a loud staff member.

**Step 4: the experimental analysis.** The analysis is conducted in a structured way in the placement's quiet room. The conditions are: alone; play; attention; demand; and the corresponding controls. The results are clear and they are not the results the placement team expected.

The behaviour occurs at a high rate in the alone condition, at a low rate in the play condition, at a low rate in the attention condition, and at a low rate in the demand condition. The behaviour is **automatically maintained**, and the placement team's expectation — that the behaviour was a bid for attention, which was the reason their previous placements had escalated to restraint — was wrong.

**The establishing conditions.** The behaviour is not new; it existed, more mildly, in the previous placement, and it was described there as attention-seeking. The question of what it was originally for is not answerable from the records, and the honest answer is recorded.

**The treatment.** The plan has four elements.

*The medical and environmental work first.* The headaches are treated. The sleep and the eating are addressed, because a young person in pain and in a state of sleep deprivation is more likely to self-injure, and because these were the changes that preceded the increase. The loud staff member is consulted — with a discussion about the approach rather than about the young person, and with the specific request that the bathroom not be rearranged during the late shift, which is a small change with a large effect.

*The modification of the environment.* The weighted item is returned, and its use is not treated as a problem. The bathroom door is not closed on him; the door has a gap and he is visible. The clothing modification is made permanent for the late afternoon, and the young person chooses the clothing, and the choice is the point.

*The differential reinforcement of the alternative.* The alternative behaviour — going to the quiet room with the weighted item when the distress builds — is reinforced immediately, and the reinforcement is frequent and small, and the adult response to the alternative is warm and specific.

*The extinction of the behaviour, with a safety overlay.* The behaviour is placed on extinction: the consequence that has been maintaining it, which is the quiet and the relief, is no longer delivered immediately, and the bathroom is no longer available as a place of immediate relief, because the consequence of the behaviour is now the intervention of the staff, which is the safety response. This is the step that is most likely to produce an increase in the first week, and the staff are told so in advance, and the safety response is specified in the plan so that the increase does not become a crisis.

**The outcome.** The behaviour decreases over approximately six weeks, with a consistent pattern of a slight increase at the point of each staff change and a rapid decrease once the new staff member is established. Marcus's placement continues. The medication review is now monthly. The sleep and the eating have improved.

**The outcome that the plan was not designed to produce.** Marcus's own account, offered in a session in the eighth week, is that the biting was "the only way to make it stop" — and the "it" is the noise and the rushing and the being told what to do. The behavioural analysis found the automatic consequence and the treatment addressed it, and the young person's own account found the escape and named it precisely. Both were correct, and the plan that had been built only on the experimental analysis would have addressed the automatic function and left the escape function intact, and the behaviour would have persisted in a modified form.

The case is therefore an argument for the method described in Chapter 24: the young person's own account is data, and it should be gathered as early as possible, and it is not an alternative to the experimental analysis but a supplement to it.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The status of the evidence

The evidence for the treatment of self-injury and aggression is more methodologically mature than the evidence for most of this book, and this is because the behaviour is severe enough, and the outcomes are severe enough, and the field has invested heavily in the experimental analysis that produces reliable individual treatment plans.

**The functional analysis of self-injury is a well-established procedure with a large literature.** The proportion of cases in which the behaviour is maintained by automatic reinforcement is consistently reported to be high, and this is the single most clinically consequential finding in the field.

**Function-based treatment plans are more effective than non-function-based plans**, and this is the finding that the whole approach rests on.

**Differential reinforcement, differential reinforcement of compliance, and planned ignoring are effective for the escape- and attention-maintained cases**, and the evidence for the treatment of aggression maintained by escape is good.

**Pharmacological management is effective for a subset of self-injury**, particularly in the specific genetic syndromes, and it is essential in those cases. The clinician who works only behaviourally will miss the treatable neurochemical cases.

**The evidence for the environmental and medical components is weaker**, largely because these are difficult to evaluate in controlled trials, and the field's assessment of them rests on the clinical logic that a change in the antecedents precedes a change in the behaviour.

**The evidence on restrictive interventions is substantial and it is consistently negative**, with the studies showing higher rates of injury, higher rates of subsequent aggression, and worse outcomes. This is the point at which the evidence and the ethics converge: the interventions that are used most often in the settings where this work happens are the interventions with the worst evidence and the greatest risk.

### 8.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The samples are small and highly selected**, and they are drawn disproportionately from the most severe cases, who are the least representative of the population.

**The outcome measures are almost exclusively the frequency of the behaviour**, and the measures of the young person's quality of life, of their sense of dignity, of the family relationships, and of the durability of the change in naturalistic settings are much less systematically reported.

**The designs are the single-case experimental designs of Chapter 24**, and although they are rigorous, they are vulnerable to the reactivity of the participants and to the observer expectancy that the field has worked hard to address.

**The long-term outcomes are under-researched**, and there is a serious question about what happens to the young people whose behaviour has been reduced: what becomes of them, what is the placement plan, and what is the plan for the transition to adulthood.

**The literature is dominated by English-language research in North American and European settings**, and the cultural specificities of the settings in which this work happens — the tolerance for certain behaviours in a community, the family's understanding of the behaviour, the role of the extended family — are under-examined.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of the Functional Approach to Dangerous Behaviour

### 9.1 Strengths

**It is the only approach with a demonstrated mechanism.** The treatment is targeted at the contingency that maintains the behaviour, and when the contingency is identified and altered, the behaviour changes.

**It is the only approach that produces a differentiated plan.** A young person whose self-injury is automatically maintained and a young person whose aggression is escape-maintained receive entirely different plans, and the analysis is what makes the difference visible.

**It reduces the reliance on the most restrictive interventions**, because the analysis identifies the environmental changes that remove the need for them.

**It produces a written, teachable, and auditable plan**, which is what a team, a family, and a regulator can all work with.

**It treats the young person's behaviour as information rather than as a moral failure**, which is the foundation of every intervention in this chapter.

### 9.2 Limitations

**The analysis is slow and the behaviour is urgent.** The experimental analysis of self-injury takes days to weeks, and in the interval the young person is at risk. The safety-first framework is the solution, and it is an imperfect one.

**The analysis requires a stable setting with trained staff**, and the population in which this work is needed is characterised by instability and turnover. A plan written in one placement is frequently irrelevant in the next.

**The analysis can be wrong, and it can be wrong in a way that is hard to detect.** A behaviour that is maintained by several functions may appear to respond to one, and the response may be partial.

**The treatment of the behaviour is not the treatment of the young person.** A young person whose self-injury is a symptom of psychosis requires the treatment of the psychosis, and a young person whose aggression is a symptom of a depressive episode requires the treatment of the depression, and the behavioural plan is an adjunct. The functional approach is powerful precisely because it can be applied to behaviour that has no other treatment, and this is also its limitation, because it can be applied to behaviour that does have one.

**The approach requires an institutional commitment that many settings do not have.** The analysis is only as good as the environment in which the plan is implemented, and a placement that wants the behaviour to stop without wanting to change its environment will produce a plan that will fail.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 The cultural frame of dangerous behaviour

**The definition of dangerous is culturally and legally specific.** What is considered a serious risk, what is considered an acceptable level of self-injury, and what the state is willing to do about it vary considerably between jurisdictions and between communities, and a practitioner must know the local legal framework, the local threshold for intervention, and the local understanding of the young person and the family.

**The meaning of the behaviour is culturally framed.** A young person's aggression towards a family member may be read in one community as a symptom, in another as a discipline that has been applied to her, and in a third as a form of protest. The practitioner who applies a functional analysis without asking the family what they understand the behaviour to be doing will design a plan that is fighting the family's understanding.

**The family's role is culturally variable.** In some settings the family is the treatment team; in others, the family is a source of additional risk; and in a substantial number of cases the family is the placement that the behaviour has already destroyed, and the practitioner must be able to work with a family that has been told that their child is dangerous.

**The stigma attached to the behaviour is severe and it is socially reproduced.** A young person with a record of violent behaviour or of self-harm is excluded from ordinary opportunities long after the behaviour has stopped, and the practitioner who reduces the behaviour and does nothing about the consequences of the record has delivered a treatment with a short-term benefit and a long-term harm.

### 10.2 Ethical requirements

**The safety of the young person is the first consideration**, and the safety of others is the second, and both are treated as absolute in the immediate term. The rest of the ethical requirements operate within that frame.

**The least restrictive effective procedure.** Every plan should be able to answer the question of what alternatives were considered and why they were rejected, and the answer should be written down. This is both an ethical and a legal requirement in most jurisdictions.

**Proportionality and the documentation of restrictive interventions.** Every use of restraint, of seclusion, and of any restrictive medication must be documented with the specific circumstances, the duration, the less restrictive options that were attempted, the injuries sustained by anyone, and the review that followed. A plan that does not document these is a plan that cannot be defended.

**The young person's dignity.** The interventions that protect a young person can be experienced as punishment, and the practitioner should name this in the plan and design against it. Three specific practices help: the young person is told what will happen before it happens, and the telling is not conditional on their cooperation; the interventions are applied with as little force and as little audience as possible; and the young person is involved in the review of the plan, which is the single most effective protection against the accumulation of unexamined restrictions.

**The right to be heard.** The young person has a right to be consulted about the plan, and to have their account of the behaviour recorded, and — in most jurisdictions — a right to complain about the interventions used. The presence of a complaints route in the plan is itself a protective practice.

**The right to the least restrictive environment.** The plan should specify the target environment, and the goal should be stated as the young person's return to the least restrictive setting in which the behaviour can be managed, with the steps and the criteria. A plan that has no stated goal of restoration is a plan of permanent containment.

**Family inclusion.** The family should be included in the plan, supported, and given something to do, and the support should be practical. The alternative is a family that is excluded from the plan and is then unable to support it.

**Coordination of the multidisciplinary team.** The plan should be developed by a team, reviewed by a team, and shared with the family, and a practitioner working alone in this area is working outside the professional standard.

**Avoidance of the instrumentalisation of the young person.** A young person whose dangerous behaviour makes them valuable as a demonstration, as a training case, or as a research participant has been abused, and the field's history contains a number of such episodes.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Self-injurious behaviour (SIB)** — Behaviour in which a person inflicts physical harm on themselves, including skin picking, scratching, cutting, head banging, eye poking, and hair pulling.
- **Aggression** — Behaviour intended to cause harm, damage, or intimidation to another person or to property; including verbal aggression, destruction, and physical violence.
- **The safety-first framework** — The ordering of the clinical work in which immediate safety precedes medical assessment, which precedes the functional assessment, which precedes the treatment.
- **Safety plan** — The written, shared, and rehearsed specification of the predictors of the behaviour, the response at each stage, the provision of the alternative, and the response after an episode.
- **Early indicators** — The physiological and behavioural precursors of an episode, individual to the young person, and usually the most valuable single output of the descriptive assessment.
- **Functional analysis of self-injury** — The experimental determination of the consequence that maintains the behaviour, using alone, play, attention, demand, and control conditions.
- **The alone condition** — The experimental condition in which the young person is alone with an observer, used to identify automatically maintained behaviour.
- **The play condition** — The condition in which the environment is arranged to be engaging and the behaviour is not attended to, used as the control for automatic maintenance.
- **The attention condition** — The condition in which attention is delivered contingently on the behaviour, used to identify attention-maintained behaviour.
- **The demand condition** — The condition in which demands are made and escape is contingent on the behaviour, used to identify escape-maintained behaviour.
- **The control condition** — The condition in which the consequence of interest is delivered non-contingently, controlling for the presence of the consequence rather than its contingency.
- **Automatic reinforcement** — Maintenance of a behaviour by its own sensory or affective consequences, without reference to the social environment; the most common function of self-injury.
- **Automatically maintained behaviour** — Behaviour that persists in the alone and play conditions and is not maintained by a social consequence.
- **Escape-maintained aggression** — Aggression that reliably terminates or postpones a demand; the most common function of aggression in young people and the most reliably treated.
- **Attention-maintained aggression** — Aggression that reliably produces adult attention; the treatment is differential reinforcement of the appropriate behaviour and the planned ignoring of the aggression.
- **Differential reinforcement of compliance (DRC)** — The reinforcement of compliance with a demand together with the extinction of the escalation; the core treatment for escape-maintained aggression.
- **Planned ignoring** — The deliberate and complete non-response to a behaviour, with no eye contact, speech, or signal, used to extinguish attention-maintained behaviour.
- **The escalation sequence** — The detailed record of the adult behaviour and the young person's behaviour during an escalation; usually the most informative single component of the analysis of aggression.
- **Establishing conditions** — The historical circumstances in which the behaviour was first performed and acquired its function.
- **Setting demands** — The automatic, attention, escape, and combined demands describing the circumstances under which the behaviour occurs.
- **The behavioural alternative** — A behaviour that is functionally equivalent to the target behaviour and is more efficient, more acceptable, and less dangerous.
- **Extinction burst** — A temporary increase in the frequency or intensity of behaviour at the start of extinction, which is expected and should be predicted to the young person and the team.
- **Restrictive intervention** — Physical restraint, seclusion, or the chemical equivalent; a last-resort intervention of shortest possible duration.
- **Last-resort intervention** — An intervention used only when the young person poses an imminent danger and less restrictive measures have been tried and failed.
- **Manifestation determination** — The procedural requirement, under disability legislation, that a behaviour resulting in a change of placement be assessed against the possibility that it is a manifestation of the young person's disability.
- **Functional behavioural assessment (FBA)** — The assessment required in many jurisdictions before a change of placement for a student with a disability.
- **Medical review** — The systematic review of medication, pain, sleep, seizures, nutrition, and the sensory environment, conducted before the behavioural analysis of a dangerous behaviour.
- **The mood-disorder presentation of aggression** — Irritability as the characteristic presentation of a depressive episode in adolescence, frequently misreferred as aggression.
- **Lesch–Nyhan syndrome** — A rare genetic disorder associated with a characteristic pattern of severe self-injury and with a neurochemical profile that is substantially responsive to pharmacological management.
- **Corneal self-injury in Smith–Magenis syndrome** — A characteristic and severe self-injurious behaviour associated with a specific genetic syndrome and with a distinctive sleep disturbance.
- **Dignity protection** — The clinical practice of designing safety interventions so that they are not experienced by the young person as punishment.
- **Restoration goal** — The explicit target of returning the young person to the least restrictive environment in which the behaviour can be managed.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

This chapter treated two behavioural presentations that are severe enough to require a different set of clinical conventions from the rest of the book, and it stated the safety-first framework at the outset: the safety of the young person comes first and is not contingent on the completeness of the assessment; the least restrictive effective procedure governs the intervention; the treatment of the behaviour is not the treatment of the condition; and the intervention must be accompanied by a plan for the young person's dignity.

The history explained why the field arrived where it has: the institutional tradition treated unexplained behaviour harshly, and the decisive development was the experimental analysis of self-injury, whose central finding — that the behaviour is overwhelmingly maintained by automatic reinforcement — changed practice fundamentally, because containment removes the behaviour without removing the contingency that produces it. The parallel work on aggression established that much of it is escape-maintained, which is the direct opposite of the assumption embedded in most schools' responses.

The safety hierarchy was set out at four levels, and the safety plans for self-injury and for aggression were described in their five components each, with the escalation procedure and the return condition identified as the most important and the most frequently improvised elements. The position on restrictive interventions was stated plainly: last resort, shortest duration, with oversight, and associated with higher rates of injury and worse outcomes, which is an argument for the seriousness of their use and for the institutional responsibility to design them out of the environment.

The clinical presentations were described with their differential diagnoses, and two specific points were emphasised: the genetic syndromes in which pharmacological management is a substantial part of the treatment, and the mood-disorder presentation of aggression in adolescence, which is frequently misreferred to a behaviour service. The assessment was described as a comprehensive exercise, with the risk assessment identified as a separate document with a separate structure.

The functional analyses of both behaviours were described in detail, with the conditions, the interpretation, and the establishing conditions, and the treatment of the escape-maintained case of aggression was set out in the detail in which it must be implemented, including the predicted extinction burst and the requirement for the adults' response to the escalation to be specified, trained, and consistent.

The case of Marcus demonstrated the sequence in operation and, in doing so, demonstrated the limits of the method. The experimental analysis found the automatic function and the treatment addressed it, and the young person's own account, offered in the eighth week, named the escape function that the plan had not addressed. Both were correct, and the argument of the case is that the young person's own account is data, is gathered as early as possible, and is a supplement to rather than an alternative to the experimental analysis.

The evidence is methodologically mature for the functional analysis and for function-based treatment, and the evidence on restrictive interventions is consistently negative. The limitations were stated: the small and highly selected samples, the behavioural rather than experiential outcome measures, the single-case designs, the under-researched long-term outcomes, and the cultural narrowness of the literature.

The ethical requirements were set out in full — least restrictive procedure with documented alternatives, proportional and documented restrictive interventions, dignity protection with three specific practices, the right to be heard and to complain, the right to the least restrictive environment with a restoration goal, family inclusion with practical support, team coordination, and the refusal to instrumentalise the young person. The cultural analysis addressed the culturally and legally specific definition of danger, the culturally framed meaning of the behaviour, the variable role of the family, and the severe social stigma that follows the young person long after the behaviour has stopped.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 29.1 — The Safety Plan (90 minutes, individual)

Write a complete safety plan for a fictional young person with a behaviour that is dangerous to themselves. Specify the functional prediction, the early indicators, the response to the early indicators, the response to the behaviour, the provision of the alternative, and the response after an episode. Then write the specific sentence that an adult will say to the young person at each of the three points. The sentences are the hard part, and the exercise produces a plan that is genuinely usable rather than a document that satisfies a template.

### Activity 29.2 — The ABC of a Dangerous Behaviour (75 minutes, field-based)

With supervision, record the antecedents, the behaviour, and the consequences of a dangerous behaviour in a placement, using the detailed record rather than the summary one. Record in particular what the adult did immediately after the escalation, because the escalation sequence is usually the most informative component of the whole assessment. Then write the two competing functional hypotheses and the observation that would distinguish them. Do not intervene in the escalation, and do not record information that identifies the young person to anyone who does not need it.

### Activity 29.3 — Restorative versus Extinction (60 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, take a behaviour maintained by escape and design two plans: one that uses differential reinforcement of compliance with extinction of the escalation, and one that uses a restorative approach in which the young person repairs the consequence of the escalation. Identify what each plan predicts will happen in the first two weeks, and identify what each plan requires of the adults. Then explain why a plan that mixes the two approaches is usually worse than either. The discussion of the adults' requirements is where the exercise earns its value.

### Activity 29.4 — The Dignity Audit (45 minutes, individual)

Take a safety plan for a young person, real or fictional, and audit it from the young person's point of view. For each element, write what the young person will think and feel when it happens. Then revise the plan so that the young person's experience of it is defensible. The revisions are usually small — the timing of a warning, the choice of a location, the wording of a sentence, the presence of an audience — and the sum of them is the difference between a plan the young person co-operates with and one they will not.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the method.** Read Iwata and colleagues (1982) and then the 1994 book, and read the chapter on the functional analysis of self-injury in any current applied behaviour analysis textbook. The 1982 paper is short and is still the best introduction to the logic of the experimental analysis of dangerous behaviour.

**For the treatment.** Read Aman and colleagues (2000) and the current treatment reviews, and read the population-specific chapters on autism spectrum disorder and on the genetic syndromes. The pharmacological section is not optional in those populations.

**For the safety and the law.** Read the local regulations on seclusion and restraint, and read the professional position statements. Read them before working in the area, not after an incident.

**For the evidence.** Read the reviews of function-based treatment and the studies of restrictive interventions, which are consistently negative and which are the most important empirical basis for the ethical position in this chapter.

**For the ethical and cultural analysis.** Read the disability rights literature on the treatment of people who are dangerous and on the rights of people with intellectual disabilities, and read the literature on the social exclusion that follows a history of violent behaviour. The combination is the preparation for the part of the work that happens after the behaviour has stopped.
