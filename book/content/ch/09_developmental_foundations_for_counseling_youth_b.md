## 7. The Family Life Cycle and the Adolescent

### 7.1 The developmental position of the adolescent in the family

The adolescent occupies a distinctive structural position in the family system, and the structural family therapists of the 1970s identified this position with a precision that remains useful: the adolescent is the family member whose developmental task is the most clearly incompatible with the family's established organisation, and who is therefore the member most likely to become the location of the family's unresolved tensions.

The clinical observation is that families frequently organise around the adolescent's difficulty in ways that serve the system rather than the adolescent, and that the adolescent's symptoms, conduct, or withdrawal may be doing work for the family. Chapter 31 develops the structural account and Chapter 33 the practical work with parents.

Three consequences for practice follow.

**The apparent problem may be located in the wrong place.** A young person's refusal to attend school may be maintaining a marital conflict, a sibling rivalry, a grandparent boundary, or a parental avoidance. A practitioner who locates the problem entirely in the adolescent will spend the work on the wrong target.

**The developmental task is a family task.** The adolescent's push toward autonomy is a developmental task, and the family's response to it is a family process. The useful clinical question is not "how do I get this adolescent to comply?" but "what does this family do when one of its members wants to become an adult?"

**The risk of a too-early closure.** The structural clinicians described the developmental misfiling of the adolescent, in which a seventeen-year-old is placed in a role — the responsible one, the emotional caretaker of a parent, the surrogate parent of siblings, the holder of the family's emotional expression — that belongs to an earlier developmental stage. A good deal of what presents in adolescent practice is the consequence of a role that was allocated in the family long before the young person was asked to grow into it.

### 7.2 Family transitions

The family life cycle framework, in the formulation of Duvall, describes a sequence of developmental stages with characteristic tasks. The stage relevant here is the one in which the adolescent is leaving the family of origin and the young adult is establishing an independent household. Its tasks are the reorganisation of the marriage, the redefinition of the parent–child relationship, the development of the young adult's own household and occupational role, and the establishment of a satisfactory system of boundaries between the family of origin and the new household.

The clinical relevance of the framework is that it locates the young person's difficulty in a *transitional* period and makes the work of transition explicit, rather than treating the difficulty as a character trait. The 21-year-old who cannot leave home is not "dependent"; the 21-year-old who cannot leave home is in a transition whose tasks have not been accomplished, and the reasons are usually a mixture of developmental readiness, family system resistance, and the material conditions of housing and employment.

The framework also has a well-known limitation, which is that it describes an idealised sequence, with a nuclear family in a prosperous society, and that families in the present period — reconstituted, single-parent, transnational, multigenerational, in poverty, in exile — frequently do not pass through the stages in the sequence, and that the framework has been used historically to pathologise families that depart from it. The transitional understanding is nonetheless valuable when used descriptively rather than normatively.

## 8. Moral Development and the Adolescent's Ethical Reasoning

Kohlberg's three-level model of moral reasoning remains the most commonly used framework in the field, and its developmental sequence — the preconventional, the conventional, and the postconventional — has a straightforward clinical relevance: the level at which a young person reasons about a matter predicts the kinds of appeal that will work.

**The preconventional level** (approximately ages four to ten) is oriented to obedience and punishment and to the exchange of favours; the young person is oriented to what they will get and what they will avoid.

**The conventional level** (approximately ten to sixteen) is oriented to interpersonal approval and to law and order; the young person is oriented to what others will think and what the rules require. This is the level at which most adolescents are, and it is the level at which the appeal to "what would your friends and family think?" is developmentally appropriate and the appeal to abstract principle is not.

**The postconventional level** (approximately sixteen onwards, and reached by a minority) is oriented to social contract and to universal ethical principles.

The clinical use of the framework is specific. With a young person at the conventional level, a discussion framed in terms of principles will be heard as adult preaching; a discussion framed in terms of the effect on the people they care about, or in terms of the agreement they themselves made, will be heard as relevant. And an intervention that appeals to a level of reasoning the young person has not reached will fail, however morally satisfying it is to the practitioner.

The framework's limitations are significant and should be acknowledged. The research on gender differences in moral reasoning that originally supported the model has been substantially undermined, and the sequence is now understood as less rigidly age-linked than Kohlberg proposed. And the model's individualistic and rights-based assumptions are not universal; the research on moral reasoning in non-Western settings finds a variety of structures, including a strong emphasis on relational and contextual reasoning, which the three-level scheme does not accommodate.

## 9. Social Development and Peer Relationships

### 9.1 The structure of the peer network in the second decade

The developmental research on peer relationships across the second decade establishes a clear sequence.

In early adolescence, roughly ages eleven to thirteen, there is a marked increase in the salience of peers, a decrease in the time spent with parents, and a rapid expansion and restructuring of the peer network, with the formation of larger and more complex groups, the rise of cliques, and the beginning of the developmental sequence of peer relations that moves toward the stable, reciprocal dyadic friendships of later adolescence.

In middle adolescence, roughly thirteen to sixteen, the developmental task of establishing a stable reciprocal relationship is prominent; the developmental literature identifies the capacity to sustain a mutual relationship, characterised by the ability to give and receive support, to manage conflict, and to sustain the relationship through periods of distance, as a key developmental competence.

In late adolescence, roughly sixteen to nineteen, the peer network contracts towards a smaller number of intimate relationships, and the developmental significance shifts towards the capacity to sustain a committed relationship over time, with a concomitant decline in the pressure of the peer group.

### 9.2 Why the peer relationship is a clinical target

The developmental significance of the peer relationship for an adolescent explains why peer relationships are a legitimate target of the therapeutic work, and why their absence is a developmental task uncompleted rather than a peripheral deficit.

The evidence linking peer connectedness and quality of close relationships to a wide range of adolescent outcomes is substantial, and it runs in both directions: the quality of the adolescent's relationships is associated with lower rates of depression, anxiety, substance use, and suicidality, and with better academic engagement, and the direction of the causal relationship between peer relations and internalising symptoms is itself complicated by the fact that depressed and anxious young people are, on the one hand, more likely to experience peer difficulties and, on the other hand, more likely to withdraw from peers in a way that is then read as a preference.

The clinical implication is that a young person whose peer relationships have collapsed is a young person for whom a central developmental task has become blocked, and that the work of restoring the possibility of peer connection — which usually means restoring the conditions for it, rather than instructing the young person in social skills — is a legitimate and often central part of the treatment.

### 9.3 The limits of social skills training

The social skills training literature is large, and its evidence base for children with conduct problems is reasonably good. Its application to adolescents with peer withdrawal is more problematic, for three reasons.

The first is that skills training presupposes that the deficit is in skills, and in many cases of adolescent withdrawal it is not. The young person may have the skills and not the opportunity, or may have the skills and not the motivation, or may be correctly assessing a social environment that does not offer a safe place to be a friend. A skills programme delivered in the second case teaches a young person to approach people who have already shown they are not safe to approach.

The second is that the research on the generalisation of social skills training is not encouraging: the skills are frequently learned in the training context and do not transfer to the natural environment.

The third is that the approach can be experienced as a further instance of the adult world telling the young person what is wrong with them and prescribing a performance of normalcy, and for an adolescent whose withdrawal is a deliberate response to a social world that has been hostile, the prescription is both ineffective and ethically questionable.

## 10. Developmentally Appropriate Formulation

The developmental perspective transforms the formulation in five specific ways, and a student should be able to name them for any case.

**The developmental description.** The formulation should state what is developmentally expected at the young person's stage, in the specific domains in question, and what the deviation consists of. "She is avoidant of unfamiliar peers in a way that exceeds the normal caution of early adolescence" is a developmental statement; "she has social anxiety" is a classificatory one that does no work.

**The developmental task.** The formulation should identify the developmental task that is blocked, in the young person's own terms. For an adolescent with a peer collapse, the task is the establishment of a stable reciprocal relationship; for a fifteen-year-old who is sexually active and is frightened of a pregnancy, the task is the integration of a new sexual and relational identity; for a nine-year-old who is failing at school, the task is the acquisition of the skills of industry and the sense of competence.

**The developmental mismatch.** The formulation should identify where the young person's current development and the demands being made of them are out of alignment. The most common such misalignments in practice are the school that demands adult-level self-regulation from a fourteen-year-old; the family that demands adult-level emotional responsibility from a twelve-year-old; and the service that demands adult-level insight from a nine-year-old.

**The developmental trajectory.** The formulation should state what the direction of change is likely to be with or without intervention. This is the most clinically useful and the most neglected element, because developmental trajectory is the thing that tells a practitioner whether to wait.

**The developmental timing of the intervention.** The formulation should state why now is the time to intervene and why the specific intervention is appropriate at this developmental stage rather than earlier or later. A family-based intervention with parents of a fourteen-year-old's peer withdrawal may be better delivered at fifteen, when the developmental task becomes more salient.

## 11. Developmental Assessment

### 11.1 Sources of developmental information

Developmental assessment draws on five sources, and a practitioner should systematically consider all five rather than relying on the young person's self-report.

**The young person's account.** Valuable, developmentally biased in predictable ways, and subject to the distortions discussed in the cognitive chapter. A young person at the concrete operational stage will report concrete events accurately and will be unreliable about abstract, general, or future-oriented questions, and a practitioner who asks "how often do you usually feel sad?" of a nine-year-old will receive an uninterpretable answer.

**The parent's account.** Informed by a longer time span and by the parent's own developmental expectations, and shaped by the factors that make parent accounts unreliable — the parent's own unmet developmental needs, the difficulty of a parent's appraisal of an adolescent's internal state, and the tendency to attribute to the young person what is actually a family process.

**The teacher's account.** Informed by a different context and a different set of observations, and usually focused on the visible and the disruptive. A teacher may be an excellent observer of a young person's social position and an unreliable observer of their internal state.

**Records and previous assessments.** Often the only source of information about early development, and frequently available where the young person's own account is not. Chapter 53 discusses the assessment instruments and their use.

**Direct observation.** The only way to establish what actually happens, and under-used in practice. Where it is possible to observe the young person in a peer setting, in the classroom, or with a family member, the observation is frequently more informative than any account.

### 11.2 The developmental interview

A developmentally appropriate developmental interview has five components, and the ordering matters.

**The current functioning.** What the young person is doing now, in concrete terms. The anchor of the whole interview, because it is the most reliable data and because it establishes the base rate.

**The history.** The sequence of significant events, in order, with attention to the timing in relation to the developmental periods. The developmental significance of an event is a function of when it occurred, and the practitioner should be reconstructing the timeline explicitly.

**The developmental tasks.** What the young person is currently working on, in the domains of the framework: identity, intimacy, autonomy, industry, and so forth. Asking about a task in a developmentally appropriate way requires the abstract language of the framework and the concrete language of the young person.

**The caregiving environment.** Who is in the household, who does what, how the young person's care is arranged in practice rather than in intention, and what the history of the family has been.

**The young person's own account of their development.** How they see themselves changing, what they are trying to become, what they are afraid of becoming. This is the most clinically useful part of the interview and the part most often omitted, because it requires a level of trust that takes more than one session.

### 11.3 The developmental formulation template

The template in Table 9.1 is a useful working structure for a developmental formulation and is used in the formulation assignment described in Chapter 58.

| Element | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Developmental stage and its tasks | What is expected at this stage and what is the young person working on? |
| Developmental description | What is observed, in what domains, and how does it depart from expectation? |
| History and timing | What happened, and when, and what is the developmental significance of that timing? |
| Caregiving and context | Who cares for the young person, and how, and with what history? |
| Current maintaining processes | What is keeping the difficulty going, at each level of the system? |

## 12. Case Illustration: The Child Who Could Not Be Silly

**The referral.** Ruben, nine, is referred by his school and his mother for "anxiety." He has not attended school for eleven weeks. He had been markedly shy in his first year and had one friendship, which ended in the summer before the referral. He is bright, does his homework, reads voraciously, and his mother reports that he "is perfectly fine at home — he's just got on with his games and his books and he's not interested in people."

**The developmental questions.** The practitioner, working from the developmental framework, asks a series of questions that are the same questions one would ask in an adult case and that are worth spelling out, because the answers change the case entirely.

**What is expected at nine?** Industry versus inferiority. The developmental task is the acquisition of the skills of the school subjects and the sense of competence that comes from doing them well. This is the year in which the child learns that he is good at some things and not others, and the sense of "I am a person who can do some things" is established.

**What happened, and when?** The friendship ended in the summer. Ruben's mother, a single parent working shifts, describes the summer as "fine" and then concedes that she was working eighteen-hour shifts and that Ruben's grandmother, who had been his daytime carer, moved to her daughter's house in another town in May. The single most important fact in the case arrives at the third interview.

**What does Ruben do with people?** He does not know how to answer the question. When pressed, he says that he "can talk to people about things" — and then, after a long pause, that he has thought about what to say first, and that he has not been able to think of anything. The practitioner recognises this as a description of a failure of the social-cognitive problem solving that the developmental literature describes as central to the peer task in early adolescence and important already in the school-age years.

**What is the caregiving environment?** Ruben's mother is present but exhausted, and the practical arrangements are that Ruben is largely alone in the evenings.

**The formulation.** The developmental formulation is that Ruben is a child who has lost, in the space of two months, the peer relationship that was the site of his developmental task, and the caregiving arrangement that supported it, and has retreated into the one domain in which he is unambiguously competent — his reading and his games. The anxiety is not a primary emotional disorder; it is the developmentally expectable response to the loss of the social context in which a nine-year-old's industry can be exercised, in a child who has no other domain in which to be competent, and who has lost the adult who was available.

**Why the standard approaches would have failed.** A behavioural approach built on graded social exposure would have required Ruben to approach other children, and the reason he does not is that he has not yet worked out how to begin a conversation. A cognitive approach would have required him to produce a thought record, and at nine, in the concrete operational stage, the abstract propositional thought required is at the limit of what he can do, and in any case the problem is not a distorted appraisal. A family approach would have been the right first move in any case, and it is complicated by the fact that his mother's shifts are not a choice she has made freely.

**What the practitioner does.** The first intervention is practical: the shifts are renegotiated, the grandmother's departure is acknowledged, and the school is asked to accommodate a return with a specific adult peer to talk to, because the developmental research is clear that peer-supported rather than adult-supported re-entry is what works with young children. The second intervention is to the developmental task itself: Ruben's competence is extended from the solitary domain into a social one, through a structured, small-group, play-based activity with three other children, run by a practitioner who is explicitly a play therapist. The third is the developmental check: what happened to the sense of competence that he has in reading, and can it be generalised?

**The teaching point.** The referral said "anxiety." The developmental formulation says "loss of the site of the developmental task, with a predictable developmental response." The second is a formulation that predicts a course and identifies an intervention; the first is a label that predicts nothing and generates a treatment that will not work.

## 13. Evidence and Research Support

**Well supported.** The association between supportive adult relationships and adolescent outcomes. The association between peer connectedness and quality and adolescent mental health. The developmental sequence of peer relationships across the second decade. The increase in peer salience and the shift in the parent–adolescent relationship across adolescence. The normative progression of cognitive capacity and the specific limits of abstract reasoning in the school-age years. The association of sleep restriction with adolescent mood and attentional difficulties. The relationship of attachment classification in infancy to a range of later relational and emotional outcomes, with the magnitude of the associations being modest and the causal claims being appropriately cautious.

**Supported with qualifications.** The claim that the prefrontal cortex continues to develop into the mid-twenties and that this has behavioural consequences; the anatomical data are well established and the behavioural inferences are less direct than the popular presentation implies. The claim that the adolescent brain is "prone to risk"; the risk-taking findings are robust and the explanation of them is contested. The claim that identity exploration is the central developmental task of adolescence; the framework is theoretically influential and empirically weaker than its popularity suggests. The claim that moral reasoning develops in the Kohlbergian sequence; the sequence has partial support and the specific predictions are weakly supported.

**Weakly supported or contested.** The developmental appropriateness of the stage boundaries. The claim that the person is fully capable of insight at a given age. The claim that early attachment predicts later adjustment with the precision usually implied. The universal applicability of the nuclear-family developmental sequence.

**Absent or very thin.** Evidence on the developmental adaptation of specific therapies, which is generally limited to a small number of well-studied approaches. Research on the effectiveness of developmentally adapted interventions with specific populations. Longitudinal research linking specific developmental tasks to specific intervention components.

## 14. Strengths and Limitations of the Developmental Perspective

**Strengths.** It replaces moral judgement with interpretation, and that substitution is the single most valuable thing the perspective offers a practitioner working with adolescents. It constrains technique selection in ways that improve outcomes. It provides a framework for prognosis and for timing, which are the aspects of practice most often handled by habit. And it supplies the developmental task as a concrete, non-pathologising description of what a young person is trying to do.

**Limitations.** Its normative data are old, Western, and derived from unselected samples, and the norms have shifted considerably since they were collected — which means that a considerable part of the "developmental" knowledge in the field is in fact historical. Its stage models are more rigid than the data support, and the risk of a practitioner treating a young person as "too young" for a task is a real one. The perspective is individualistic, and it sits awkwardly with the ecological and systemic approaches. And the enormous research literature on the second decade has been generated in cultures whose adolescence looks unlike that of the cultures many of the field's practitioners work in.

## 15. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**Development is culturally organised.** The developmental tasks, their timing, and their content are not universal. The developmental literature on schooling shows that the age of formal secondary education, the content of the curriculum, and the age at which adult-like responsibilities are assumed vary enormously across societies, and the adolescent in one context may be functioning at a developmental level that the adolescent in another context reaches several years later. The developmental framing in this chapter is a Western developmental framing, and the practitioner should treat it as one set of expectations among several.

**The danger of developmental explanation of harm.** The developmental perspective can be misused to explain away the harm a young person has experienced — to treat the adult's failure as a developmental stage, or to treat the consequences of poverty or discrimination as a maturational lag. A practitioner who uses the developmental lens to soften the description of a young circumstances will find that the softening is itself the harm. The developmental perspective should describe what the young person is doing; it should not describe what has been done to them.

**The ethics of developmentally appropriate pacing.** The requirement that interventions be developmentally matched carries an ethical obligation that is not always recognised: that the young person should be treated in a manner appropriate to their age in matters of privacy, explanation, decision-making, and confidentiality, and that the explanation of the treatment given to a young person should be in language the young person can actually use. A nine-year-old who is given a "confidentiality" explanation designed for a forty-year-old has been given less than they were owed.

**The ethical requirement of developmental assessment.** The ethical codes require assessment that is appropriate to the client's developmental capacity, and that requirement is not satisfied by the mere administration of an adult instrument to a child. The assessment of a young person should be developmentally appropriate, should gather information from multiple sources, and should be reviewed in light of what the young person can contribute.

## 16. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Developmental appropriateness** — The requirement that an intervention be matched to the young person's cognitive, emotional, social, and autonomy capacities; a condition of validity rather than an accommodation.
- **Zone of proximal development** — The distance between what a child can do independently and what she can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other; the basis for the optimal difficulty of a task.
- **Scaffolding** — The provision of temporary support that is progressively withdrawn as the young person becomes capable; a description of both the learning process and the developmental shape of the counselling relationship.
- **Concrete operational stage** — The developmental stage from approximately seven to eleven, in which logical reasoning is available for concrete material but not for abstraction.
- **Formal operational stage** — The stage from approximately eleven onwards, in which abstract and hypothetical reasoning becomes available, unevenly and in a context-dependent way.
- **Decentration** — The developmental capacity to consider more than one aspect of a situation simultaneously, and to distinguish appearance from reality; acquired in the concrete operational stage.
- **Executive function** — The set of capacities — working memory, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and planning — that support goal-directed behaviour; a key target of developmental assessment with young people.
- **Working memory** — The limited-capacity system for holding and manipulating information; a common bottleneck in the cognitive techniques with young people.
- **Identity foreclosure** — A premature and rigid commitment, often made under family or community pressure, that forecloses further exploration; the developmental form of difficulty in the adolescent identity task.
- **Possible self** — The version of oneself one is trying to become, together with the feared version; central to the developmental account of adolescent anxiety and depression.
- **Attachment** — The early developmental system of proximity-seeking and security; the foundation of the account of relational expectation and of the individual differences in it.
- **Internal working model** — The enduring representation of the self and of others formed in the early attachment relationship and carried forward into later relationships.
- **Strange Situation** — Ainsworth's procedure for classifying the quality of infant attachment into secure, avoidant, resistant, and disorganised patterns.
- **Disorganized attachment** — The attachment pattern associated with trauma, maltreatment, and unresolved loss in the caregiver, marked by contradictory or frightened behaviour in the presence of the caregiver.
- **Parental autonomy support** — The caregiving characteristic of providing rationale, choice, and responsiveness rather than control; associated with better adolescent outcomes.
- **Peer attachment** — The developmental significance of peers as attachment figures in adolescence, and of the capacity to sustain a reciprocal relationship.
- **Eveningness chronotype** — The developmental shift in circadian timing during puberty, and the source of the mismatch with early school start times.
- **Possible risk sensitivity** — The adolescent tendency to weight the potential for social reward more heavily, and uncertainty less accurately, than adults do.
- **Developmental trajectory** — The expected course of the young person's difficulty with and without intervention; the basis of prognosis and of the decision whether to wait.
- **Developmental mismatch** — The misalignment between the demands made of a young person and their developmental capacity in that domain.
:::

## 17. Chapter Summary

Every theory in this book was developed with adults, and the transfer of an adult theory to a young person requires developmental modification in the areas of interpretation, technique selection, dosage, and meaning. The developmental perspective is a discipline of interpretation that replaces moral judgement with a developmental account of what the young person is doing.

The frameworks in this chapter contribute different things. Piaget's stages and Vygotsky's zone of proximal development constrain technique selection and establish the optimal difficulty of a task. Erikson's psychosocial tasks, and the more recent work on the adolescent identity task and the possible self, describe what the young person is working on. The attachment tradition explains relational expectations, the parent's role, and the developmental significance of the adolescent's separation from the family. The neurodevelopmental literature explains the adolescent's heightened sensitivity to social and emotional information, the delayed maturation of self-regulation, the shift in circadian timing, and the specific asymmetry in the capacity to reason about an uncertain and emotionally charged future.

Two domains are easy to overlook and central in practice: the family life cycle, which locates the adolescent's difficulty in the family system and identifies the developmental task as a family task, and the peer relationship, which is a developmental task in its own right and a legitimate target of the work.

The developmental formulation specifies the stage and its tasks, the developmental description, the history and its timing, the caregiving and context, the maintaining processes, the trajectory, the developmental appropriateness of the plan, and the review points. The case of Ruben illustrates the whole argument: a referral that said "anxiety" was reformulated, through the developmental questions, into a loss of the site of the developmental task with a predictable developmental response — a formulation that predicted a course and identified an intervention.

## 19. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 9.1 — The Developmental Timeline (2 hours, individual)

Take a case from your placement or a peer role-play and construct a developmental timeline: a single vertical axis, aged zero to eighteen, on which every significant event, both developmental and historical, is placed. Then draw a second axis, marked with the developmental tasks, and overlay it. In three hundred words, identify the two points at which the historical event and the developmental task interact. This exercise is the fastest way to learn to see a case developmentally, and it is directly transferable to the formulation assignment.

### Activity 9.2 — The Same Technique, Three Ages (90 minutes, groups of four)

Each group takes a single technique from this book — thought recording, exposure, behavioural activation, or the miracle question — and specifies exactly how it would be delivered to a seven-year-old, a twelve-year-old, and a seventeen-year-old: the wording, the medium, the session length, the presence of the parent, the homework, the review points, and the expected timeline. The differences between the three versions will be larger than most students expect, and the exercise is the best preparation for the developmental adaptations described in the technique chapters.

### Activity 9.3 — The Developmental Interview (75 minutes, triads)

Conduct a developmental interview with a peer playing a fourteen-year-old who has recently lost a friend and has withdrawn. Use the five components in Section 11.2 and the five assessment questions in Section 11.1. The observer records which questions produced the most useful information and which produced confusion. The purpose is to establish the developmental history in a way that is developmental rather than an interrogation, which is a skill that takes practice and is not taught anywhere else.

## 20. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the developmental science.** Read Steinberg (2014) for the adolescent synthesis, the relevant chapters of Damon (1998) for the primary sources, and Sroufe (2005) for the attachment tradition. Read them critically: the normative data are old and the field's confidence exceeds its data.

**For the clinical application.** Read the developmental chapters of the child and adolescent psychotherapy handbooks, and then read the "developmental considerations" sections in each technique chapter of this book as a set. Read them together, because the pattern of what is required is clearer when the chapters are read consecutively.

**For the neurodevelopmental claims.** Read the review literature rather than the popular accounts, and be especially careful with the language: "the adolescent brain is not fully developed" is a statement that is both true and misleading, and the group of students who can explain precisely in what sense it is misleading will be at a professional advantage.

**For the cultural critique.** Read the developmental literature outside the United States and Western Europe, and the cross-cultural developmental psychology literature on the cultural organisation of the second decade. The developmental expectations in this chapter are a specific cultural product, and a practitioner who works with a different population should be able to say so.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is the developmental science that constrains and informs any theory applied to children and adolescents. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is conceptual clarity, evidence appraisal, therapeutic relationship, and disciplined formulation. The mechanism to keep in view is how a theory turns observations into explanations, predictions, interventions, and reviewable outcomes. The characteristic professional risk is mistaking an elegant explanation for evidence, or applying a preferred theory before the young person has been understood. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in the first counselling sessions, assessment meetings, and case discussions, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Leah is fifteen and is seen in a family support service for repeated classroom escalation. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Leah's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Leah explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on conceptual clarity, evidence appraisal, therapeutic relationship, and disciplined formulation.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Leah chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Leah has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Leah can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Developmental Foundations for Counseling Youth to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
