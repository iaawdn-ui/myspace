# Stabilisation, Grounding, and Phase-Oriented Work

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain what stabilisation is for, and distinguish it from both the processing work and the ordinary supportive conversation.
- State the core skills of a stabilisation programme and describe how each is taught to a young person.
- Describe the grounding techniques and the conditions under which each is used.
- Explain the phase-oriented approach to case management, and the criteria that determine the phase.
- Apply a stabilisation programme to a case involving a young person with severe dysregulation.
- Critically evaluate the limitations of the stabilisation work, including the risk that it becomes a way of avoiding the difficult work.

## Chapter Overview

Stabilisation is the least glamorous and the most necessary work in this book. It is the work that happens before anything else can happen: the young person who cannot remain in the room, who cannot stop checking the door, who cannot sleep, who cannot stop dissociating in the middle of a conversation, cannot do a cognitive technique, cannot process a memory, and cannot be helped by a practitioner who is trying to do either.

The central clinical proposition of this chapter is simple: **a young person's capacity to be in a relationship, to think, and to feel is a capacity that is regulated by the body, and the body can be taught to be more regulated.** A young person who is constantly in a threat state is not being difficult; the nervous system is doing what it was trained to do, and it can be trained differently, and the training takes time and repetition and it usually happens before any talk about the trauma.

The skills themselves are deceptively simple, and the chapter is largely devoted to them: the orienting to the environment, the grounding through the senses, the paced breathing, the "safe place", the "container", the "light switch", the body scan, the grounding through movement, the time travel to a safe memory, and the interpersonal skills of asking for help and of saying no. Each of them is a well-specified technique with a specific mechanism, a specific teaching procedure, and a specific set of contraindications, and a practitioner who knows them can do a great deal with a young person in the first weeks of a treatment.

The chapter also treats the two most common failures of this work: the practitioner who stabilises the young person in order to be able to do the processing work, and the practitioner who stabilises the young person instead of doing the processing work. The second is more common and is more insidious, and the chapter sets out the clinical criteria that distinguish them.

## 1. What Stabilisation Is For

### 1.1 The purpose

Stabilisation has four purposes, and a student should be able to state all four.

**To widen the window of tolerance.** A young person who is constantly hyperaroused or constantly shut down cannot process information, and the widening of the window is a precondition for everything else.

**To build the capacity to remain present.** Dissociation, avoidance, and the dorsal vagal shutdown are the young person's solutions to an intolerable state, and the solutions have to be replaced with something less costly before the original state can be addressed.

**To establish the working relationship.** A young person with a developmental trauma history will not be able to trust a practitioner until the practitioner has demonstrated that they can be relied upon to be present, to be predictable, and to be controllable — and the only demonstration that works is the demonstrated over time.

**To restore the young person's ordinary functioning.** School attendance, sleep, eating, and the relationships are the things that are immediately at stake, and the restoration of them is a legitimate goal in its own right and is frequently the thing the young person most wants.

### 1.2 What stabilisation is not

**It is not the trauma processing.** A young person can be perfectly stabilised and still have not begun to process anything, and the work must not be confused with the work.

**It is not supportive counselling.** The supportive conversation is unstructured, is about reassurance, and is of undetermined duration, and it does not build the specific capacities that the processing work requires. It is valuable and it is not the same thing.

**It is not symptom suppression.** The aim is not to make the symptoms go away; the aim is to give the young person a set of skills that they can use when the symptoms arise, so that the symptoms are no longer the only option.

**It is not a prelude that can be skipped.** A practitioner who intends to do a trauma-focused intervention and does not stabilise first will produce a worse outcome than a practitioner who never intended to do one.

### 1.3 The evidence for the stabilisation work

The evidence base here is more limited than the evidence base for the specific trauma-focused protocols, and a student should be able to describe it accurately.

**There is good evidence for the general principle** that the reduction of arousal precedes the cognitive work, that the reduction is achieved through the body, and that a young person who is dysregulated cannot use the cognitive and the narrative techniques. This principle is well supported across the psychophysiology and the clinical literature.

**The evidence for the specific individual skills is thinner and more recent.** The systematic reviews of the grounding, the stabilisation, and the self-regulation skills in the treatment of trauma report positive effects on the symptom measures and on the distress, with the most consistent findings for the paced breathing, the grounding through the senses, and the orienting.

**The evidence for the phase-oriented model as a package is thin**, and the most useful empirical support for it is indirect: the trials of the specific trauma-focused protocols with children, in which the protocols that include a substantial preparation phase do at least as well as those that do not, and the trials of the treatments that are *only* stabilisation, which show meaningful improvements in functioning and in the symptoms without any processing.

**The clinical consensus is strong** even where the evidence is thin, and this is one of the cases in the field in which the clinical consensus is better established than the empirical support, and a student should be able to say so.

## 2. The Core Skills

### 2.1 The orienting skill

**Orient to the here and now.** The young person is asked to name, in order, five things they can see, four they can hear, three they can touch, two they can smell, and one they can taste. The technique is derived from the standard grounding exercises and it is one of the most useful in the repertoire, because it requires no training, it produces a measurable change in the autonomic state within ninety seconds, and it is available to the young person at any moment.

**Orient to the internal.** The young person is asked to name five things they can feel within their body. This is harder, it is more useful for the young person who is dissociating, and it is the bridge to the body scan.

**Orient to the temporal.** The young person is asked, in the middle of distress, to name the day, the date, the time, the season, the weather, and their age. This is a simple and effective technique for a young person who has lost track of where they are, and it is particularly useful with a young person who is dissociating or who is having a flashback.

**The teaching.** The technique is taught in a low-arousal moment, it is practised in a low-arousal moment, it is written on a card, and the young person is taught to use it proactively as well as reactively. The proactive use — using the card when the arousal is at a four rather than a nine — is the difference between a skill that is used and a skill that is not.

### 2.2 The grounding through the senses

**The five senses as an anchor.** "Feel the chair. Feel the floor under your feet. Hear the hum of the light. See one green thing. Feel the temperature of the air on the back of your hand." The technique is taught as a script and it is the most portable of the skills, because it requires nothing and it can be used in a corridor, on a bus, in a waiting room.

**The technique is for the young person who is in the room with the practitioner**, and the practitioner should use it themselves in the room, and the practitioner should say, out loud, when they are using it, because the demonstration is part of the teaching.

**The contraindication.** For a young person whose abuse involved sensory detail, the sensory grounding can be activating rather than settling, and the practitioner should ask about this before teaching it, and the alternatives are the movement-based and the orientation-based techniques.

### 2.3 The paced breathing

**The physiological rationale.** Slow exhalation lengthens the interval between heartbeats, and the vagal tone increases, and the arousal reduces. The mechanism is straightforward and it is one of the few places in this book where a specific physiological claim can be made and used with confidence.

**The technique.** The young person is taught to breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six, or to use any other ratio in which the exhalation is longer than the inhalation. The practitioner should not over-specify: the ratio matters less than the exhalation being longer, and a young person who finds counting irritating should be given a simpler version.

**The teaching.** The technique is best taught lying down, in a low-arousal moment, with the practitioner doing it alongside the young person, and the young person is taught to notice the difference between the breathing before and after.

**The caution.** The breathing must not be used with a young person who is in the dorsal vagal state, because the deepening of the exhalation will deepen the shutdown. The breathing is for the sympathetic state; the dorsal vagal state requires orienting and movement.

### 2.4 The safe place and the container

**The safe place** is a concrete, sensory, personally meaningful location, and it is taught in detail. For a young person who finds it hard to imagine, a photograph, a place in the building, or a physical object can serve. The safe place is used as the resourcing intervention during the processing work, and it is used in the stabilisation work as a general resource.

**The container** is a different and complementary technique. The young person is asked to imagine a container — a box, a safe, a vault, a large drum — that can hold whatever they need to put away. The contents are named: the frightening images, the bad thoughts, the difficult feelings, the things that are too much. The young person then imagines placing them in the container and closing the lid. The technique is cognitively simple and it is used with young people who are ruminating or who are overwhelmed by intrusive material, and it is best taught in the drawing, with a box drawn and the contents drawn in it.

### 2.5 The body scan

**The technique.** Attention is moved through the body, from the feet to the head, with the young person reporting anything that is noticed, and nothing is interpreted. The practice is derived from the mindfulness-based body scan of Chapter 22, and the developmental adaptation for a young person with a trauma history is that the young person is in control of where the attention goes and can stop at any point.

**The two uses.** The body scan is used in the stabilisation work, as a daily practice that builds the young person's awareness of the body's signals, and it is used in the processing work, as the technique for completing the autonomic component of the memory, which is the last part to change.

**The contraindication.** The body scan is contraindicated in a young person with a history of bodily abuse, and the practitioner should ask before teaching it, and the alternative is the movement-based grounding.

### 2.6 Movement and orienting through the body

**The physical discharge.** A young person who is in a constant sympathetic state needs to move, and the movement should be large, undemanding, and not competitive: walking, running on the spot, pushing against a wall, shaking, jumping, climbing. The practitioner should be prepared to do it with the young person, and the room should be one in which this is possible.

**The orienting through movement.** The "up and down" exercise, in which the young person lies on the floor and moves their gaze up and down while a helper moves a red object, is derived from the EMDR literature and is a genuine orienting technique with a good evidence base.

**The slow, deliberate movement.** Tai chi, yoga, and structured dance have all been used in the stabilisation of young people, and the evidence base is growing, and the group format makes them practical.

### 2.7 The time travel to a safe memory

**The technique.** The young person is asked to recall a specific, real, and genuinely pleasant memory from their own life — not a fantasy, not a generic image, but a real occasion with real detail — and to place themselves in it, in the body, and to notice what they can feel now. The technique works because the recollection of a genuinely safe and pleasant experience is itself an orienting and regulating event, and because it accesses the same systems that the traumatic memory has colonised.

**The specific requirements.** The memory must be real, and it must have been checked with the young person — many young people with a difficult history have very few genuinely safe memories, and the practitioner must establish which ones they have before asking for one. The memory must be detailed. And the practice must be short, because the memory fades and the young person must not be left with the fading.

### 2.8 The interpersonal skills

Two skills are taught in the stabilisation phase because they are the skills that will keep the young person safe and are the skills that are most often missing.

**Asking for help.** The young person practises asking for help in a concrete situation, and the practice is repeated, and the response to the request is guaranteed. A young person who has never had a request met will not make one, and the point of the exercise is that the request is met, every time, in the sessions with the practitioner and in the sessions with the parents.

**Saying no.** The young person practises declining, and again the practice is repeated and the outcome is predictable. This is a skill that a young person with a trauma history frequently does not have, and it is a skill that is also a right, and a practitioner who teaches it is teaching a right as well as a skill.

### 2.9 The skill of the window of tolerance

**The traffic light.** The young person learns to rate their own arousal on a three-colour scale, and to know their own early indicators, and to know what they need at each level. At the green level, the young person is in the learning zone. At the amber level, the young person uses the grounding skills. At the red level, the young person goes to the safe place, the container, or the trusted adult, and does not attempt to process anything.

**The value of the traffic light is that it moves the responsibility for the regulation from the practitioner to the young person**, and that is the objective of the whole phase, and the practitioner who is doing the regulating for the young person has not completed the phase even if the symptoms have reduced.
