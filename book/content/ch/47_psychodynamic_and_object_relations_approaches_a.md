# Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the development of psychodynamic thinking from Freud through ego psychology, object relations, self psychology, and relational approaches.
- Explain unconscious processes, conflict, defence, transference, countertransference, and enactment in clinically usable language.
- Describe object relations concepts: internal objects, splitting, projection, projective identification, and containment.
- Apply psychodynamic formulation to adolescent identity, family relationships, school behaviour, and therapeutic ruptures.
- Distinguish evidence-informed psychodynamic practice from unfalsifiable interpretation.
- Recognise cultural, ethical, and safeguarding limits.

## Chapter Overview

Psychodynamic approaches ask how earlier relationships, conflicted wishes and fears, defences, and unconscious patterns continue to organise present experience. Their language can appear remote from school corridors and family kitchens, yet their central observations are familiar. A student experiences a neutral teacher as contemptuous. A parent reacts to a fifteen-year-old as if she were still seven—or as if she were the parent's own critical mother. A counsellor feels an inexplicable urge to rescue, punish, withdraw, or become unusually helpless. Psychodynamic practice slows these moments down and asks what relational pattern is being repeated, avoided, or communicated.

## 1. Historical Development

Freud's work introduced the ideas that mental life includes unconscious processes, that symptoms may express conflict, that childhood experience matters, and that the therapeutic relationship itself becomes a field in which old patterns appear. Many specific Freudian claims have been revised or rejected, but the clinical attention to hidden meaning, ambivalence, defence, and repetition remains influential.

Ego psychology emphasised adaptation, defence analysis, and strengths of the ego. Object relations theorists—including Klein, Fairbairn, and Winnicott—shifted attention toward internalised relationships: the self in relation to “objects”, meaning significant others as represented in the mind. Self psychology, associated with Kohut, emphasised empathy, self-cohesion, and needs for mirroring, idealisation, and belonging. Relational and intersubjective approaches later stressed that therapy is co-created by two subjectivities rather than observed from outside by a neutral analyst.

Adolescent psychodynamic work must be developmentally flexible. Long silences, free association, and couch-based adult psychoanalysis are not automatically appropriate. Young people often need activity, clarity, family involvement, school liaison, humour, and explicit safety structures.

## 2. Core Concepts

### 2.1 Unconscious processes

Unconscious does not mean a mystical basement of forbidden content. In contemporary practice it includes processes outside awareness: automatic expectations, disowned feelings, procedural relational patterns, avoided memories, and motives that conflict with the person's self-image.

A student may consciously want to succeed yet repeatedly sabotage examinations because success feels disloyal to a struggling parent, exposes him to envy, or threatens an identity built around not caring. The counsellor does not announce this as fact. It becomes a tentative formulation tested against history, affect, repetition, and the young person's response.

### 2.2 Conflict and ambivalence

People can love and resent the same person, want independence and fear abandonment, desire success and dread visibility. Adolescence intensifies ambivalence because dependence and autonomy coexist.

Symptoms may compromise between wishes and prohibitions. School refusal can express fear, protect a depressed parent from loneliness, avoid humiliation, and punish controlling adults—all at once. A single-cause formulation may therefore be too thin.

### 2.3 Defences

Defences are psychological strategies that reduce awareness of painful feelings or conflict. They are adaptive in origin and costly when rigid.

- **Denial:** refusing to register reality.
- **Projection:** attributing one's disowned feeling to another.
- **Splitting:** experiencing self or others as all good or all bad.
- **Displacement:** redirecting feeling from a dangerous target to a safer one.
- **Intellectualisation:** discussing feeling abstractly to avoid experiencing it.
- **Humour:** transforming pain into shared perspective; it may be mature or avoidant depending on use.
- **Acting out:** communicating through action what cannot yet be thought or spoken.

The counsellor should describe defences compassionately. A defence is protection before it is pathology.

### 2.4 Transference and countertransference

Transference refers to the activation in therapy of expectations and feelings derived from earlier relationships. An adolescent may test whether the counsellor will become disgusted, intrusive, bored, rescuing, or abandoning.

Countertransference includes the counsellor's emotional responses, shaped both by the young person's interpersonal pull and by the counsellor's own history. It is data, not a command. Feeling bored may signal dissociation, hopelessness, practitioner fatigue, or an unspoken mismatch. Supervision prevents enactment.

### 2.5 Enactment and repetition

When patterns cannot be verbalised, they may be enacted. The counsellor is drawn into the role of critical parent, helpless child, rescuer, rival, or absent adult. Recognising enactment allows repair: “I notice I've started sounding like I'm cross-examining you. That may be exactly what happens at home. Let's pause.”

## 3. Object Relations and Internal Objects

An internal object is not a literal person inside the mind. It is an affect-laden representation of self in relation to another. A child repeatedly comforted develops an expectation that distress can be brought to someone and survived. A child repeatedly shamed may internalise a contemptuous other and a defective self.

These templates influence perception. Ambiguous teacher feedback is heard through the internal critic. A friend's silence activates an abandoning other. Therapy offers a new relational experience, but one relationship does not erase a template; repetition, reflection, family work, and real-world relationships all contribute.

Splitting may dominate under stress: one teacher is wonderful, another evil; mother is perfect until she sets a limit, then wholly cruel. Development involves integrating ambivalence—recognising that the same person can be loved, disappointing, safe, and limited.

## 4. Containment and Mentalization Links

Bion's concept of containment describes a caregiver receiving a child's unbearable state, tolerating and transforming it, and returning it in more manageable form. The parent who can hear panic without panicking or punishing helps the child learn that feeling can be thought about.

Psychodynamic adolescent practice often works through containment before interpretation. The counsellor remains present with rage, shame, or despair without retaliating, collapsing, or rushing to cheer up. Over time the young person internalises a more reflective relationship to their own states. This links psychodynamic work to attachment and mentalization approaches in Chapters 34 and 42.
