
## 6. Psychodynamic Assessment and Formulation

A psychodynamic formulation is a structured hypothesis, not a pronouncement. It may include:

1. **Presenting pattern:** What repeats across settings and relationships?
2. **Developmental history:** Early care, losses, separations, trauma, illness, school transitions, and strengths.
3. **Internal world:** Representations of self and others; dominant affects; feared and wished-for relationships.
4. **Conflicts:** Autonomy versus loyalty, anger versus need, success versus belonging, sexuality versus shame, dependence versus contempt.
5. **Defences:** How painful states are avoided or communicated.
6. **Transference possibilities:** What role may the counsellor be assigned?
7. **Family and social context:** What present realities sustain the pattern?
8. **Capacity and risk:** Reflection, affect tolerance, support, self-harm, safeguarding, and ability to use therapy.

The formulation should be revisable. If every response confirms the theory—agreement means insight and disagreement means resistance—the theory has become unfalsifiable and clinically dangerous.

## 7. Technique with Adolescents

### 7.1 Frame and reliability

Time, place, confidentiality, cancellations, digital contact, and endings carry meaning. Reliability is treatment, especially for young people whose adults have been unpredictable. Changes should be explained and repaired.

### 7.2 Play, activity, and metaphor

Younger adolescents may think through drawing, music, games, stories, sport, or shared tasks. The counsellor follows meaning without forcing symbolic interpretation. A drawing of a locked house may be a house, a memory, a wish for privacy, or all three.

### 7.3 Clarification before interpretation

Ask what happened, what was felt, what was feared, and what was expected. Interpretation, when used, should be tentative, brief, close to experience, and timed when affect is present but manageable. “Perhaps when your tutor went quiet, it felt like the criticism you brace for at home” is preferable to a grand claim about the unconscious.

### 7.4 Working with defences

Do not strip defences faster than alternatives develop. Intellectualisation may allow a young person to attend sessions at all. Humour may preserve dignity. Denial may be temporarily necessary after loss. The counsellor names cost and function: “Joking helps you stay in the room; it also means no one knows when you're actually frightened.”

### 7.5 Rupture and repair

Missed appointments, silence, anger at the counsellor, and disappointment are material. Repair requires acknowledgement, curiosity, and sometimes apology. A counsellor who can survive anger without retaliation offers a powerful corrective experience.

## 8. Case Illustration: The Boy Who Made Teachers Give Up

**Presentation.** Yusuf, fifteen, is described as bright but sabotaging. He begins coursework enthusiastically, then stops near completion. Teachers initially invest heavily, become frustrated, and withdraw. Yusuf says he does not care.

**History and pattern.** His father, a high-achieving professional, left the family after repeated conflict and now offers intense praise during brief visits followed by long silence. Yusuf longs for his father's recognition and protects himself by deciding in advance that effort is pointless. Teachers repeat the cycle: special encouragement, disappointment, withdrawal. Yusuf interprets withdrawal as proof that adults value performance, not him.

**Transference.** In counselling, Yusuf tests the frame. He misses a session after a particularly open conversation. The counsellor feels irritated and then unusually inclined to send a long reassuring message. In supervision, this is understood as a pull to become the idealising rescuer who will later feel betrayed.

**Intervention.** The counsellor maintains boundaries, acknowledges the missed session without punishment, and explores sequence rather than motive. Yusuf can say, “When I tell people things, they get weird.” The counsellor responds, “Perhaps leaving first felt safer than waiting to see whether I would become less interested.” Yusuf is quiet, then says, “That's what Dad does—goes quiet.”

Work includes practical study structure, teacher consultation to provide steady rather than inflated feedback, and a family session in which his mother stops mediating all contact with his father. Yusuf writes but does not send several letters, distinguishing expression from action.

**Outcome.** Coursework completion improves unevenly. The more important shift is relational: Yusuf can tell a teacher, “I need feedback before the final week or I shut down,” instead of disappearing. Psychodynamic formulation did not replace practical planning; it explained why planning collapsed at the point of anticipated judgement.

## 9. Evidence and Limitations

Psychodynamic therapies have evidence for several adult conditions and growing evidence for children and adolescents, including short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy for adolescent depression in some trials and service evaluations. Effects depend on condition, severity, treatment length, and comparison care. Evidence is less strong where immediate behavioural control, skills acquisition, family contingency change, or crisis stabilisation is the urgent need.

The approach's concepts vary in empirical status. Attachment and defence processes have research support; broad claims about unconscious causation are harder to test. Good practice uses psychodynamic thinking as formulation and relationship discipline, not as licence to override the young person's account.

Duration and access are concerns. Open-ended therapy may be unavailable or unnecessary. Brief psychodynamic models, mentalization-based treatment, and integration with family and school work improve applicability.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

Psychodynamic theories emerged largely from European and North American clinical contexts. Concepts of self, family obligation, emotional expression, sexuality, and authority are culturally shaped. A counsellor must not interpret collectivist loyalty as enmeshment, religiously grounded moral commitments as mere defence, or justified mistrust of institutions as projection.

Interpretation is an exercise of power. The practitioner should invite correction, use tentative language, and avoid claiming privileged access to the young person's true motives. Safeguarding information must not be reframed as fantasy or transference. Where abuse, coercion, or discrimination is occurring, present reality takes precedence.

Confidentiality and parental involvement require developmental judgement. Parents may need guidance on patterns without receiving session content. Endings should be planned carefully where abandonment themes are central; abrupt service termination can repeat the very pattern under treatment.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Defence** — A psychological strategy that reduces awareness of painful affect or conflict.
- **Transference** — Activation in therapy of relational expectations derived from earlier experience.
- **Countertransference** — The practitioner's emotional responses within the therapeutic relationship, used reflectively rather than acted upon.
- **Internal object** — An affect-laden mental representation of self in relation to a significant other.
- **Splitting** — Experiencing self or others in all-good and all-bad terms under stress.
- **Projective identification** — A process in which disowned states are evoked in another person, who may be pressured to enact them.
- **Containment** — Receiving and transforming unbearable affect so it can be returned in thinkable form.
- **Enactment** — Repetition of a relational pattern in action before it can be verbalised.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Psychodynamic and object relations approaches teach counsellors to listen for repetition, ambivalence, defence, and the relational meanings of behaviour. With adolescents, their value lies not in remote interpretation but in disciplined curiosity: What pattern is recurring? What feeling is being protected against? What role is the counsellor being invited to play? What new experience might make the pattern thinkable and therefore changeable?

## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is the relational substrate of personality, transference, and developmental lines of interpretation. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is development, agency, identity, culture, relationships, motivation, and ecological resources. The mechanism to keep in view is how beliefs, social models, environments, power, belonging, and developmental timing interact to support or restrict change. The characteristic professional risk is individualising structural harm, stereotyping culture, or demanding resilience while leaving unsafe conditions unchanged. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in schools, families, peer contexts, community services, and culturally responsive practice, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Ines is fifteen and is seen in a school counselling service for shame after academic failure. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Ines's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Ines explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on development, agency, identity, culture, relationships, motivation, and ecological resources.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Ines chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Ines has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Ines can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Psychodynamic and Object Relations Approaches to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
