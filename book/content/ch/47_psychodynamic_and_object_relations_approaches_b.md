
## 6. Psychodynamic Assessment and Formulation

A psychodynamic formulation is a structured hypothesis, not a pronouncement. It may include:

1. **Presenting pattern:** What repeats across settings and relationships?
2. **Developmental history:** Early care, losses, separations, trauma, illness, school transitions, and strengths.
3. **Internal world:** Representations of self and others; dominant affects; feared and wished-for relationships.
4. **Conflicts:** Autonomy versus loyalty, anger versus need, success versus belonging, sexuality versus shame, dependence versus contempt.
5. **Defences:** How painful states are avoided or communicated.
6. **Transference possibilities:** What role may the counsellor be assigned?
7. **Family and social context:** What present realities sustain the pattern?
8. **Capacity and risk:** Reflection, affect tolerance, support, self-harm, safeguarding, and ability to use therapy.

The formulation should be revisable. If every response confirms the theory—agreement means insight and disagreement means resistance—the theory has become unfalsifiable and clinically dangerous.

## 7. Technique with Adolescents

### 7.1 Frame and reliability

Time, place, confidentiality, cancellations, digital contact, and endings carry meaning. Reliability is treatment, especially for young people whose adults have been unpredictable. Changes should be explained and repaired.

### 7.2 Play, activity, and metaphor

Younger adolescents may think through drawing, music, games, stories, sport, or shared tasks. The counsellor follows meaning without forcing symbolic interpretation. A drawing of a locked house may be a house, a memory, a wish for privacy, or all three.

### 7.3 Clarification before interpretation

Ask what happened, what was felt, what was feared, and what was expected. Interpretation, when used, should be tentative, brief, close to experience, and timed when affect is present but manageable. “Perhaps when your tutor went quiet, it felt like the criticism you brace for at home” is preferable to a grand claim about the unconscious.

### 7.4 Working with defences

Do not strip defences faster than alternatives develop. Intellectualisation may allow a young person to attend sessions at all. Humour may preserve dignity. Denial may be temporarily necessary after loss. The counsellor names cost and function: “Joking helps you stay in the room; it also means no one knows when you're actually frightened.”

### 7.5 Rupture and repair

Missed appointments, silence, anger at the counsellor, and disappointment are material. Repair requires acknowledgement, curiosity, and sometimes apology. A counsellor who can survive anger without retaliation offers a powerful corrective experience.

## 8. Case Illustration: The Boy Who Made Teachers Give Up

**Presentation.** Yusuf, fifteen, is described as bright but sabotaging. He begins coursework enthusiastically, then stops near completion. Teachers initially invest heavily, become frustrated, and withdraw. Yusuf says he does not care.

**History and pattern.** His father, a high-achieving professional, left the family after repeated conflict and now offers intense praise during brief visits followed by long silence. Yusuf longs for his father's recognition and protects himself by deciding in advance that effort is pointless. Teachers repeat the cycle: special encouragement, disappointment, withdrawal. Yusuf interprets withdrawal as proof that adults value performance, not him.

**Transference.** In counselling, Yusuf tests the frame. He misses a session after a particularly open conversation. The counsellor feels irritated and then unusually inclined to send a long reassuring message. In supervision, this is understood as a pull to become the idealising rescuer who will later feel betrayed.

**Intervention.** The counsellor maintains boundaries, acknowledges the missed session without punishment, and explores sequence rather than motive. Yusuf can say, “When I tell people things, they get weird.” The counsellor responds, “Perhaps leaving first felt safer than waiting to see whether I would become less interested.” Yusuf is quiet, then says, “That's what Dad does—goes quiet.”

Work includes practical study structure, teacher consultation to provide steady rather than inflated feedback, and a family session in which his mother stops mediating all contact with his father. Yusuf writes but does not send several letters, distinguishing expression from action.

**Outcome.** Coursework completion improves unevenly. The more important shift is relational: Yusuf can tell a teacher, “I need feedback before the final week or I shut down,” instead of disappearing. Psychodynamic formulation did not replace practical planning; it explained why planning collapsed at the point of anticipated judgement.

## 9. Evidence and Limitations

Psychodynamic therapies have evidence for several adult conditions and growing evidence for children and adolescents, including short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy for adolescent depression in some trials and service evaluations. Effects depend on condition, severity, treatment length, and comparison care. Evidence is less strong where immediate behavioural control, skills acquisition, family contingency change, or crisis stabilisation is the urgent need.

The approach's concepts vary in empirical status. Attachment and defence processes have research support; broad claims about unconscious causation are harder to test. Good practice uses psychodynamic thinking as formulation and relationship discipline, not as licence to override the young person's account.

Duration and access are concerns. Open-ended therapy may be unavailable or unnecessary. Brief psychodynamic models, mentalization-based treatment, and integration with family and school work improve applicability.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

Psychodynamic theories emerged largely from European and North American clinical contexts. Concepts of self, family obligation, emotional expression, sexuality, and authority are culturally shaped. A counsellor must not interpret collectivist loyalty as enmeshment, religiously grounded moral commitments as mere defence, or justified mistrust of institutions as projection.

Interpretation is an exercise of power. The practitioner should invite correction, use tentative language, and avoid claiming privileged access to the young person's true motives. Safeguarding information must not be reframed as fantasy or transference. Where abuse, coercion, or discrimination is occurring, present reality takes precedence.

Confidentiality and parental involvement require developmental judgement. Parents may need guidance on patterns without receiving session content. Endings should be planned carefully where abandonment themes are central; abrupt service termination can repeat the very pattern under treatment.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Defence** — A psychological strategy that reduces awareness of painful affect or conflict.
- **Transference** — Activation in therapy of relational expectations derived from earlier experience.
- **Countertransference** — The practitioner's emotional responses within the therapeutic relationship, used reflectively rather than acted upon.
- **Internal object** — An affect-laden mental representation of self in relation to a significant other.
- **Splitting** — Experiencing self or others in all-good and all-bad terms under stress.
- **Projective identification** — A process in which disowned states are evoked in another person, who may be pressured to enact them.
- **Containment** — Receiving and transforming unbearable affect so it can be returned in thinkable form.
- **Enactment** — Repetition of a relational pattern in action before it can be verbalised.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Psychodynamic and object relations approaches teach counsellors to listen for repetition, ambivalence, defence, and the relational meanings of behaviour. With adolescents, their value lies not in remote interpretation but in disciplined curiosity: What pattern is recurring? What feeling is being protected against? What role is the counsellor being invited to play? What new experience might make the pattern thinkable and therefore changeable?
