# Case Conceptualization and Treatment Planning

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Distinguish description, diagnosis, formulation, and treatment plan.
- Construct a developmental, cultural, relational, and evidence-informed case conceptualization.
- Use predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective, and presenting factors.
- Translate formulation into goals, interventions, sequencing, responsibilities, and review criteria.
- Write SMART but humane goals with adolescents and families.
- Identify risk, safeguarding, medical, educational, and social needs within a plan.
- Revise formulation in response to outcome data and new information.

## Chapter Overview

A diagnosis can name a pattern; it cannot by itself explain why this young person, in this family, in this school, at this time, is struggling in this way. Case conceptualization—also called formulation—is the bridge between assessment and treatment. It organises information into a testable explanation and identifies where change is possible.

![The five-part formulation: presenting issues, predisposing factors, precipitating events, perpetuating mechanisms, and protective factors.](fig80_five_part_formulation.png)

A good formulation is neither a biography nor a list of deficits. It is a concise, collaborative hypothesis that can be shared in developmentally appropriate language: what happened, what it meant, what keeps the problem going, what strengths exist, and what we will try first.

## 1. Description, Diagnosis, and Formulation

**Description** records what is happening: symptoms, behaviour, context, frequency, duration, severity, and impact.

**Diagnosis** classifies patterns according to recognised criteria. It can guide evidence, communication, eligibility, and prognosis, but may also stigmatise or obscure context.

**Formulation** explains development and maintenance for an individual in context. It includes strengths and systems as well as symptoms.

**Treatment plan** specifies goals, interventions, people, timeframe, measures, and review.

Confusing these levels leads to poor practice. “She avoids school because she has anxiety” is circular if anxiety is inferred only from avoidance. A formulation might instead identify panic sensations, catastrophic appraisal, avoidance relief, parental accommodation, bullying, sleep disturbance, and academic gaps—each suggesting intervention.

## 2. Sources of Information

Formulation draws on interviews with the young person, parents or carers, school information, previous records, observations, standardised measures, medical information, and the young person's creative or written expression. Disagreement between informants is not noise to erase; it reveals setting-specific patterns and different perspectives.

The counsellor should distinguish observation, report, inference, and speculation in records. “Mother reports that he refuses” is a report. “He appeared tense when discussing mathematics” is observation. “He may fear humiliation” is inference. “He is manipulative” is an unhelpful character judgement unless operationalised and evidenced.

## 3. The Five-Part Framework

### Presenting factors
Current concerns, symptoms, risks, functional impact, and the reason help is sought now.

### Predisposing factors
Temperament, neurodevelopment, early relationships, prior adversity, learning history, health, family patterns, cultural context, and earlier coping.

### Precipitating factors
Recent losses, transitions, conflicts, illness, bullying, academic pressure, family changes, or developmental demands that exceeded resources.

### Perpetuating factors
Avoidance, reinforcement, accommodation, insomnia, substance use, rumination, conflict cycles, inaccessible teaching, untreated pain, discrimination, and service fragmentation.

### Protective factors
Relationships, skills, interests, cultural and community resources, school belonging, prior successes, family commitment, practical resources, and hope.

The model becomes powerful when arrows are drawn between parts. A precipitant matters because of predisposition; a perpetuating loop explains why distress persists after the precipitant ends.

## 4. Levels of Formulation

A comprehensive adolescent formulation considers:

- biological and physical health;
- cognition, emotion, behaviour, and identity;
- attachment and family interaction;
- peers, school, and digital contexts;
- culture, language, discrimination, and community;
- material conditions and service systems;
- development and time.

Not every level requires intervention. The art lies in choosing leverage points: factors that are influential, changeable, acceptable to the client, and within available competence.

## 5. Collaborative Formulation

Young people should recognise themselves in the formulation without feeling reduced by it. Use their words where possible. Draw diagrams together. Offer uncertainty: “This is our best current map, not a final truth. What fits? What is missing? What feels wrong?”

Parents may need a parallel version focused on interactions and support, without disclosing confidential adolescent material. Schools need a functional, privacy-respecting summary focused on responses and adjustments.

A formulation that cannot be communicated is unfinished.
