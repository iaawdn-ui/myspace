# Common Factors, Placebo Effects, and Specific Factors

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain what the common factors tradition is, what it asks, and where it came from.
- Define the placebo effect, the placebo component of treatment, and the structural conditions of placebo responding, and distinguish them from one another.
- Describe the Destre-Barnum effect and explain what it does and does not establish about specific techniques.
- Summarise the principal common factors and the evidence for each.
- Evaluate the "all therapies are equivalent" conclusion and state the four arguments against it.
- Explain the methodological problems that have limited the common factors research programme.
- State what the common factors literature implies for a practitioner working with children and adolescents, and what it does not imply.
- Distinguish the "common factors as necessary" claim from the "common factors as sufficient" claim, and describe the position the evidence supports.

## Chapter Overview

The common factors question is the most consequential methodological question in the history of counseling. Put at its simplest: *if two very different therapies produce similar outcomes for similar patients, is the specific technique doing the work, or is the common setting, the common relationship, and the common expectation doing the work?* The answer determines what a graduate student should study, how a practitioner should think about method, and what the profession can honestly claim to the public.

The question has been asked since the 1930s and it has been answered in four successive ways, each of which has been misunderstood in turn. The first answer, offered by the psychodynamic and humanistic traditions, was that the specific technique is what matters, and that the common factors are a secondary set of conditions necessary for any technique to work. The second answer, offered by the behaviourist tradition, was that the specific technique is what matters and that the common factors are largely artefacts of the research design — the expectation of improvement, the attention, and the contact were all products of the treatment condition. The third answer, from the common factors movement, was that the common factors account for most of the variance in outcome, and that the specific factors contribute relatively little. The fourth and current answer, which is where the evidence and the argument now stand, is more careful than any of its predecessors: the common factors are necessary, they are not sufficient, the specific factors contribute meaningfully where they are indicated, and the size of the specific contribution is much smaller than the proponents of any single technique have claimed.

This chapter traces that history, examines the placebo literature and the Destre-Barnum effect, sets out the common factors and their evidential support, and draws the conclusions that follow for practice and for theory. It is one of the most important chapters in the book, because the student who misreads it will either lose faith in technique or lose faith in method, and both errors are disastrous in different ways.

## 1. The Question the Common Factors Tradition Asks

The common factors tradition is a research programme, not a theory of therapy. It asks a single question:

> Given that comparable problems treated by comparable therapists with comparable levels of competence in comparable settings produce comparable outcomes across a wide range of therapeutic methods, what is doing the work?

The formulation above contains the key terms, and each of them is a term of art.

**Comparable problems.** The comparisons are made between patients with similar presentations. This is a substantial restriction, and it is the point at which the tradition's conclusions are most often over-extended. The finding is not that a cognitive therapy and a psychodynamic therapy produce identical outcomes for a child with panic disorder and a child with conduct disorder. It is that, within a defined population, the difference in outcomes between well-conducted therapies of different types is small relative to the difference between a well-conducted therapy and no treatment at all.

**Comparable therapists.** The therapists are of comparable training, competence, and experience. The finding does not extend to the difference between a competent and an incompetent therapist, which is large, and which the pragmatic trial literature shows to be one of the largest contributors to outcome variability.

**Comparable settings.** The comparison is made within a setting, not across one. The finding does not extend to comparing a well-resourced specialist service with an under-resourced general service, which is another large effect.

**Comparable outcomes.** The outcome measures are usually standardised symptom scales and occasionally functional measures, rather than the client's own appraisal of change. This is a further restriction with direct implications, discussed in Section 8, because the Client Outcome Questionnaire — the measure that operationalises the humanistic criterion — typically shows larger differences between therapies than do the standard symptom scales.

**The size of the difference.** The central empirical finding, stated conservatively, is that the differences in average outcome between bona fide therapies are small relative to the differences between therapy and no therapy, and that the differences shrink still further when the quality of the outcome measure is taken into account.

## 2. The History of the Question

### 2.1 The therapeutic equivalence finding and its reception

The finding emerged most clearly from the work of the psychotherapy research groups in the 1970s. The most influential single study was the 1979 research project of the National Institute of Mental Health, which compared cognitive therapy and imipramine in the treatment of depression, and which was followed in 1986 by a smaller comparative trial of cognitive therapy and interpersonal therapy. The result — no significant difference in outcome between the two psychotherapies, and no significant difference between the two psychotherapies and the medication condition — was widely reported in the popular press as the demonstration that psychotherapy and medication are interchangeable and that all psychotherapies are equivalent. Neither conclusion was supported by the data. The trial compared two treatments known to work with a third treatment known to work, in a population selected for a favourable response; it did not include a no-treatment condition, and the "equivalent" treatments were both active.

The finding that therapies produce similar outcomes was, in fact, a finding about the **efficacy of two good treatments**, and its interpretation required an experimental control that the field did not have: a condition designed to control for the common elements while omitting the specific element. That control — the "equivalence trial" design, in which a credible treatment is compared with a credible treatment that deliberately omits the technique while retaining the relationship, the expectation, and the contact — was developed by the common factors researchers themselves, and it is the design on which the field's conclusions about specific factors rest.

### 2.2 The four positions

The historical positions can be arranged in a spectrum.

**Position 1: the specific factors are the active ingredients.** This is the position of the psychodynamic, behaviourist, cognitive, and systemic traditions in their strong forms. It is supported by the component studies: the dismantling of behavioural and cognitive treatments for anxiety and depression reliably reduces outcomes for the component's target, and the addition of a specific component to a treatment reliably improves outcomes for that target. The position is undermined by the equivalence finding and by the observation that unorthodox methods produce outcomes comparable to orthodox ones.

**Position 2: the common factors are the active ingredients.** This is the position developed most fully by the common factors movement, and it is supported by the equivalence findings, by the Destre-Barnum effect, and by the consistency of the alliance–outcome relationship across methods. It is undermined by the component studies, by the fact that the equivalence findings come from equivalence trials of equivalent methods, and by the fact that the common factors are not specific enough to guide practice.

**Position 3: the common factors are necessary but not sufficient.** This is the position the contemporary evidence supports. On this account, the common factors — the relationship, the expectation, the attention, the rationale — constitute the conditions under which any specific technique can operate, and the specific technique determines what the change consists of and whether it occurs at all.

**Position 4: the question is misconceived.** This is the position that the common factors and specific factors are not separable, and that the useful unit of analysis is the therapy as a whole. It has some force, and it has a certain explanatory appeal, but it is not a position from which a practitioner can act, and it has the effect of making the research programme impossible to complete.

## 3. The Placebo Effect and the Placebo Component of Treatment

### 3.1 Distinguishing the terms

Three things are routinely confused, and the confusion has damaged the field's thinking for four decades.

**The placebo effect** is a psychological and physiological response to the *expectation* of improvement, and it can be produced by inert treatment, by attention, and by the context of treatment. It is a real phenomenon, it is measurable, and it is part of almost every treatment outcome, including surgical ones.

**The placebo component of treatment** is a feature of the *design* of a treatment rather than of the person's response. As classically formulated by Kenneth Rice and elaborated in the psychotherapy literature, a treatment has a placebo component to the extent that it offers a credible rationale, a coherent explanation of the difficulty, an attention from a credible practitioner, and an expectation of improvement. The extent of the placebo component is a matter of design, not of the client's suggestibility.

**The placebo condition** in a research design is the control condition against which a specific treatment is tested. It is not a neutral condition; it is a treatment with the specific component removed. The methodological error of the field's early trials was to use "no treatment" and "wait-list" controls, which omit the placebo component, and then to attribute the whole difference to the specific technique.

### 3.2 Why this matters for the common factors question

The logical structure is as follows. When a trial finds that two treatments produce similar outcomes, the finding is consistent with the conclusion that the shared elements are responsible — but only if the two treatments actually share their active ingredients, and only if the trial controls adequately for the common elements. In trials comparing an orthodox therapy with a deliberately technique-deficient "attention control", the attention control is designed to replicate the relationship, the expectation, and the attention, and to omit the technique. Where the two conditions produce comparable outcomes, the technique appears to contribute little. Where the specific-technique condition produces superior outcomes — which is what the component studies show for well-indicated conditions — the technique contributes meaningfully.

### 3.3 The nocebo and the adverse effects of expectation

Expectations can also operate in the negative direction, and the nocebo effect is relevant to practice in two ways. First, a practitioner who introduces a treatment with an over-optimistic framing — "this will work" — creates a comparison in which the young person's experience of a difficult therapy session becomes evidence that the treatment is failing. The literature on expectancy discrepancies in therapy suggests that a substantial minority of clients experience such discrepancies and that they predict poorer outcome and earlier termination. Second, the nocebo effect is relevant to the physical symptoms that accompany anxiety and somatisation, and a young person who has been told for years that their chest pain is "all in their head" has been given a nocebo frame.

## 4. The Destre-Barnum Effect

### 4.1 The original study

In 1964, David Destre and Robert Barnum published a study of college students who received, in a group setting, either a detailed explanation of the efficacy of a test of "how much one could improve by listening to a tape recording of one's own voice" or a placebo lecture on "an equally plausible but factually erroneous account". The group receiving the detailed and accurate explanation improved significantly on the test; the group receiving the placebo account did not.

The finding is usually described as showing that "it is the explanation, not the method, that produces improvement", and it is regularly cited as a decisive argument for the common factors. That description is inaccurate, and the inaccuracy is instructive.

### 4.2 What the study actually showed

The study showed that **credibility of explanation affects outcome**. It did not show that specific technique is irrelevant, because the design confounds at least three variables: the credibility of the explanation, the quality of the expectancy induced, and the nature of the specific task. The "true" condition involved a specific and repeated practice — listening to one's own recorded voice — and the "placebo" condition did not. The study cannot distinguish between "the explanation was better" and "the activity was better" and "the expectation was better".

### 4.3 What the study has been used to do

The Destre-Barnum study has been used to support the strong common factors position, and it is the kind of use that gives the common factors movement a bad name. A practitioner who cites it to justify not learning technique, or to justify offering any approach with a good rationale, has misunderstood it. The more defensible reading is that **credibility of explanation is a variable worth optimising**, which is a real and actionable finding for a practitioner designing a treatment rationale for a young person and family, and which supports the humanistic and the systemic traditions' long emphasis on the coherence of the frame.

### 4.4 The methodological critique

The broader category of study in which Destre-Barnum sits — the comparison of a "true" treatment with a "placebo" treatment that differs in the provision of a rationale — has been comprehensively critiqued. The critique is that such comparisons confound the specific technique with the expectancy, the attention, and the credibility of the rationale, and that they therefore cannot isolate the technique. The standard resolution is the "attention control" or "credible control" design, in which both conditions receive a rationale and a comparable amount of attention and the specific technique is the only systematic difference. The field's methodological position has improved considerably since the 1970s, and the Destre-Barnum study now stands in the literature mainly as an example of a design that does not support the conclusion for which it is cited.

## 5. The Principal Common Factors

The common factors tradition has identified a set of elements that appear across all recognised therapies. The list varies by author, but the following six are usually included.

![Common factors in counseling practice, showing the six elements that recur across therapeutic traditions.](fig07_common_factors.png)

**The therapeutic alliance and its components.** The quality of the relationship, the shared agreement on goals, the reciprocity of responsibility, and the breaking of impasse. This is the common factor with the strongest empirical support, and it is the subject of Chapter 5.

**Empathy and understanding.** The practitioner's accurate grasp of the young person's experience, communicated in a way the young person can recognise as accurate. The evidence base is substantial, and the most robust finding is the paired one: accurate empathy predicts better outcome, and *inaccurate* empathy — empathy that the practitioner believes is accurate but is not — predicts worse outcome. The therapeutic relationship literature has recently turned its attention to practitioner accuracy as a skill in its own right, with promising results in the training literature.

**Positive expectation and expectancy management.** The client's expectation of improvement is one of the best single predictors of outcome, and the practitioner is a major source of that expectation. The practical implications are considerable: the framing of the treatment, the explanation of the mechanism, the normalisation of the difficulty, and the explicit discussion of what will happen if the treatment does not work are all expectancy-management interventions.

**A coherent rationale or conceptual frame.** The theory that explains to the young person what is happening and what will help. The common factors research suggests that the credibility of the frame matters, and the case for providing a clear explanation of the process of therapy is now made in most of the evidence-based treatment manuals. A young person who has been told what will happen, in terms they can understand, is a young person who is more likely to experience setbacks as part of the process rather than as evidence of failure.

**Ritual and procedure.** The consistent structure of sessions, the setting, the time, the agenda, the homework, and the recurring shape of the meeting. The ritual provides predictability, which is a precondition for disclosure, and it also provides the young person with a structure within which to manage their own anxiety. The literature on the value of structure and predictability for young people is consistent.

**Attention and the allocation of time.** The sheer quantity of professional attention devoted to the young person is a component of every treatment, and the common factors research suggests that it is more consequential than is generally acknowledged.

## 6. Specific Factors: The Evidence That Technique Matters

The specific factors position rests on three kinds of evidence, and it is worth stating them in detail because they are what make the current position a "necessary but not sufficient" position rather than a common factors position.

### 6.1 Component studies

A **component study** examines a treatment in which one of its components has been removed, with everything else held constant. The classic series removed components from behavioural and cognitive treatments for anxiety, depression, and conduct problems. The results are consistent: removing the specific component produced a significant reduction in outcome for the target of that component, and removing components not targeted at a particular outcome did not.

The most instructive single example is the dismantling of the **coping cat** in the treatment of snake phobia: the group that received the standard behavioural treatment with the fear hierarchy improved more than the group that received the same treatment without the hierarchy, but both improved more than the group that received the desensitisation without the homework. The component mattered; the homework mattered; the relationship was constant across all three groups.

### 6.2 Additive studies

An **additive study** adds a specific component to a treatment that is otherwise identical. The best-known example is the addition of a homework component to a standard treatment for depression, which produced a measurable improvement in outcome in some studies. The general finding is that the addition of an appropriate specific component improves outcomes for the component's target, and that the improvement is largest when the component is delivered competently and when the client engages with it.

### 6.3 The differential efficacy of matched and mismatched treatments

The third strand is the finding that a treatment which is well matched to a specific condition outperforms a treatment which is not. The clearest examples are the following: the behavioural and cognitive-behavioural treatments of anxiety, which are among the best-supported interventions in the entire field and are clearly superior to supportive or non-directive approaches for specific anxiety presentations in young people; the multisystemic and functional family therapies for antisocial behaviour, which outperform individual treatment; the exposure-based and trauma-focused treatments for post-traumatic symptoms, which outperform nonspecific treatment; and the family-based approaches to eating disorders, which outperform individual approaches in the cases where family involvement is possible.

The conclusion from this strand is precise and important: **there is no general hierarchy of therapies, but there are specific indications for which therapy is appropriate for which condition.** A student who has learned that is in a position to make a real clinical decision.

## 7. The Methodological Problems

The common factors research programme has been limited by four methodological problems, and a student who is evaluating a claim about the common factors should look for how a given study has handled each.

**The problem of the conditions compared.** The finding of equivalence holds for well-conducted therapies of comparable quality. It does not extend to comparing a well-conducted therapy with a poorly conducted one, with a non-equivalent modality, or with a treatment delivered by practitioners at very different levels of skill.

**The problem of the outcome measure.** Standardised symptom scales systematically understate the differences between therapies, partly because they measure the presence of symptoms rather than the person's experience of change. Where the client is asked how much they have improved, the differences between therapies are larger. The field's standard practice of relying on symptom scales was adopted for good methodological reasons — reliability, comparability, and resistance to expectancy bias in the client — but it has the effect of systematically favouring the common factors position.

**The problem of statistical power and of equivalence testing.** Studies designed to detect differences between therapies are usually underpowered to detect small differences, and a failure to reject the null hypothesis is not evidence of equivalence. The methodological standard now requires equivalence or non-inferiority designs with pre-specified margins, and the number of studies using them remains small.

**The problem of the researcher's allegiance.** The common factors movement has had a strong prior commitment to its conclusion, and the field's tendency to treat the equivalence finding as decisive was, in part, a movement's tendency to find its conclusion in the data. The alliance researchers have been the most careful in this respect, and the current, more careful position owes a great deal to that care.
