# Behavioral Activation and Activity Scheduling

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the origins of behavioural activation in the treatment of depression, and state the theory of depression it is based on.
- Describe the three components of behavioural activation, and explain the mechanism of change each involves.
- Describe the treatment rationale and the course of behavioural activation with an adolescent.
- Explain the relationship between behavioural activation and the cognitive model, and between behavioural activation and the behavioural principles of Chapter 23.
- Apply activity monitoring and activity scheduling to a case of adolescent depression and withdrawal.
- Describe the evidence for behavioural activation with young people, and evaluate its limitations.
- Explain the adaptations required for young people, including the specific modifications for developmental level and for the comorbidity of anxiety.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of behavioural activation with young people.

## Chapter Overview

The cognitive revolution gave depression a theory of mind. Behavioural activation gave it a theory of action, and the two theories turned out to be compatible in a way that was not obvious at the time.

The behaviour therapy tradition had observed since the 1930s that the defining features of depression are not attributions and not beliefs but **withdrawal**: the reduction of activity, of engagement, of contact, and of reward. People who are depressed do not think differently about the world so much as they do *fewer things*, and the reduction in activity produces a reduction in reward, and the reduction in reward produces a further reduction in activity. The account is a behavioural account of a cyclical process, and it makes a prediction that the cognitive account did not: that interrupting the cycle would produce improvement, and that the improvement would precede rather than follow the change in thinking.

The intervention follows from the account. Depression is maintained by a deficit of rewarding activity and by the avoidance of situations that have been associated with non-reward. The treatment therefore consists of identifying the rewarding activities that the person has stopped, scheduling them, and doing them — and, where the activities have been stopped because they have become unrewarding, restructuring them so that they are rewarding again.

For adolescents, the case for behavioural activation is unusually strong, for reasons that are developmental. The adolescent's primary developmental task is the construction of a social identity through competence and connection, and withdrawal is directly opposed to that task. The adolescent with depression is not only distressed; the depression is interfering with the very process by which distress would ordinarily be resolved. The treatment that re-engages the adolescent in activity is a treatment that works on the depression and on the development at the same time, and this is one of the clearest cases in the book of a theory-driven intervention that addresses a developmental principle as well as a symptom.

This chapter presents the theory, the procedure, the modifications for young people, the evidence, and the case illustration.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The behaviour therapy tradition and depression

The first wave of behaviour therapy approached depression through the classical conditioning literature, and the account was of depression as a learned helplessness response to uncontrollable aversive events. Seligman's work on learned helplessness was the most influential formulation, and it drew on the animal literature on exposure to uncontrollable shock, in which the animal's failure to escape is learned and generalised to situations in which escape is possible.

The treatment implication was a form of exposure: the deliberate restoration of control. Seligman and colleagues developed what became known as the **control therapy** or the **mastery** approach, in which the patient is given tasks of graded difficulty whose successful completion demonstrates that outcomes are contingent on behaviour.

This work was important and it was largely eclipsed. The reason is partly historical, because the cognitive revolution captured the field, and partly conceptual, because the learned helplessness account of depression is a theory of the acquisition of a deficit and says little about how the deficit is maintained, which is the question that most depressed patients present.

### 1.2 The activity record

The most important antecedent to behavioural activation was Jacobson's work in the 1930s, and specifically the **activity record**: a daily log in which the patient recorded, in hourly units, the activities of the day and, for each, a rating of the degree of pleasure and of the degree of self-mastery achieved.

The activity record was developed for a purpose that the field has since forgotten. Jacobson used it to demonstrate that the depressed patient over-estimates the unpleasantness and under-estimates the pleasure of their activities, and that the discrepancy between the patient's report and the actual experience is itself a maintaining factor: the patient avoids the activity on the basis of an expectation that the activity is unpleasant, and the avoidance prevents the disconfirming experience. In this respect, the activity record was the first treatment technology to target a belief through behavioural data rather than through argument, and the discrepancy it measured is the ancestor of the modern behavioural activation rationale.

### 1.3 The cognitive revolution and the divergence

The cognitive model of depression, developed from Beck's work in the 1960s, proposed that depression is maintained by a specific pattern of negative automatic thoughts, by negative core beliefs, and by the cognitive triad. It produced an effective treatment, and for two decades cognitive therapy was the dominant approach.

The divergence between the two traditions was sharp in the 1970s and softened in the 1990s, and the softening came from a direction nobody expected: from the recognition, inside the cognitive tradition, that behavioural activation was effective and that its effect on mood was more rapid than that of the cognitive interventions. The treatment produced a substantial improvement in mood in the first few sessions, before any cognitive work had been done, and this was a striking finding because the cognitive model had predicted that the cognitive work should come first.

The resolution was the observation that the cognitive and the behavioural interventions address the same process at different points in the cycle, and that the behavioural intervention acts upstream, on the behavioural withdrawal that generates the negative thoughts, and the cognitive intervention acts on the content of the thoughts. The two are complementary, and the combination — which is now the most common form of the treatment — is described in Chapter 16 and Chapter 17.

### 1.4 Behavioural activation as a distinct treatment

The 1970s and 1980s saw the development of behavioural activation as a treatment in its own right, most influentially by Jacobson and colleagues, and later by the group associated with the New Haven school, who produced the treatment manual and the outcome studies.

The rationale was stated in a form that has become the standard statement of the treatment:

> Depression is a disorder of behaviour in which the person has withdrawn from rewarding activities, and the withdrawal is maintained by the avoidance of situations in which the person has experienced non-reward and by the loss of the reinforcing consequences of activity. The treatment consists of the identification of activities that the person finds rewarding, the scheduling of those activities, and the performance of them, together with the reduction of avoidance and the mastery of necessary tasks.

Two features of this rationale are worth emphasising, because they distinguish behavioural activation from everything else in the book.

**It predicts the sequence of change.** Mood improves before cognition changes. This is a testable and unusual prediction, and it has been supported.

**It explains why the depression persists even when the person "knows" better.** The person with depression has usually tried to think their way out of it and failed, because the withdrawal continues, and the withdrawal is what maintains the negative thoughts.

## 2. The Theory

### 2.1 The behavioural model of depression

The behavioural model holds that depression is not primarily a disorder of mood with a disorder of thought attached. It is a **disorder of behaviour** in which four processes operate together.

**The reduction of reinforcing activity.** The person stops doing the things that are rewarding. The reasons are a lack of energy, an absence of expectation that the activity will be rewarding, and the withdrawal itself.

**The increase in aversive activity.** The person substitutes activities that are aversive or numbing: rumination, worry, self-criticism, substance use, sleep, screen time, and overeating. These activities are frequently more immediately reinforcing than the alternatives, which is why the depression is so difficult to escape by intention.

**The avoidance of situations associated with non-reward.** The person avoids situations in which they have experienced rejection, failure, or a lack of recognition. The avoidance prevents the corrective experience.

**The disruption of the circadian and the physiological systems.** Sleep, appetite, activity, and light exposure become irregular, and the irregularity is itself a maintaining factor.

The model is a cycle rather than a list, and the cycle is the treatment's target: the withdrawal reduces the reward, the reduced reward increases the withdrawal, and the increasing withdrawal increases the avoidance, which further reduces the reward.

### 2.2 The role of the cognition

The behavioural model does not exclude cognition; it locates it. Cognition enters at three points.

**As an anticipation.** The person does not undertake an activity because they expect no reward from it. The expectation is the mechanism of the avoidance, and it is directly testable against the actual outcome.

**As an attribution.** The person attributes outcomes to global and stable causes — "I never get anything right", "nothing ever works out for me" — and the attribution generalises from a specific event to the whole of the person's life.

**As a self-evaluation.** The person evaluates the self through the activities undertaken and the results obtained, so that the reduction in activity reduces the number of occasions on which the self can be evaluated positively.

The behavioural approach to each of these is the same: change the activity, and the cognition follows. This is why behavioural activation can be delivered without cognitive disputation, and why it is often the preferred treatment for a young person who is not ready to examine his own thoughts.

### 2.3 The reinforcing value of activity

The treatment rests on an assumption that is worth examining: that activity is reinforcing, and that the reinforcing value of an activity is a function of its *immediacy* and its *certainty*.

**Immediacy.** A consequence that is delayed is less reinforcing, and this is why many of the activities that a depressed adolescent gives up are those with delayed reward — studying for an exam, applying for a job, learning an instrument, writing to a friend. The behavioural approach is to restructure the activity so that the reward is immediate: study for twenty minutes and then do something enjoyable, rather than studying for three hours in the hope of a result in June.

**Certainty.** An activity whose outcome is uncertain is less reinforcing than one whose outcome is predictable, and this is the rationale for the graded task in mastery work: a task with a predictable and achievable outcome provides a reliable small reward at a time when the young person needs reliable small rewards.

**Magnitude.** A large activity is more rewarding when it is done, but it is much less likely to be done, and in a depressed adolescent the probability of undertaking matters more than the magnitude of the reward.

**Social contact.** Almost all activities that maintain well-being in adolescence are social, and the social component is usually the component that has been lost. The activity schedule must therefore be a schedule of *contact* as much as a schedule of tasks.

## 3. The Three Components

### 3.1 Activity monitoring

**Activity monitoring** is the daily record in which the young person logs their activities, together with a rating of the pleasure and of the mastery associated with each. The Jacobsonian version used hourly units across the day; the modern versions use a simpler daily or twice-daily log, and the simplification is a developmental adaptation.

The purposes of the monitoring are four.

**To establish the baseline.** The clinician needs to know what the young person's days actually contain, and the answer is frequently surprising. A young person who presents as "doing nothing" has days that are full — of sleeping, of scrolling, of eating, of avoidance, of small tasks that occupy the time without occupying the person.

**To make the discrepancy visible.** The monitoring shows, on the page, the difference between the activity and the predicted quality of the activity. The young person can see the entries for the Saturday when they "did nothing" and can see the rating of pleasure recorded against it.

**To establish the pattern.** A weekly graph of activity levels against mood levels shows the relationship, and it usually shows it clearly.

**To provide the data for the schedule.** The activities with the highest pleasure ratings and the lowest mood ratings are the candidates for the schedule, and this is why the monitoring precedes the scheduling.

**The practical problem with monitoring is compliance**, and the clinician should expect that a depressed adolescent will not complete the log. Three responses are useful. The log should be short. The clinician should complete the first day with the young person, sitting with them and logging their day. And the log should be reviewed in the session, not collected and left.

### 3.2 Activity scheduling

**Activity scheduling** is the specification of what the young person will do, when, and with whom, and it is the active component of the treatment. The specification should be written in three columns: the activity, the day and time, and the person involved.

Four principles govern the schedule.

**Schedule activities that are rewarding, in the sense of the section above.** The activity should be selected on the basis of the pleasure ratings from the monitoring, and it should be one the young person has actually enjoyed at some point, not an activity chosen by the clinician because it would be good for them.

**Schedule mastery activities alongside pleasure activities.** This is the Jacobson and colleagues' contribution and it is the part most often omitted. The depressed adolescent's problem is not only that they do nothing; it is that they have stopped doing the difficult things, and the difficult things are where the self-evaluation is generated. The mastery activity is specified in graded steps, with the smallest step achievable.

**Schedule social contact.** Because the social element is the one that has been lost and the one that most distinguishes the adolescent depression from the adult depression, the schedule must include contact with other people, and the contact must be specified in terms of the people and the duration.

**Schedule into the difficult slots.** The schedule is built around the times in the week when the withdrawal is worst, and not around the times when the young person is already functioning.

### 3.3 Activity monitoring and the graded task

**Graded task assignment (GTA)** is the third component, and it is the one most associated with behavioural activation and with the treatment of depression in young people.

The procedure has two halves. In the first, the young person and the clinician construct a **task hierarchy** of activities, from those that are currently impossible to those that are currently easy, with the activities graded by difficulty, and with the steps specified in the units in which they would actually be done. A typical hierarchy for an adolescent who has withdrawn from school might run: get out of bed before my mother calls me; get dressed; have breakfast; sit at the table with my phone; walk to the end of the road; walk to the shop; go to the college café; go to a lesson.

In the second, the young person works up the hierarchy, completing one or two steps beyond their current position, and each completed step is recorded and acknowledged, and the acknowledgement is the reinforcement.

The hierarchy is constructed collaboratively and is based on the young person's own specification, and the clinician's role is largely to break the steps down into a fine enough grain. The most common clinical problem is a hierarchy whose first steps are too large, and the fix is always the same: subdivide further, until the first step is something the young person is confident they could do today.

**The rules of graded task assignment** that the evidence supports are specific. The tasks should be achievable but not trivial. The young person should choose the tasks, from a list the clinician has helped to construct. The hierarchy should be reviewed and adjusted weekly. Completion should be recorded and acknowledged immediately. The tasks should be broken down until the first step is clearly achievable, and no exceptions to this rule should be made.

### 3.4 The session structure

The typical behavioural activation session is short, structured, and practical.

**Review of the monitoring.** What did the young person do, and what was the mood? The review is brief and the clinician is careful not to moralise about the content of the log.

**The discrepancy.** The comparison between the predicted and the actual quality of the activities. The clinician's posture is curious rather than triumphant.

**The schedule.** The activities for the coming week are agreed and written down, and the young person takes the schedule away, and the schedule is the homework.

**The graded task.** One step of the hierarchy is added, and the step is small.

**The problem-solving of obstacles.** What is going to make this hard, and what will you do? The question is asked in advance, because it is much easier to solve the problem on Wednesday than in the session on Friday.

**The social component.** Where the young person is isolated, the schedule includes the specific steps by which contact will be made, and these are graded like everything else.

## 4. Behavioural Activation with Children and Adolescents

### 4.1 The developmental case

The case for behavioural activation with adolescents is stronger than the case for adults, and the reasons are developmental.

**The withdrawal is developmentally costly.** The adolescent's task is the construction of a stable identity through competence and connection, and every week of withdrawal is a week in which the developmental work does not happen. A young person who withdraws at fourteen has a harder task at twenty.

**The adolescent's mood is more behaviourally determined.** The adolescent's emotional state is substantially a function of activity, sleep, and social contact, and the behavioural levers are correspondingly more powerful.

**The cognitive intervention is harder.** A young person with depression is often not able to examine his own thoughts productively, and may find the cognitive model alienating, and behavioural activation gives him a way of working that does not require him to challenge his own perceptions. Many adolescents who refuse cognitive therapy will accept behavioural activation.

**The competence dimension is directly available.** The graded task is a developmental intervention as well as a clinical one, because it generates evidence of competence, and evidence of competence is what the adolescent's self-evaluation requires.

**Social reconnection is a protective factor.** The literature on adolescent development identifies supportive social connection as one of the most robust predictors of good outcomes, and behavioural activation is one of the few treatments that targets it directly.

### 4.2 The specific modifications

**Shorten the monitoring.** A daily log with two ratings, completed in under three minutes, is realistic. The full hourly record is not.

**Complete the first day with the young person.** A depressed adolescent will not start the log unaided, and the session in which the clinician logs the young person's day alongside them is often the session that establishes the alliance.

**Use a social, concrete activity list.** The clinician proposes activities from a list the young person has helped to make, and the activities are named specifically — "walk to the bridge and back" rather than "exercise" — and the specificity is what makes them doable.

**Grade more finely than feels necessary.** The clinician will feel that the steps are too small. They are not.

**Prioritise contact over productivity.** For a withdrawn adolescent, a coffee with a friend is a more valuable activity than a fortnight of revision, and the schedule should say so.

**Build around existing anchors.** The days on which the young person does something already — a music lesson, a shift, a football match — are the days on which the rest of the schedule should be built, and the clinician should look for them in the monitoring.

**Involve the family carefully.** Family involvement supports the schedule and provides the practical conditions, and it also introduces the risk that the schedule becomes a family performance. The young person should be consulted about what is shared.

### 4.3 The management of comorbidity

**Anxiety.** A young person with depression and anxiety presents a specific difficulty: the activities that relieve the depression are the activities the anxiety prevents, and the activities the anxiety tolerates are the ones that do not relieve the depression. The resolution is graded exposure combined with activation, and the principles of Chapter 25 apply, with the added specification that the exposure and the activity are the same activity.

**Trauma.** A young person whose withdrawal is trauma-related is not helped by an activity schedule that pushes them into situations in which they feel unsafe, and the trauma assessment in Chapter 38 comes first. The activity schedule in that case is built inside the young person's window of tolerance, and Chapter 40 develops this.

**Substance use.** A young person who uses substances to manage the depression presents a situation in which the behavioural analysis must account for the function of the substance use, which is frequently relief from the very states the activity schedule is designed to address. Chapter 47 addresses this in detail.

**Apathy and the loss of interest as a symptom.** A young person with depression may not be able to identify any activity they find rewarding, and the monitor may record no pleasure at all. The clinical response is to start with activities that are *mastery* activities and that are physically activating, and to rely on the improvement in energy that follows.

### 4.4 The school context

Depressed adolescents are often not in school, and the school is therefore the setting in which the activation must be designed.

**Attendance as the first target.** A graded return to school, using the first-day design from Chapter 25, is often the first step in the graded task hierarchy, and it is frequently the graded task that has the greatest downstream effect, because attendance unlocks the rest of the school-based activity.

**The school-based activity schedule.** A short daily schedule of specific activities, with at least one social component, agreed with the school, is more effective than a schedule kept at home, because the young person is in the building anyway.

**Coordination with the pastoral team.** The pastoral team is usually already managing the young person's absences and is likely to be applying a monitoring system of its own. The behavioural activation schedule and the school's attendance system should be the same system, and a practitioner who adds a third system has increased the burden rather than reduced it.

## 5. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Stopped Leaving Her Room

**Presentation.** Nadia is sixteen and has been out of school for seven weeks. She spends her days in her room, emerging to eat and to use the bathroom. Her mother reports that she used to be "loud and funny" and is now "like a ghost". She has not been in the same room as her younger sister for most of the period. Her mother is at work during the day, and the youngest sibling is at school, so the house is empty.

Nadia's own account, given with some reluctance at the fourth session, is that she stopped going in because "everyone's got their group and I'm not in one, and I'm not going to be the new girl again." She has not been to school in seven weeks and describes each morning as "wanting to disappear".

**The assessment.**

*The mood.* Nadia describes low mood, anhedonia, poor sleep, poor appetite, and a loss of energy. The picture is a depressive episode with a marked social withdrawal, and there is no history of mania, of psychosis, and of any previous episode.

*The functional analysis of the withdrawal.* This is where the behavioural work begins, and the analysis is short.

The antecedents of staying in bed are: waking, and the morning's requirement to get dressed and travel. The consequences are: the escape from the demand, and the relief. But there is a second and more important consequence, and the monitoring makes it visible. Nadia's mother, in desperation, brings her breakfast to the room, and stays and talks to her for twenty minutes, and does not make her come down. The mother's attention — which is warm, and is not given under any other circumstances — is contingent on the withdrawal. The mother is reinforcing the withdrawal with the very behaviour that is most sustaining of it, and she is doing it out of love.

The consequences of going to school, by contrast, are that Nadia spends a day in a building where she is not known, is looked at, and is required to interact. The school has been, in effect, delivering a strong punishment for attending.

*The antecedents of the social withdrawal.* Nadia's account identifies a specific trigger: a change of school in the previous year, and a period of a few weeks in which she was not spoken to by anyone. The withdrawal is not a mood that began the absence; it is a response to a specific social loss that was then maintained.

**The formulation.** A depressive episode, maintained by a behavioural cycle: the withdrawal reduces the reinforcing activity, the reduced activity produces a deeper depressive mood, the deeper mood produces a wider withdrawal, and the mother's daily attention to the room sustains the withdrawal behaviour itself. The social anxiety is real and is not a symptom of the depression; it preceded it, and it is the reason the depression is maintained.

**The intervention.** The plan has five elements, and the sequencing is critical.

*Element one: the mother's contingency.* The mother is asked to stop bringing breakfast to the room and to stop sitting with Nadia in the morning, and this is the hardest part of the plan. The reason is explained carefully: the daily attention is what is keeping the pattern in place, and the love that is in the attention is not in question. What is proposed instead is a daily contact that is not contingent on the withdrawal — a fixed fifteen minutes at a fixed time, with a specific activity, in which the mother talks about her own day, and which Nadia may or may not attend. The purpose is to preserve the relationship while removing the contingency.

The mother also agrees to a gradual reduction in the practical support: the breakfast comes to the kitchen, the door is left open, the answer to the question "is Nadia up?" becomes "she's in her room", and the mother's own daily functioning is protected, because a mother who has not slept properly for seven weeks cannot implement any plan.

*Element two: the graded task.* Nadia constructs the hierarchy, with the help of the counsellor, and it runs: get up; get dressed; come downstairs; have breakfast at the table; sit in the garden; walk to the corner; walk to the park; go to the shops; get the bus; go to college.

The first step — get up — is placed at the level where Nadia is confident, and the advance criterion is three consecutive days. The first four steps are all achieved within the first fortnight, and the counsellor does not push past the fifth step even when Nadia is ready, because the advance criterion is a clinical tool and not a suggestion.

*Element three: the social contact.* The graded task alone produces an improvement in mood that is real but limited, because the tasks are solitary. The schedule therefore adds, in week two, one scheduled contact per day with a named person, and the contact is chosen with Nadia — a former friend, a cousin, a member of the football team Nadia used to play in — and each contact is preceded by a text and is short, and the shortness is the point, because the first successful contact after seven weeks of isolation is a short one.

*Element four: the school.* The graded return to school uses the first-day design. Nadia attends for one lesson in week three, in a subject she does not feel exposed in, with the pastoral support worker, and leaves having spoken to one person. The school plan specifies what will happen in the corridor, which is the place she most fears, and the plan has the pastoral support worker walk with her from the gate to the classroom for the first two weeks.

*Element five: the monitoring continues.* The daily log continues throughout, because it is the data that will show whether the plan is working, and because it is one of the few things in Nadia's life that is structured and predictable.

**The outcome.** At three months, Nadia is in school four days a week, taking three subjects, and is meeting one friend weekly. Her mother reports that the mornings are "normal-ish". The depression has not fully lifted and the work continues, and the partial response is the expected one: behavioural activation works faster on the behavioural symptoms than on the mood, and a young person who has withdrawn for seven weeks does not rebuild a social world in three months.

**The interpretation.** Two features of this case are generalisable. The first is the maternal contingency, which is present in a large proportion of cases of adolescent withdrawal, and which is rarely identified because it presents as care. The second is the ordering of the work: the graded task before the social contact, and the social contact before the school return, because the school return requires a social confidence that the graded task alone cannot supply. A practitioner who sent Nadia to school in week one would have produced a worse outcome than one who did nothing, because the failure would have confirmed her expectation.
