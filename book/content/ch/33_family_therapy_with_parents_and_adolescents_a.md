# Family Therapy with Parents and Adolescents

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why the parent–adolescent relationship is the central developmental relationship of the second decade of life, and why the work with the parents is the essential work.
- Describe the developmental tasks of the adolescent years and the parental tasks that correspond to them.
- Explain the parent training literature and the behavioural analysis of the parent–adolescent interaction cycle.
- Describe the mainstream and conflict patterns of parent–adolescent communication and the clinical significance of each.
- Apply the principles of developmental and systemic consultation to a school or clinic case involving a young person and their parents.
- Design a parent-focused intervention for a case of adolescent non-compliance or conflict, including the components and the sequencing.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of work with parents in the adolescent years, including the question of whose problem the work addresses.

## Chapter Overview

This chapter is the practical centre of Part V, and it is the chapter in which the preceding three theories are brought together around a single clinical situation: a young person and their parents in conflict.

The argument of the chapter is simple and it is the most important thing in this part of the book. **When a young person and their parents are in conflict, the parents are the more tractable target.** This is not a value judgement about who is more important. It is a statement about what a practitioner can actually work with. The parents are adults, they are available, they are generally motivated by the referral, and the relationship between them and the young person is the mechanism through which everything else operates. A young person who is caught in the middle of a conflict between two adults cannot think, cannot develop, and cannot use the therapy; the adults can.

The developmental literature makes the same point from the other direction. The adolescent's task is a renegotiation of the relationship with the parents, and the renegotiation is a *developmental* task that can only be completed if the parents make the renegotiation possible. A young person cannot unilaterally leave the child subsystem; the parents hold the key to the door and, if the door stays locked, the young person's options are to force it, to break it, or to give up and stay.

The chapter therefore covers: the developmental rationale; the parent training literature and the behavioural analysis of the escalation cycle; the communication patterns and their clinical significance; the practical structure of a parent-focused intervention; the consultation relationship with the school; and the ethical and cultural analysis, which is substantial because the work with parents raises the question of whose difficulty is being treated.

## 1. The Developmental Rationale

### 1.1 The second decade

The period from approximately ten to twenty is the period in which the young person becomes a person rather than a child, and the mechanism is the renegotiation of the relationship with the parents. This is a period of enormous change in the young person's cognition, in which the ability to think hypothetically and to reason about other people's states develops rapidly, and it is a period in which the peer world becomes the primary arena of development.

The developmental literature gives four accountabilities to the parents during this period, and each is a task that can be observed and worked on.

**To provide a base of secure attachment that the adolescent can gradually relinquish.** The adolescent needs a secure base to move out from, and the secure base is not a helicopter parent; it is the reliable, non-intrusive availability of an adult who is not frightened of the adolescent's development.

**To maintain the parental authority that the adolescent needs.** The authority question is the one that generates most of the conflict in this period, and the adolescent's developmental task is to test the limits of the parental authority, and the parents' task is to hold the limit without needing to win. A parent who cannot hold a limit without a fight will lose the developmental negotiation.

**To provide a structure that is firm enough to be safe and flexible enough to grow into.** The adolescent needs a structure that increases in responsibility as the capacity increases, and a structure that does not increase in responsibility produces a young person who cannot manage, and a structure that increases too fast produces a young person who cannot cope.

**To maintain a relationship with the adolescent that is not the same as the relationship with the child.** The parents have to be able to relate to a person who is not the child they had, and the parental task of mourning the child is routinely skipped, and the young person experiences the mourning as rejection.

### 1.2 The mismatch and the symptom

The clinical presentation in this period is very often a **structural mismatch**: the parents are operating a pre-adolescent structure over an adolescent child, and the symptom is the report of the mismatch. The report is in the language of behaviour — the arguments, the slammed doors, the refusal, the secretiveness, the risk-taking, the disappearing — because the young person has no other channel through which to say "the rules here are for a younger person than I am".

This is the diagnosis that the structural model of Chapter 31 makes most precisely, and it is very frequently the correct one. The clinical test is simple and a student should be able to apply it: **compare the demands and the expectations the parents are making with the developmental capacity of the specific young person.** The demands for a fourteen-year-old's contribution to the household, the expectations about curfews, about the use of the phone, about disclosure, about the treatment of the parents' requests, about the sharing of information — these are the variables.

### 1.3 The task of the practitioner

The practitioner's task in this period has three parts, and they are in a definite order.

**First, to establish what the parents want.** Not what they say they want in the referral, which is usually the symptom's removal, but what they want for the young person's life. Almost every parent in this period wants something they cannot say, because saying it would be to acknowledge that the family is not in the state they have described. A mother who refers her fifteen-year-old for "attitude" and who actually wants a relationship in which her daughter tells her about her life is a mother who can be worked with, and a mother who wants the daughter to obey is a mother who can be worked with differently.

**Second, to help the parents make the changes that the developmental situation requires.** This is the work, and it is the work with the adults.

**Third, to work with the young person** on the developmental tasks that the improved family environment makes possible.

## 2. The Parent–Adolescent Interaction Cycle

### 2.1 The behavioural analysis of conflict

The parent training literature produced one of the most thoroughly validated findings in the child behavioural literature: the **escalation cycle** in which oppositional and antagonistic behaviour between parents and children is maintained by a predictable sequence of responses, and the sequence is not the one the parents intend.

The cycle runs as follows. The parent makes a request. The child refuses. The parent repeats the request, with escalating insistence, with warnings, with increased volume, and often with the addition of a threat. The child escalates in response. The parent then backs down, either explicitly, or by changing the subject, or by shouting and then leaving, or by threatening a consequence that does not arrive. The child's escalation has produced the parent's withdrawal, and the withdrawal is the reinforcement.

The sequence has three features that a practitioner should be able to name.

**The escalation is a communication.** It is a bid for attention, for a change of activity, for a delay, for help, or for the removal of the demand, and it is the only bid available if the polite bid has been ignored.

**The parent's escalation is the trigger for the child's.** A parent who increases the volume produces a child who increases the volume, and the increase is not a character trait; it is a reaction to a reaction.

**The parent's backing down is the reinforcement.** The behaviour that ends the battle is the behaviour that is rewarded by the end of the battle, and the reward is the removal of the demand, which is the most reliable and most reinforcing consequence available in the situation.

### 2.2 The parent training evidence

The parent training literature is the strongest evidence in the field for the claim that parental behaviour is a tractable target, and the findings are consistent.

**Parent training has a robust effect on oppositional behaviour and on conduct problems**, and the Cochrane reviews report substantial and durable effects, and the effects are maintained at follow-up.

**The improvements are broad.** Parent training produces improvements in the child's oppositional behaviour, in the parent's distress, in sibling conflict, in the family's functioning, and in some studies in the child's academic performance.

**Parent training works best when the parent's behaviour changes and when the family is engaged with the work**, and the fidelity of the parent's use of the techniques determines the outcome.

**Parent training works less well with young people who are severely antisocial**, and the pattern of the research is that the interventions work better with younger children and with less entrenched patterns.

**The sessions with the parents are the sessions.** Almost all parent training programmes involve the parents alone for the core of the work, and the child is not in the room.

### 2.3 The skills parent training teaches

The core content of the parent training that has the best evidence is short and teachable.

**Positive reinforcement and attention.** The parent learns to attend to the desired behaviour promptly, specifically, and with a description of what the child did, and to increase the attention to the desired behaviour relative to the undesired.

**Ignoring and planned ignoring.** The parent learns to withdraw attention from the undesired behaviour, genuinely, without a verbal signal, and the parent learns that the withdrawal is temporary and that the behaviour will get worse before it gets better.

**Time-out from reinforcement** and **response cost**, taught with the specific rules that make them safe and effective.

**Consistency between the adults**, which is the most commonly neglected and most necessary element, and which requires the practitioner to work with every caregiver who has significant contact with the young person.

**The skill of talking rather than shouting**, taught through modelling, role-play, and behavioural rehearsal.

**The "catch them being good" practice**, which is a specific, concrete, and effective technique: the parent is instructed to notice, describe, and respond to every instance of the desired behaviour, and to record them, and the recording is what makes the noticing systematic.

### 2.4 A caution about the behavioural model of parenting

The parent training literature is the strongest evidence base in the field, and it is also the literature with the strongest cultural and moral assumptions, and a student should be able to state the concern.

The model treats the parent's attention as a resource to be allocated according to a set of technical rules, and it treats the child's behaviour as a set of responses to be selected. Two consequences follow. First, the young person is understood as a set of behaviours to be managed rather than as a developing person with a point of view, and a practitioner who works exclusively in this frame will produce a compliant young person and a distressed relationship. Second, the model sits uncomfortably with a body of evidence and ethical argument that the consistent use of reinforcement and the withdrawal of attention is a form of conditioning that is appropriate for a behaviour and inappropriate for a relationship.

The resolution is the one that the evidence itself supports: parent training works when it is used to increase the *quality* of the relationship, not to increase the *compliance* of the child. The techniques that improve the relationship — attending to what the child is doing well, describing it specifically, being consistent, being calm — are the same techniques. The difference is in what the practitioner is trying to achieve with them, and the difference is observable in the outcome.

## 3. Communication Between Parents and Adolescents

### 3.1 The mainstream and the conflict pattern

The developmental literature on parent–adolescent communication identifies a **mainstream pattern** of good communication and a **conflict pattern** of poor communication, and the characteristics of each are the basis of the assessment.

The **mainstream** is characterised by the parents listening without interruption; by the parents being accepting of the young person's feelings even when they disapprove of the behaviour; by the parents acknowledging the young person's point of view as a legitimate one; by the parents being reasonably consistent; by the young person disclosing; by the parents being involved in the young person's world; and by the parents showing a mild sense of humour.

The **conflict pattern** is characterised by the parents' frequent criticism; by the parents' hostility and their belittling of the young person; by the parents' physical punishment; by the parents' inconsistent discipline; by the young person's withholding of information; by the young person's refusal to talk; by the young person's escape from the family; and by the young person's physical and verbal aggression outside the home.

The patterns are almost perfectly correlated with the outcomes, and the correlation is large. A student should know the list and should be able to use it as an assessment instrument in the first session, because the two patterns are readily observable in a single family session.

### 3.2 The disclosure process

The literature on adolescent disclosure identifies a developmental sequence with direct clinical implications.

**The early childhood phase** (approximately six to twelve) is one of high parental control and high disclosure, in which the parents determine what is disclosed and the young person does not select.

**The early adolescence phase** (approximately twelve to fifteen) is one of decreasing parental control and a shift towards peer orientation, in which the young person's disclosure to the parents declines and the parental knowledge of the young person's world declines with it. The developmental task of this phase is described as **detachment from parental control**, and it is a normal and necessary stage.

**The middle adolescence phase** (approximately fifteen to seventeen) is one of increasing peer orientation and of the development of an **individualised system of beliefs, values, and perceptions** that is often incompatible with the parents'. The developmental task is the development of an independent moral and value system, and the young person needs to have arguments in order to do it, and the arguing is the work, not the failure.

**The later adolescence phase** (approximately seventeen to twenty) is one of a gradual re-establishment of a relationship with the parents that is more equal and more companionate, and the relationship that eventually forms is the one in which the young person can disclose.

Two clinical conclusions follow, and they are important.

**The decline in disclosure during early adolescence is normal and is not a problem to be solved.** A practitioner who treats the young person's refusal to tell the parents about their weekend as a symptom is doing something harmful, and a parent who experiences it as a symptom will respond with intrusion, and the intrusion produces more withdrawal. The developmental task is detachment from parental control, and the parent's task is to allow it.

**The young person's arguments in the middle adolescent years are part of the work.** The developmental literature is unusually clear that the "who owns me" arguments are necessary for the development of an individualised value system, and that a parent who wins these arguments wins them at the cost of the young person's development. The parent's task is to survive them, and to set the limits on the *form* of the argument while not requiring the young person to abandon the content.

### 3.3 The three common conflicts

Three conflicts recur in clinical work, and each has a different clinical character.

**The autonomy conflict.** The young person is asserting control over choices — clothing, friends, music, the use of the phone, the choice of clothes, the choice of music, the choice of friends, the choice of what to wear, the choice of where to go, the choice of which music to listen to, the choice of which friends to see. The underlying developmental issue is the differentiation of self, and the parental contribution is usually the overreaction. A parent who treats a preference about clothing as a challenge to authority produces a fight in which neither the clothing nor the authority is really the point.

**The sexuality conflict.** This conflict, which arises in the context of the family's boundaries, the young person's emerging sexuality, the family's cultural and religious values, the parents' own relationship with their own bodies, and the question of privacy, is unusually charged and it requires cultural competence rather than technical skill.

**The achievement conflict.** The young person's performance in school is the site of the parents' own histories, their own educational experience, their own social class, and their own comparative standing. This conflict is frequently a conflict between the young person and the parent's projected self, and it is the conflict in which the family therapy work is most clearly indicated, because the parents' investment in the young person's achievement is not the young person's own.
