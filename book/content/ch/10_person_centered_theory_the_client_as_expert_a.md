# Person-Centred Theory: The Client as Expert

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the historical and philosophical context of person-centred theory, and identify the four influences on Rogers' thinking.
- State the propositions of Rogers' theory of personality, including the self-concept, the organismic experience, and the fundamental needs.
- Explain the concept of incongruence and describe the process Rogers proposed for the reduction of incongruence.
- State the necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic personality change and explain what "necessary" and "sufficient" mean in this context.
- Describe the core therapeutic conditions and give a concrete clinical example of each.
- Apply person-centred theory to a case involving a child or adolescent, and identify what the theory requires and what it makes difficult.
- Describe the modern developments in person-centred theory, including the focus on the person as a process, the integration of affect and cognition, and the social and political dimensions.
- Evaluate the evidence for person-centred therapy and for the therapeutic alliance as the common factor.

## Chapter Overview

Carl Rogers' client-centred theory was published in 1942 and revised in 1951, and it is one of the very few theories in counseling that has remained continuously in clinical use for eight decades. Its longevity is remarkable and its reasons are instructive: it was the first theory in the field to give the practitioner a testable account of the conditions under which change occurs; it was the first to make the relationship itself the object of theory; it was the first to be written in the language of the client's own experience, which made it immediately usable by practitioners without a research background; and it produced the most replicated finding in the field's history, the relationship between the quality of the therapeutic alliance and outcome.

This chapter presents the theory in its full structure. It begins with the context, in which the personal and intellectual situation of its author and the professional situation of the field in the 1940s are both relevant, and it includes the episode of Rogers' break with the psychoanalytic movement, which was the first public act of theory-making in the profession and set a pattern for the conflicts that followed.

The chapter then works through the theory's propositions: the person as an organism with an innate actualising tendency; the self-concept and the internal frame of reference; the distinction between the organismic experience and the self-concept; the fundamental needs; and the process of change, which Rogers described with an accuracy that remains remarkable.

The final third of the chapter addresses three practical questions: what person-centred theory requires of a practitioner working with children and adolescents, which is a harder question than it is usually treated as; what the evidence says; and what the modern developments in the theory, including the attention to power and to social conditions, add to the original formulation.

## 1. Historical and Philosophical Context

### 1.1 The four influences

Rogers' theory is usually presented as emerging from a single clinical tradition, and it is more accurate to present it as the convergence of four.

**The clinical method of Otto Rank and the Rankian "will" psychology.** Rogers' first clinical work was as a psychologist at a mental hospital in Ohio, where he was a member of a team following Rank's approach. From it he took the emphasis on the *will* — the striving of the person, not merely the reaction of the person — and the emphasis on the creative, forward-moving quality of human behaviour. Rogers' formulation of the actualising tendency is the direct descendant of Rank's will.

**The field theory of Kurt Lewin.** Rogers' doctoral work was in Lewin's group, and he inherited the field theory's central proposition: behaviour is a function of the person and the environment, understood as a field in which the person is located, rather than as a reaction to a stimulus. The phrase "person and environment" appears in Rogers' formulations in a way that reveals the lineage, and the entire framework is a person-in-field theory in Lewin's sense.

**The phenomenological tradition.** Rogers' college training included a year of study in religion and philosophy, and he was decisively influenced by the phenomenology of the European tradition, transmitted through the American reception of Brentano and the work of the Chicago school. The consequence is the client-centred theory's central methodological commitment: that the only authority on a person's experience is that person.

**The psychodynamic tradition, from which he broke.** Rogers' work at the Menninger Clinic in the 1930s exposed him to psychoanalytic and neo-Freudian practice, and the experience of the analytic relationship — which Rogers described as an "inescapable" and often puzzling encounter — is the direct source of the client-centred method's central concern with the practitioner's own congruence.

### 1.2 The break with psychoanalysis

The episode of 1939, in which Rogers presented a paper at the annual meeting of the American Psychological Association challenging the practice of psychoanalytic interpretation, is the founding moment of the theory and of the profession's reputation for theoretical conflict. Rogers' argument was that the analyst's interpretations were a form of advice, and that the analytic relationship had no clear goals, no responsibility, and no accountability; and that the analyst, in effect, "reframed" the client's experience according to the analyst's own frame, which was a "schizophrenic" withdrawal from the client's actual frame of reference.

The paper was received as an attack on the psychoanalytic establishment, and it cost Rogers his position. The episode matters to the present-day student for two reasons. First, it is the origin of the professional distinction between the client-centred and the psychoanalytic traditions, and the reasons for the distinction are still the best short statement of the difference between them. Second, it is the origin of the profession's tradition of theoretical debate conducted in public and with considerable personal cost, which is a tradition with a mixed record and which the student should approach with a critical eye.

### 1.3 The professional context

Client-centred theory appeared at a moment when the dominant professional model was the medical one, in which the practitioner was an expert in a disorder and the client was a passive recipient of an expert service. Rogers' theory inverted every element of that model: the practitioner was not an expert in disorders but a facilitator of a process; the client was not a patient but the expert on their own experience; the goal was not the cure of a symptom but the resolution of a discrepancy within the person; and the theory of the practitioner became a part of the theory of the process.

The subsequent history of the theory is a history of the professional adoption of these commitments, which is why client-centred theory is now less a distinct therapy than a set of commitments that pervade the field. The codes of the profession, the measurement instruments (the Client Outcome Questionnaire, the Session Rating Scale), the emphasis on the alliance, the practice of routine outcome feedback, and the widespread use of the term "client" rather than "patient" are all descendants of Rogers' argument.

## 2. The Theory of the Person

### 2.1 The organismic experience

Rogers' account begins with what he called the **organismic experience** — the total, ongoing, ever-changing stream of experience that constitutes the person's actual life, comprising three distinguishable elements: the organism's experience of itself, of the environment, and of the relationship between the two. This is a phenomenological, not a neurophysiological, claim. Rogers is describing the person from the inside, and the description is of a live, moving, purposive process.

Three characteristics of the organismic experience are critical.

**It is always changing.** The organismic experience is a process, not a thing, and at every moment the whole of the person's experience is reorganising in the direction of its fulfilment, subject to the conditions described below.

**It is available to awareness.** The organismic experience is, in principle, fully available to the person's awareness, although the accuracy with which it is available varies enormously and is the key to the theory's account of maladjustment.

**It is governed by the actualising tendency.** This is the deepest claim. The organism, in its functioning, moves toward the fulfilment of its needs, and the fulfilment of the actualising tendency is a consequence of the organisation of the whole experience toward the coherence and integration of the organism's experience and the self. The actualising tendency is not a drive to be satisfied like a hunger; it is a tendency in the organisation of experience, and it is described in process terms rather than in need terms.

### 2.2 The self-concept

The **self-concept** is Rogers' term for the organism's internal representation of itself — the set of perceptions, judgments, and feelings that the person holds about who they are, which is accessible to awareness and which is organised in a way that is consistent with the person's experience.

The critical feature of the self-concept is that it is **incomplete**. It does not and cannot encompass the whole of the organismic experience, because it is a symbolisation, and symbolisation is always selective. There are two consequences. First, experiences that are inconsistent with the self-concept are denied symbolisation and are therefore not available to awareness. Second, and more consequentially for the therapy, the self-concept operates as a **selection apparatus**, admitting into awareness only those experiences that are consistent with it, and thereby determining what the person can learn about themselves.

This is the theoretical core of the entire theory, and the student should understand it well: **incongruence is not simply a discrepancy between two things; it is a discrepancy that operates as a filter on the person's access to their own experience.** A young person whose self-concept includes "I am the one who holds everything together" cannot fully experience the fact that they are exhausted, because the exhaustion is inconsistent with the self-concept, and the self-concept will not permit it into awareness. And the exhaustion will then be expressed behaviourally, which is exactly what the parent reports.

### 2.3 The fundamental needs

Rogers identified four basic psychological needs. The third of these is the most famous and the most often quoted out of context, and the other three are essential to the theory.

**The need for positive regard.** The need to be regarded by others as a person with worth, and to regard oneself in the same way. It is not a need for approval, and it is not a need to be liked; it is a need to be *accepted as a person*, which is why the "unconditional" qualification is decisive.

**The need for affection.** The need for some form of tender, caring response from others. It is a need for the experience of being valued and cared for, and its frustration produces a sense of worthlessness.

**The need for inclusion.** The need for the experience of being included in a group or a relationship, and the need to have an effect on the people with whom one is connected. Its frustration produces a sense of isolation and of being a stranger.

**The need for congruence.** The need for one's own behaviour, and one's own experience, to be in accordance with one's self-concept. It is a need for authenticity, for the sense that what one is and what one does and what one feels are one and the same thing. Its frustration produces anxiety, and it is the need whose frustration Rogers' theory locates at the centre of maladjustment.

### 2.4 The conditions of growth

The actualising tendency operates, in Rogers' account, under three conditions, and all three must be present for growth to occur.

**The psychological condition of congruence.** The person must be in a state in which the experience is fully congruent with the self-concept — which is to say, in which the person is in contact with their own experience.

**The psychological condition of unconditional positive regard.** The person must experience the *other* as understanding and accepting the self as it is, without conditions of worthiness.

**The psychological condition of empathic understanding.** The person must experience the other as understanding the person's experience from the inside.

Rogers' account is that the actualising tendency operates in the individual, in the family, and in the group, and that its operation in each depends on the presence of these three conditions. The implication for the therapy is direct and is the most important single proposition in the theory: **these three conditions are also the conditions of therapy**. The conditions in which the person grows are the conditions the practitioner must create, and nothing else is required of the practitioner for growth to occur.

## 3. The Theory of Maladjustment

### 3.1 The process of incongruence

Maladjustment, in Rogers' account, is not a set of symptoms and not a disease. It is a process with a specific structure, and the structure is the following.

An experience occurs that is inconsistent with the person's self-concept — because the experience is threatening, because it would require a major reorganisation of the self-concept, or because the person has never learned a way of symbolising it.

The experience cannot be symbolised into the self-concept without distortion. Two things follow. First, the experience is denied access to awareness, and the person becomes out of touch with it — which is the origin of the anxiety. Second, the inconsistent experience is expressed in behaviour that is distorted, in the sense that it expresses the underlying need in a form that is incongruent with the self-concept and is consequently ineffective or maladaptive. The characteristic example is the aggressive or destructive behaviour of a person whose self-concept includes "I am a loving and gentle person" and who has learned to express anger only in forms that preserve the self-concept.

The distortion of need into maladaptive behaviour is the theoretical heart of the account, and it has a number of important clinical implications. It explains why a young person's problem behaviour is often *the best available option within their self-concept*, and why telling them that they "could behave differently" is not only unhelpful but theoretically incoherent. It explains why a young person who is intelligent and capable can be extraordinarily clumsy with their own feelings. And it explains why the therapeutic task is not the elimination of the maladaptive behaviour but the restoration of the person's contact with the experience beneath it — because once the experience is available to awareness, the actualising tendency will begin to organise it more effectively, and the distorted expression will no longer be required.

### 3.2 The concept of the "real self"

Rogers' term for the person's underlying, organising experience — the source of the actualising tendency, and the potential for a fuller functioning that is experienced in the person but not symbolised — is the "real self" or the "self" in the process sense, as against the self-concept. The "fully functioning person" is one in whom there is a close correspondence between the organismic experience and the self-concept, so that the person is in touch with their own experience and able to act from it.

The therapy is the reduction of incongruence, and the goal is not to install a better self-concept but to remove the filter that prevents the person from having their own experience. The subtlety here is worth dwelling on, because it is the point at which Rogers' theory is most frequently and most seriously misunderstood: **the aim is not to make the person into what they should be, or what the practitioner thinks they could be, or what their potential allows. The aim is to make it possible for the person to be who they already are, without the distortion.** This is why the theory is a theory of the client as expert, and why the practitioner's imposition of an alternative identity is a violation of the theory's central claim.

## 4. The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions of Therapeutic Personality Change

The 1957 paper in which Rogers set out these conditions is, in the judgement of many in the field, the most important paper in the twentieth-century counseling literature, and it is worth working through its argument closely.

Rogers' claim is that certain conditions, if present in a counselling relationship, are both **necessary** and **sufficient** for personality change to occur. The conditions are the three already described — congruence, unconditional positive regard, and empathic understanding — with the addition of the structural conditions: that the two parties are in psychological contact; that the client is incongruent; and that the client is capable of, and motivated to, a process of change.

The claim of sufficiency was and remains the most contested part of it. Rogers himself later qualified it, saying that the conditions are sufficient to *initiate* a process of change and that the process itself then proceeds by the person's own actualising tendency, and that the conditions are necessary throughout for the process to continue. The logical objection is straightforward: the claim that a set of relational conditions is sufficient for a specific outcome invites the counter-example of a client who is not yet ready, or who does not want change, or whose circumstances make change impossible. Rogers' answer — that such a client does not meet the sixth condition, of motivation — is elegant and, in practice, unfalsifiable, since the motivation is inferred from the outcome. The student should hold the claim in this light: it is a powerful and practically useful claim, and it is not a strictly logical one.

The claim of necessity has fared much better. The consistency of the alliance–outcome relationship across therapies, and the research showing that the alliance mediates the delivery of specific technique, together support the proposition that these conditions are necessary for the change that the therapy is trying to produce.

## 5. The Core Therapeutic Conditions in Practice

### 5.1 Empathic understanding

Empathic understanding is the practitioner's accurate grasp of the client's experience, communicated in a way the client can recognise as accurate. It is not agreement, and it is certainly not sympathy. Rogers' formulation of the conditions of empathic understanding — the psychotherapist is in a state of primacy of interest in the client's phenomenological field; the client is the centre of attention; and the therapist attempts to communicate this understanding — is exact about the attitude and imprecise about the skill, and the modern research literature has addressed the skill directly.

The modern finding is significant and clinically useful: **accurate empathy predicts better outcome, and inaccurate empathy predicts worse outcome than a low level of accuracy.** A practitioner who believes they are understanding and is not is doing more harm than one who is careful and tentative. The training literature on practitioner accuracy — the extent to which a practitioner's attunement matches the client's internal state — suggests that this is a learnable skill, and the finding that accuracy improves when the practitioner is given structured feedback is a direct argument for the feedback practices developed in later chapters.

Three clinical failures are common and worth naming. **Intellectualisation** is the substitution of a conceptual account for an emotional response. **Over-identification** is the practitioner's own unprocessed material intruding into the empathic response. **Over-interpretation** is the practitioner's hypothesis offered as the client's experience. All three are more common in the early years of practice than the curriculum's emphasis on technique would suggest.

### 5.2 Unconditional positive regard

Unconditional positive regard is the practitioner's sustained acceptance of the client as a person of worth, without conditions of worthiness, and it is directed at the client as a person rather than at any particular behaviour or statement.

The requirement is demanding in two ways. First, it is demanding of the practitioner's own internal state: it cannot be performed reliably if the practitioner is disapproving, frightened, or bored, and the failure is frequently not a matter of the practitioner failing to try but of the practitioner not having the emotional resources to sustain the stance. Second, it is demanding because the practitioner's genuine disagreement and concern are part of the practice, and the theory's requirement is that the disagreement be held without withdrawal of regard. A practitioner who is being asked to work with a young person who is harming themselves has a genuine concern, and Rogers' framework does not ask the practitioner to abandon it; it asks that the concern not be converted into a withdrawal of acceptance.

The commonest failure is conditional regard in the form of approval and disapproval — the subtle "good" and "not so good" that Rogers and his colleagues described, and that research on the impact of practitioner behaviour on young people suggests can be quite readily detected.

### 5.3 Congruence

Congruence is the practitioner's authenticity — the matching between what the practitioner says and what the practitioner actually experiences, including the awareness and the expression of the practitioner's own feelings, which are in any case the practitioner's real feelings and not the ones the theory might require.

Congruence is the most difficult of the three, and the one most often superficially implemented. A practitioner who says "I can see this is really hard for you" when they are in fact bored has been dishonest; and the dishonesty is not a moral failure but a clinical one, because a client reliably detects incongruence and responds to it by withdrawing. A practitioner who says "I am frustrated, and I notice that I am frustrated, and I want to talk about that" is being congruent, and the invitation is the point.

The value of congruence with young people is considerable, and it is also the condition most likely to alarm a trainee. A young person who is testing — who wants to know whether this adult will survive contact with them — is testing precisely this, and a practitioner whose congruence is a performance of warmth will fail the test and lose the relationship. A practitioner who can be present with their own genuine reaction, including irritation, and who can own it, will usually pass it.

### 5.4 The practitioner's own theory of the person

The fourth condition in Rogers' later formulation is that the practitioner must have a theory of the person, and that this theory must be compatible with the conditions. A practitioner who believes that the client is fundamentally resistant, or that the client is manipulative, or that the client's account is unreliable, cannot sustain unconditional positive regard for long, and the failure will be experienced by the client as a change in the practitioner's stance. The practitioner's beliefs about the client are themselves a therapeutic instrument, and a practitioner who has not examined their own is working with a theory they have not chosen.

## 6. The Therapeutic Process

### 6.1 The client's frame of reference

The organising concept of the client-centred process is the **frame of reference**, which is the internal, subjective, psychological world of the client, including all the perceptions, thoughts, and feelings that are within the client's awareness, together with the client's own view of the situation and of the therapeutic relationship.

The practitioner's task is to enter the client's frame of reference. Rogers described this as the practitioner becoming "a companion in the exploration of the client's world," and the metaphor is exact: the practitioner does not analyse the client's experience from outside, and does not direct the client toward a predetermined destination. The practitioner explores, and the exploration is genuinely mutual — the practitioner is changed by it, and Rogers' claim is that the practitioner is changed in the direction of greater congruence and self-acceptance.

### 6.2 The process of change

The account of the process is the following, and it is a model of a mechanism.

The client is incongruent, and experiences anxiety, defensiveness, and an impaired sense of personal worth, which are the three characteristics Rogers' theory assigns to maladjustment.

The conditions of therapy are present, and the client experiences the practitioner as understanding, accepting, and genuine.

As the client experiences empathy, it becomes possible for the client to communicate the experience that has been denied symbolisation — the experience that is inconsistent with the self-concept.

The communication of the experience into a relationship with a non-defensive, accepting other allows the experience to be fully symbolised, and the self-concept is thereby reorganised to accommodate it.

With the new experience symbolised, the previous adjustment — the distorted behaviour and the anxiety — is no longer required, and the behaviours change. The person is then free to move toward fuller actualisation.

The three characteristics of the therapeutic process are therefore: a new **understanding** of the self, a new **acceptance** of the self, and a new **action** on the self. Rogers' phrase is that the client becomes able to "own" the previously disowned experience, and the three are sequential and causally connected.

### 6.3 The role of the practitioner

The practitioner's function is not to interpret, direct, advise, reassure, or challenge. Rogers' list of what the practitioner should not do is instructive: the practitioner should not argue, interpret, evaluate, or approve or disapprove of the client's expressions, and should not reassure. The practitioner should instead communicate understanding, communicate acceptance, and communicate congruence.

The oft-repeated summary — that the practitioner is a "facilitator" — has misled a generation of students, who have taken it to mean a passive and technique-free stance. The person-centred practitioner is anything but passive. The work of providing accurate empathy under time pressure, in the presence of a young person who is hostile or withdrawn, is among the most demanding things a clinician can do.
