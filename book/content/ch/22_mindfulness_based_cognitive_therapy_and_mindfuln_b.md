## 7. Case Illustration: The Boy Who Was Not Worried Enough

**The referral.** Ravi, seventeen, is referred by a school counsellor because of "low mood and not engaging." Ravi's grades have fallen from excellent to adequate; he has stopped going to the gym, which he attended five times a week; he has been "flat" for two months. He has no history of treatment. He is quiet, polite, and gives short answers. He says: "It's fine. I'm just tired of everything."

**The first session.** The practitioner asks the standard questions. Ravi's answers are notable for their flatness and for the absence of the usual content. "Are you sleeping?" "Fine." "Eating?" "Fine." "What's the mood like?" "Fine." "What's been going on?" He shrugs. "Nothing, really."

The practitioner does not pursue the interview. She says: "Can I ask you something a bit strange? Would you be willing to close your eyes for about three minutes and just notice what's going on in your body? You don't have to do anything with it. Just notice."

Ravi looks doubtful and then agrees, and does it.

After two minutes he opens his eyes. "That was weird." "What was weird about it?" He thinks. "I could feel my face. I hadn't noticed my face for a while." "Anything else?" He hesitates. "My chest felt tight. And my hands." "What about your hands?" "They felt like they were buzzing."

**The formulation.** The practice has produced the first substantive data in the case. Ravi's flatness is not a symptom that is being concealed; it is a **narrowing of attention** so total that he is not in contact with his own experience. He is not depressed in the way that a depressed person is; he is *absent* — a developmental and attentional state that is consistent with a young person who has been managing a difficulty by attending to everything except himself, and who has now run out of the ability to do even that.

**The course.** Ravi is offered the eight-week group. He asks whether it is "like meditation" and says that he tried a meditation app once and found it "boring and stupid." The practitioner says: "It is a bit like that for some people, and you are allowed to find it boring for the first three weeks and then tell me it is boring. The rule is that you turn up."

He comes to eight. He is a poor meditator, in the sense that his attention wanders constantly and he finds the body scan irritating. He says in week three: "I don't think my brain does this." The practitioner says: "Which part?" "The not-thinking part. I can do the noticing part, sort of. But there's a bit where you are supposed to not think, and I can't." "Do you want to know what the noticing part is for?"

**The teaching moment.** This is the pivot of the case, and the practitioner takes it. "I want you to notice something about yourself, over the next fortnight, and I want you to notice it without trying to change it. You have been in here three times and you have said 'fine' to almost everything I have asked you, and I do not think you are being rude or that you are lying. I think that something is going on where the questions stop and the noticing starts. And I think you have got so good at not-noticing that the noticing is the thing you have lost, and it is the only thing that is going to get you back."

**The informal practice.** The brief practice they agree on is this: three times a day, Ravi stops, puts his hand on his chest, and takes one breath, and notices one thing — one sensation, one thought, one feeling. He is told that it will feel like nothing. He says: "That's a low bar." "It's a very low bar. The point is not to achieve anything. The point is to stop."

**The developmental framing.** The practitioner also makes the developmental point explicitly, because it is what makes the work possible with a seventeen-year-old: "You are at the age where your brain is being rebuilt, and this is the part of the rebuild that goes wrong when the wiring for attention and for noticing is under-used. The thing I am asking you to practise is the thing your brain is best at learning at the moment, and it is the thing that will make the rest of it easier."

**The outcome.** Over the eight weeks, Ravi's flatness lifts. He does not become eloquent about his feelings; he becomes able to report them, in short, unimpressive sentences, which is a considerable achievement. He says in week six: "I feel sad, actually. Quite a lot. I didn't know." The practitioner says: "That is good news, although it does not feel like it." He says: "It is horrible." "It is. It is also the noticing part working."

He returns to the gym, at reduced frequency, and his grades recover partially. He does not return to "fine."

**The teaching point.** The intervention was a practice and a question, and the question was the important part: "Why do you think I am asking you to notice your face?" The most common error with a flat, shut-down young person is to treat the flatness as resistance and to push harder, and the second most common is to treat it as depression and to medicate it. The third possibility — that the flatness is an attentional state that has become automatic and that the first task is to restore contact with the interior — is the one that a mindfulness-informed practice makes available, and it is the one that Ravi's own experience confirmed.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

**The evidence is strong for one specific population and one specific purpose, and much weaker elsewhere.**

### 8.1 The relapse prevention evidence

The evidence for mindfulness-based cognitive therapy in the prevention of depressive relapse in adults is the strongest in the mindfulness literature, and it is also among the stronger bodies of evidence in the whole field. The 1990 trial and its replications compared mindfulness-based cognitive therapy with the continuation of antidepressant medication in patients who had responded to medication, and found substantial reductions in relapse rates. Subsequent meta-analyses have confirmed the effect, with a relative reduction in relapse of approximately 40 to 50 per cent in the pooled samples.

The limitations of this evidence should be stated. The trials were conducted in adults, predominantly in specialist settings, and the comparator — the continuation of antidepressant medication — is a conservative and relatively strong comparator, so the effect is a genuine and important one. The treatment was delivered by therapists trained in both mindfulness and cognitive therapy, which is a high level of training, and the effect sizes in routine services are smaller.

### 8.2 The adolescent and child evidence

The evidence for the mindfulness-based approaches with young people is substantially weaker and should be handled with more care.

A substantial number of controlled trials have been conducted with children and adolescents, in school settings and in clinical settings, with conditions including anxiety, depression, ADHD, trauma symptoms, and behavioural difficulties. The meta-analyses report small-to-moderate effects, with the effects on anxiety and on depressive symptoms the most consistently reported. The methodological quality of the studies is generally moderate, and several systematic reviews have raised the same concerns: the small samples, the heterogeneity of the protocols, the short follow-up periods, the high risk of bias in the primary studies, and a substantial risk of publication bias.

Two specific cautions apply. The first is the **developer allegiance** problem, which is substantial in this literature, and the second is that the "mindfulness intervention" in the literature is a heterogeneous category, ranging from a three-session information leaflet on the physiology of stress to a full eight-week course in a specific format, and the pooling of these is methodologically problematic.

**What the evidence supports.** That structured mindfulness-based programmes can produce small-to-moderate improvements in anxiety and depressive symptoms in young people, that they are acceptable, that they can be delivered in schools, and that the mechanisms proposed — attentional control, decentering, and the reduction of reactivity — are measurable and are associated with outcome.

**What the evidence does not support.** Any claim that mindfulness is superior to established treatments. Any claim that it is a substitute for the evidence-based treatments for specific conditions. Any claim that the effect sizes observed in the research literature will be reproduced in a service, where the participants are not volunteers.

### 8.3 The comparison with the established treatments

The comparison with the cognitive-behavioural treatments is instructive. In the head-to-head trials in adults, the mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and the cognitive-behavioural therapies produce comparable outcomes on depression and anxiety, with the mindfulness-based approach sometimes showing an advantage in the follow-up and sometimes in the measures of well-being. The honest summary is that the two are broadly comparable, with different emphases, and that the mindfulness-based approach's distinctive contribution is the relapse prevention evidence, which the cognitive approach has not matched.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of the Mindfulness-Based Approaches

**Strengths.** The evidence for the prevention of depressive relapse is strong and clinically important, and the therapy is the best-supported example of a treatment that works by changing the young person's relationship to their own experience rather than by changing the content of that experience. The practice is accessible, the taught skills are simple, and the brief informal practice is genuinely usable in a school day. The approach fits the somatic and the trauma fields, and the compatibility is real rather than fashionable. The developmental fit with adolescents is good, and the attentional training is well matched to the developmental period. And the practices are free of the requirement that the young person verbalise their difficulties, which is a considerable advantage with the young people who most need help.

**Limitations.** The evidence for the clinical applications with young people is weak, and the field's confident tone is considerably ahead of its data. The practice can be performed rather than learned, and a young person can produce the observable form of mindfulness indefinitely without any change. The contamination of the practice by the requirement to perform is a serious methodological problem for the whole literature. The approach is culturally marked, in a way that is discussed below and is more serious than is generally acknowledged. The engagement rate in the research is low, and the young people who are most in need are the ones least likely to volunteer for a meditation course. And the theory is thin, in comparison with most of the theories in this book, which is both its weakness and, arguably, the source of its clinical flexibility.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 The cultural and religious dimension

The mindfulness-based approaches were developed in the West, in a clinical context, by a practitioner who had studied a specific Buddhist contemplative practice, and the literature has engaged with the origin of the practices in a variety of ways — from denial, through appropriation, to an increasingly sophisticated engagement with the question of what it means to take a practice out of its religious and ethical context and use it clinically.

The ethical issues are real and a student should be able to state them.

**The question of provenance.** A practice that is embedded in a religious tradition, with a cosmology, a community, a lineage, and a set of ethical commitments, is used in the clinical setting stripped of most of these. The clinical practitioner is rarely in a position to transmit the tradition, and the result is a technique without its frame, which is a legitimate concern and one that the secular mindfulness researchers have themselves raised.

**The question of cultural fit.** The clinical literature on the cultural adaptation of mindfulness interventions with minority populations is small but growing, and the studies that exist have found that the adaptations that are necessary are substantial: the language, the metaphors, the practice's relationship to the body, the family's involvement, the framing of the difficulty, and the relationship between the practice and the young person's own spiritual or cultural resources. A practice that is delivered without adaptation and that is understood by the young person as a foreign practice of a different kind of religion is unlikely to be taken up.

**The question of the young person's own framework.** A young person who has a religious practice of their own — which is a large number of young people globally — may find the mindfulness approach either compatible and clarifying, or a secular alternative they have been taught to distrust, or a valuable secular technique that they can use alongside their religious practice. The practitioner's responsibility is to ask, and the clinical literature on this is very thin.

**The question of the body.** The practices are, in their standard form, practices of interior attention, and this is a substantial cultural variable. In a context in which the body is understood as a site of danger, of impurity, or of the inappropriate, or in which the interior life is understood to be accessible only through a spiritual authority, the practice may be inappropriate. And in a young person with a trauma history, the interior attention is specifically reactivating, as Section 5.3 stated.

### 10.2 The ethical requirements

**Informed consent must include the provenance.** A young person and their parents should be told that the practice comes from a contemplative tradition, and should be told what has been changed and what has not. A practice that is presented as a neutral, secular technique when its provenance is a specific religious tradition is a misrepresentation, and a misrepresentation in a domain in which the young person and their family may hold strong commitments.

**The trauma-related requirements.** The preparation, the titration, and the monitoring of the young person's response are ethical requirements and not merely technical preferences, and the somatic and trauma-informed literature is clear that interior attention practices with a young person who has a trauma history require preparation, monitoring, and the availability of alternative grounding.

**The requirement not to substitute the practice for the work.** The practice must be used in the service of the young person's developmental task and of the relationships and situations in which the difficulty is maintained, and the practitioner must be alert to the young person who has found a way to use the practice as a way of not engaging.

**The equity requirement.** The practice is a practice of attention, and attention is a resource. A service that offers it to the young people who can attend an eight-week group and who can afford the associated time and transport is delivering a treatment that is systematically unavailable to the young people with the greatest need. This is the equity argument that Chapter 50 develops, and it applies here with particular force because the intervention is group-based and time-intensive.

**The ethical value.** The ethical value of the approach should also be recorded. It is a treatment that offers a young person a relationship with their own experience that does not require an adult's interpretation, that does not require them to be able to explain themselves, and that treats their attention as their own and not as a deficit. That is a considerable offer to a young person who has been told all their life what is wrong with them.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Mindfulness** — Attention to the present moment with a non-judgemental quality, together with an orientation of the self toward the present moment; the definition developed by the contemporary researchers.
- **Attentional framework** — The account of mindfulness as comprising attention and orientation, which locates the practice as a change in the orientation of the self rather than merely as a change in the content of attention.
- **Decentering** — The capacity to perceive a thought or feeling as a mental event rather than as a fact, and the capacity to reduce its believability or impact.
- **Reperceiving** — The capacity to see a situation from a range of perspectives, including the perspective of the wider system and of one's own history; a trainable capacity and a mechanism of change.
- **Reactivity** — The conditioned tendency to respond to stimuli in a habitual way; the mechanism that mindfulness reduces.
- **Response flexibility** — The capacity to respond appropriately rather than habitually.
- **Mindfulness with acceptance** — The capacity to be present with an unpleasant experience without suppressing it or reacting to it.
- **The eight mechanisms** — Attentional control, attentional awareness, decentering, reperceiving, reduction of reactivity, response flexibility, mindfulness with acceptance, and values-based action.
- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR)** — Kabat-Zinn's eight-week course, developed in a medical clinic, with the body scan, sitting meditation, and mindful movement as its formal practices.
- **The body scan** — A 45-minute practice of attending to the body from the feet to the head; the practice most frequently adapted for work with young people.
- **Mindful movement** — A practice of gentle, deliberate movement with the attention on the sensations; frequently the most accessible practice for a young person.
- **Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT)** — The eight-week group programme developed for the prevention of depressive relapse, combining mindfulness practice with the cognitive model and with a personalised formulation.
- **The depressive relapse formulation** — The session-one working document identifying the young person's pattern of relapse: the situation, the thoughts, the feelings, the behaviours, and the consequences.
- **The relapse prevention plan** — The structured document specifying the personal warning signs, the internal and external coping strategies, the vulnerable situations, and the plan for seeking help; reproduced in Appendix F.
- **Brief informal practice** — A short, in-the-moment attentional practice, adaptable to a few minutes and usable in a school day.
- **Contemplative psychology** — The research literature on the developmental and neurological correlates of contemplative practice, and the better empirical foundation for the clinical work.
- **Adapted mindfulness** — The culturally and developmentally modified delivery of the practices, with modification of language, metaphors, the relation to the body, the family's involvement, and the framing of the difficulty.
- **Attention as a resource** — The recognition that the capacity to attend is unevenly distributed, and that an intervention which trains it is therefore an equity issue.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Mindfulness entered clinical psychology in the late 1970s through the stress reduction clinic at the Massachusetts General Hospital, and its clinical proposition — that a person trained to attend to the present moment is less at the mercy of the reactivity that maintains distress — was for a long time unfashionable. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy was developed in the 1990s for one purpose, the prevention of depressive relapse, and the randomised trial that established it is one of the very few studies in the field that produced a large effect on a clinically important outcome.

The mechanism the tradition proposes is the development of decentring, the capacity to observe thoughts and feelings as events in the mind rather than as facts about the world, together with reperceiving, the capacity to see a situation from a range of perspectives. The contemporary research literature has identified a set of eight mechanisms, of which attentional control, decentering, and the reduction of reactivity are the most directly relevant to work with young people.

The stress reduction course is an eight-week course with a body scan, a sitting meditation, and a mindful movement as its formal practices and a specific didactic content; the cognitive therapy course adds the depressive relapse formulation in session one, the recognition of relapse signs in session six, and the relapse prevention plan in the final session, which is one of the most practically useful clinical documents in the field.

The developmental fit with young people is good — the practice is developmentally congruent, it connects to the somatic work, it does not require the young person to talk, and it matches the adolescent's developmental task of self-regulation — and the modifications are concrete: brief practices, translated language, the body scan adapted to standing or seated, and the peer group. The cautions are equally concrete, and the most important is the trauma caution, which the clinical field has been slow to state.

The evidence is strong for the relapse prevention application and much weaker for the clinical applications with young people, where the meta-analyses report small-to-moderate effects in studies of generally moderate quality with substantial allegiance and publication bias. The cultural and religious provenance of the practice is a real ethical question that the field has engaged with inadequately, and the case of Ravi shows the practice doing what it does best, which is to give a young person who has become absent from his own experience a way back in.

## 13. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Describe the origin of the mindfulness-based stress reduction course and the clinical observation that produced it.
2. State the attention and orientation components of the definition of mindfulness and explain the significance of the non-judgemental quality.
3. Define decentering and contrast it with cognitive fusion.
4. State the eight mechanisms and identify which three are most relevant to developmental work.
5. Describe the structure of the eight-week course, including the formal practices and the didactics.
6. Describe the three modifications that mindfulness-based cognitive therapy makes to the stress reduction course and state the significance of each.
7. Explain the adaptive of the body scan, the sitting practice, and the mindful movement for a depressed adolescent.
8. State the developmental modifications required for the mindfulness-based approaches with young people.
9. State the trauma-related caution and the four requirements that follow from it.
10. Summarise the evidence for the mindfulness-based approaches with young people, and state three methodological concerns about it.
:::

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 22.1 — The Body Scan (30 minutes, individual, weekly)

Practise the body scan every day for a fortnight, in the standard form or a shortened form, and keep a brief note of what you notice and what you find difficult. The purpose is not to achieve a state but to become familiar with the practice as it is actually done rather than as it is described. The notes are the useful artefact, particularly the notes on what you avoided.

### Activity 22.2 — The Relapse Prevention Plan (90 minutes, individual, written)

Write your own relapse prevention plan using the template in Appendix F: the warning signs, the internal coping strategies, the external coping strategies, the vulnerable situations, and the plan for seeking help. Then, in pairs, read one another's plans and give the feedback you would give a young person. The exercise produces a document that most students find surprisingly useful and that they continue to use, and the pairing produces the feedback about clarity that the individual exercise cannot.

### Activity 22.3 — The Provenance Conversation (75 minutes, pairs)

In pairs, one student plays a sixteen-year-old from a family for whom the family's religious practice is central and who has been sent to an eight-week mindfulness course by a school counsellor, and the other plays the counsellor. The counsellor's task is to explain the provenance, the adaptation, and the alternatives without implying that the family's practice is inferior. The exercise is the most important preparation for the cultural work in Chapter 48, and it is much harder to do than it looks.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the practice.** Read Kabat-Zinn (1990) for the course and Segal, Williams, and Teasdale (2002) for the relapse prevention work, and then Williams, Teasdale, and Segal (2014), which is the most usable of the three with young people.

**For the evidence.** Read the Goyal and colleagues meta-analysis, and then read one of the critical reviews of the mindfulness literature with attention to its methodological criticisms. Then read the adolescent systematic reviews with the same attention. The contrast between the confident public accounts and the actual state of the evidence is the most important thing a student can learn from this chapter.

**For the mechanisms.** Read the research literature on the eight mechanisms, and read the contemplative psychology literature on the developmental trajectory of attention and metacognition. The mechanisms are the substance of the therapy, and the developmental literature on them is the most practically useful part.

**For the cultural and ethical questions.** Read the literature on the adaptation of mindfulness for minority populations and on the provenance question, and read the somatic and trauma-informed literature on interior attention with young people who have been abused. The two together constitute the most careful preparation a practitioner can have for this work.
