
## 8. Exposure with Children and Adolescents

The adaptations for young people are not cosmetic. Each of them follows from a developmental fact, and a student should be able to state the fact and the adaptation.

### 8.1 Cognitive capacity and the prediction–outcome comparison

The central mechanism of contemporary exposure is the comparison of the young person's prediction with the observed outcome, and this mechanism requires a capacity to form a prediction, to hold it, and to compare. That capacity is still developing in a twelve-year-old and is weaker still in a young person with a learning difficulty or an intellectual disability.

The adaptations follow directly. The prediction is made explicit and written down, or drawn, so that the comparison is concrete rather than retrospective. The comparison is made immediately after the exposure rather than at the next session. The scale for rating distress is a visual analogue scale or a numbered scale rather than a verbal report. And with young people whose capacity for prediction is limited, the emphasis shifts further towards the behavioural components — the modelling, the graded practice, the repetition — which do not require the prediction at all.

### 8.2 The developmental gradient of the fear

Young people's fears are not distributed evenly across the developmental range, and the form the fear takes predicts the treatment.

**Early childhood.** Fears are concrete, specific, and largely realistic: monsters, the dark, thunder, doctors. Treatment is largely parental and involves graded exposure by the parent with the therapist as consultant, and it works well.

**Middle childhood.** Fears become more elaborate and more social. School phobia, often with somatic presentation, is a characteristic middle-childhood presentation, and the treatment involves a graded return to school with a specific first-day design, coordination with the school, and often a plan for the parents, whose anxiety is frequently the barrier.

**Adolescence.** Fears become more abstract and are more often embedded in identity: performance fears, social evaluation fears, fears about the future. The cognitive component of the exposure becomes more prominent, and the work on the feared self-image becomes more important, because at this age the feared outcome is frequently not a stimulus but an identity — being thought to be weak, thought to be a failure, thought to be nothing.

### 8.3 The developmental gradient of the control of the situation

A young person's control over the exposure varies enormously, and the treatment must be built around the actual degree of control.

**A very young child has no control.** The parent or the teacher controls the exposure entirely, and the therapist's role is to coach the adult. This is a common and underestimated form of behavioural work with young people, and the skill required is coaching adults rather than treating children.

**A mid-child has partial control.** The young person can choose the pace within a structure that others set, and a good treatment uses this: the adult establishes the non-negotiable elements, the young person controls the rest.

**An adolescent has substantial control, and the treatment should reflect it.** The exposure is designed collaboratively, the young person sets the goals, and the therapist's role is explicitly consultative. An exposure imposed on an adolescent is likely to fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the theory.

### 8.4 The role of the parent

The parent's role requires explicit thought, and the literature is clear that the parent's anxiety is one of the strongest predictors of poor outcome in the exposure treatment of children's anxiety.

Three practical rules follow. First, the parent is coached before the exposure begins, on what to say, what not to say, and how to respond to the young person's distress, and the coaching is specific. Second, the parent's own distress is addressed directly and compassionately, because a parent who is frightened of the school will inadvertently signal that the school is dangerous, and the young person will learn to read that signal. Third, the parent is given a role that is useful rather than a role that is anxious, which usually means a specific practical job in the exposure rather than a watching brief.

There is an important caution about the parental accommodation literature. Sustained parental accommodation of a child's anxiety is now understood to be a maintaining factor rather than a supportive one, and parents frequently need to be helped to stop accommodating. This is a hard message to deliver, and it should be delivered with the recognition that the parent has been doing the accommodating out of love, and with a great deal of support.

### 8.5 School refusal

School refusal is one of the most common referrals in school counselling and one of the most heterogeneous, and the behavioural framework requires that it not be treated as a single condition.

A functional analysis of the school refusal is the first step, and it distinguishes at least four patterns: refusal maintained by **escape** from a specific aversive school situation, often a bullying situation or a particular class; refusal maintained by **anxiety** about a specific social or performance demand; refusal maintained by **attention**, in which the attendance pattern has become a negotiation with the parents; and refusal maintained by **somatic or medical symptoms**, in which the child is genuinely unwell and the school is the setting in which the illness is expressed.

Each of these has a different treatment, and the commonest clinical error is to treat all of them with the same graded return to school, which works for the escape-maintained case and fails for the others.

**The first-day design for school refusal** has a specific and well-described form: the return to a very brief, highly structured, and rewarding visit, often on a day when the feared class is not timetabled, and with a specific activity, a specific adult, and a specific reward. The young person returns to the building, does one specific thing, and leaves having had a good experience. The second visit is longer. The plan is written down and shared with the school, and the school must be ready to receive the visit.

### 8.6 Social anxiety and the school environment

Social anxiety in the school setting requires a plan that extends beyond the therapy room, because the feared situations occur in corridors, at break, in the dining hall, and in the classroom, and the treatment must involve the whole school.

The components of such a plan typically include: a designated safe adult; a pre-arranged escape route that the young person can use without it being a visible event; a rehearsal of the specific interactions with the peers concerned; a graded ladder of classroom participation; and, in many cases, a group-based social skills intervention, which is more effective than individual work for social anxiety and has the additional benefit of normalising the difficulty.

### 8.7 Adaptive and maladaptive response styles

Young people vary in how they respond to exposure, and three response styles are worth distinguishing.

**The healthy response** is fear that rises, peaks, and falls, with the young person remaining in the situation and observing the decline.

**The avoidance response** is the young person who is distressed, who then asks to leave, and who is allowed to leave. The escape is the treatment failure, and it is the most common one.

**The agitated response** is the young person who is distressed and who becomes increasingly agitated, aggressive, or distressed to a degree that the session cannot continue. This response requires a different approach, usually involving a lower first-day dose, a greater emphasis on modelling, and a slower grade.

A student who can distinguish these three responses in a session has acquired a clinically important skill.

## 9. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Would Not Take the Lift

**Presentation.** Tomas is fourteen. He has not used the lift at his school for two years, and the consequence is that he arrives late every day, misses the first lesson, and is spoken to by the pastoral team. His mother reports that he will use the stairs at home, including in a six-storey building, but refuses the lift "because of the people."

The referral came from the school, which describes the problem as defiance. The first session with Tomas establishes that it is not.

**The assessment.** Two questions are asked before anything else is planned.

*What happens in the lift?* Tomas says that the lift is small, that there are usually other students in it, and that he is sure someone will notice him and say something. He describes in detail a sequence in which a group of boys from another year enter the lift, look at him, and start laughing. When asked how often this happens, he says "every time."

*What happened the first time?* This question takes some time, and the answer that emerges is that in his first year at the school, a group of students had been in the lift and one of them had said something about his shoes. He has never re-entered the lift alone since.

The anxiety and the avoidance are now clear, and the functional hypothesis is stated explicitly: **the behaviour — using the stairs — is maintained by escape from the feared social situation, and the escape is reinforced by the absence of the feared outcome and by the relief of the anxiety, and the young person's belief that the situation is genuinely dangerous is maintained by the absence of any disconfirming evidence, which the avoidance guarantees.**

**The plan.** The plan is built around the fact that the feared outcome is social, that the feared situation involves a small enclosed space with an uncontrollable group of peers, and that the young person has no evidence about what actually happens.

The first-day exposure is designed to be small and to be a success.

*Preparation.* Tomas is shown the lift interior in the school reception area, with a staff member standing beside it, and he walks past it twice. That is the whole of the first day. It is small enough that it is almost trivial, and it is selected because it will succeed.

*The second exposure.* On the following day, Tomas enters the lift with the pastoral support worker, goes down one floor, and exits. The counsellor has agreed in advance what will be said if anyone laughs, and has established with the head of year that there is a specific plan for the handling of any incident, because a plan that depends on nothing happening is not a plan.

*The third and fourth exposures.* Tomas enters the lift with a peer he chooses — a friend who does not make him more anxious — and stays for two floors. The friend's presence is a safety signal in the early phase and the purpose is to establish that the situation is survivable with another person present, and the plan to build toward the unaccompanied exposure is explicit from the start.

*The unaccompanied exposure.* By the second week, Tomas enters the lift alone, and the primary and secondary work of the treatment begins: the "checking" of the feared outcome, in which Tomas is asked after each exposure what actually happened, and the two behaviours he had never done, which are the ones that the avoidance was protecting him from. The first time he rides the lift with a group of boys in it and nothing happens, the session is over.

**The cognitive work.** The exposure is not only behavioural. Two beliefs are examined explicitly, because the exposure gives the young person the opportunity to disconfirm them and the therapist's job is to help him notice that he has.

*The catastrophic belief.* "Everyone will laugh." The check: after each exposure, what actually happened? The accumulated evidence is that in eleven exposures, one boy made a comment about a game he was playing on his phone. Tomas is asked to rate how much that comment had in common with his prediction. The discrepancy is enormous, and it is the discrepancy that constitutes the learning.

*The self-image belief.* "I am the kind of person who gets laughed at." This is the deeper target and the one that is not resolved by a single successful exposure. The work is on the difference between the old identity — which was constructed in a specific incident in a specific year — and the young person's current account of himself, and it is done collaboratively, in the manner of the narrative work of Chapter 36.

**The school plan.** Tomas still arrives late, because the school day starts with a first lesson and he cannot realistically be at school before it. The school plan addresses this directly: the pastoral team arranges for him to have a quiet breakfast in the room of the form tutor before the first lesson, with the tutor available but not requiring conversation. The aim is to remove the punishing consequence of the lateness, which had been adding a substantial daily burden to the anxiety, and to prevent the avoidance from being reinforced by a second mechanism — the relief of not being noticed.

**The outcome and the interpretation.** Tomas uses the lift alone within three weeks, with an occasional deliberate re-exposure, and the work continues on the social evaluation belief for a further two months. His lateness continues and the school has agreed not to pursue it, which was a decision that required the counsellor to make the case for, and which was correct: the pursuit of the punctuality target would have provided a strong reinforcement for the avoidance.

Two features of this case generalise. The first is that the referral question ("defiance") and the presenting problem (avoidance) had nothing to do with each other, and that the functional assessment is what separated them. The second is that the exposure alone would not have been sufficient, because the young person needed a specific plan for the school environment, and because the plan for the lateness was as important as the plan for the lift. Exposure works within a context, and the context is usually doing as much work as the exposure.

## 10. Evidence and Research Support

### 10.1 The status of the evidence

The evidence for exposure-based treatments is among the strongest in the psychological literature, and it is strongest where the theory would predict it to be strongest: for specific, well-defined fears, with a clear functional relation between the stimulus and the fear, and with the avoidance behaviour as the target.

The key findings are as follows.

**Exposure is the best-supported treatment for specific phobias.** The evidence base is large, the effect sizes are large, and the treatment is brief by any standard.

**Exposure-based treatments are effective for panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder**, and they are the first-line treatment for all three in most international guidelines, with the protocols specified in detail.

**Prolonged exposure is effective for post-traumatic stress disorder** in adults, with more variable evidence in children and adolescents, and the developmental adaptations are described in Chapter 39.

**The efficacy of exposure does not depend on the young person's insight.** The single most important finding in the comparative research is that psychoanalytic interpretation and exposure produce different results, with exposure markedly superior on the outcome measures that matter, and the finding has been replicated.

### 10.2 The evidence with children and adolescents

The evidence with young people is substantial and has grown considerably.

**Individual, clinician-guided exposure** has a large literature across the anxiety disorders of childhood, with meta-analyses reporting large effect sizes for the specific phobias, for social anxiety disorder, for generalised anxiety, and for separation anxiety. The age range extends down to young children in the case of the specific phobias, with parental involvement.

**Parent-delivered exposure** has good support in the specific phobias of childhood and has the advantage of generalising into the family environment, where the feared stimulus usually lives.

**School-based exposure programmes** have good support in school refusal and in social anxiety, and the group formats that include a teacher or a peer are more effective than those that do not.

**Intensive, residential, or clinic-based programmes** for severe school refusal have good support and are an important part of the service landscape, though they are resource-intensive and their availability varies.

**The treatment of OCD in children and adolescents** with exposure and response prevention has a strong evidence base, and the developmental modifications — which are considerable, and which are described in Chapter 25's companion material and in the OCD sections of Part VI — are well specified.

### 10.3 The component studies

The treatment is not a monolith, and the component literature is more instructive than the outcome literature.

**Repeated exposure beats single exposure**, which is the most firmly established component finding, and it is the foundation of the relapse-prevention work.

**Homework between sessions improves outcome**, and the finding holds across anxiety disorders and across age groups. The mechanism is generalisation.

**Predicting the outcome and checking it improves outcome**, and this is the finding that most clearly identifies the modern protocol as different from the classical desensitisation.

**Attention to the young person's predictions improves outcome**, and the studies show that the prediction–outcome discrepancy is the mechanism.

**Working with the therapist present is better in the early phase and worse in the later phase**, and the finding supports the standard clinical progression.

**In vivo is superior to imagery** in the specific phobias, and the finding is now unambiguous.

### 10.4 Limitations of the evidence

Four limitations should be stated honestly.

**The literature is dominated by individual, outpatient, motivated families.** The trials that generate the evidence are largely conducted in well-resourced settings with motivated participants, and the generalisation to a school caseload with reluctant families and logistical constraints is a genuine question.

**The follow-up periods are often short**, and the clinical observation — that the gains are maintained but that a proportion of young people relapse, and that a booster session is effective — is a clinical finding that the trials do not always capture.

**The measures are largely self-report**, which is a particular problem with children, whose reports of their own distress are unreliable in the direction that flatters the treatment, and the field's response has been the use of behavioural observation measures, of which the school-based avoidance measure developed by clinicians and researchers in this area is the most widely used.

**There is a subgroup for whom exposure does not work well**, including young people with significant developmental disability, young people whose anxiety is a secondary phenomenon of an untreated trauma or of a family system that is continuing to be unsafe, and young people with high levels of parental accommodation. These are not failures of the treatment; they are indications that the assessment was incomplete.

## 11. Strengths and Limitations of Exposure-Based Work

### 11.1 Strengths

**It is the best-evidenced treatment in the field.** For specific phobias and for several other anxiety disorders, exposure has more evidence behind it than any other intervention, and this is a statement about the quality of the research as well as about the effectiveness of the method.

**It is brief and economical.** Many young people are treated in a small number of sessions, which matters greatly in services with long waiting lists.

**It is portable.** The principles transfer to any setting, and the method requires no equipment, no special room, and no specialist apparatus.

**It is compatible with other approaches.** A young person can receive exposure within a cognitive therapy, a behavioural therapy, a family therapy, or a school-based service, and the exposure component is identifiable and specific within it.

**It gives the young person something.** A successful exposure produces a piece of evidence, and the young person keeps it. The therapeutic alliance is often established in the first session by the treatment itself.

### 11.2 Limitations

**The early sessions are unpleasant.** The treatment works by producing distress, and a young person who is asked to endure distress deserves a practitioner who is honest about the fact and who is prepared to sit with them while it happens.

**The therapeutic relationship can be damaged by the treatment.** There is a period in every course of exposure in which the young person experiences the therapist as the person who is making them do the thing they most fear. The alliance usually recovers, and it recovers faster where the therapist has prepared the young person for that reaction in advance.

**It is easily misapplied.** A treatment that involves deliberately provoking fear can be used in ways that damage the young person, and the field's safeguards are designed to prevent exactly that. The recent history of poorly controlled internet-delivered exposure with adults is a warning, and with young people the stakes are higher.

**It does not address everything.** Exposure treats the fear. It does not repair a family system, undo a trauma, treat a depression, or resolve a conflict about authority. Chapter 33 and Chapter 38 address the cases in which the fear is embedded in a system that must also be addressed.

**The young person's motivation is a precondition, and it is not always present.** A young person who has no interest in overcoming the fear, or who is in an environment where overcoming it would be dangerous, is not a suitable candidate for the treatment as delivered, and the assessment of motivation is part of the assessment rather than an afterthought.

## 12. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 12.1 Culture, fear, and the meaning of avoidance

The question of what a young person is afraid of, and what the fear means, is culturally patterned in ways that the exposure model must not overwrite.

**Some fears are collectively held and socially sanctioned.** A young person who is afraid of the dark, of particular foods, of particular religious or cultural practices, or of particular social obligations, may be reflecting a shared and respected understanding of the world rather than an individual pathology. A treatment that extinguishes such a fear without understanding its social context removes the young person's connection to a community, and the young person will be supported in maintaining the fear by everyone around them.

**Some fears are real.** A young person who is afraid of a particular adult, of a particular neighbourhood, of a particular school corridor, or of a particular person in the family, is making a reasonable assessment of a real danger, and an exposure programme that confronts that stimulus without a safety assessment is a serious ethical failure. Every exposure plan requires an explicit check of whether the feared outcome is possible, and the discipline's protocols are explicit that exposure is contraindicated where the threat is real.

**Avoidance can be a cultural value.** Familial and community norms about modesty, about respect for elders, about appropriate interaction, and about the visibility of distress all affect what a young person will do in a waiting room, in a classroom, in a shop, and in a group of peers. The exposure plan has to be compatible with the young person's cultural context or it will be undone by it.

**The culture of the school system matters.** A school in which public behaviour is highly visible and where a young person's distress is noticed by everyone will present a different exposure problem from a school in which it is not.

### 12.2 The ethics of deliberately producing distress

The ethical analysis of exposure is unusually rich, and a student should be able to argue it.

**The core claim.** Deliberately inducing distress in a young person in order to remove distress is defensible only when four conditions are met: the disturbance is not needed to live a full life (that is, the fear is genuinely impairing); the person has consented to the specific procedure, with the capacity to withdraw; the intervention is expected to work, on the evidence, and the professional is competent to deliver it; and the distress is not being produced for the convenience of the practitioner or the institution.

**The consent condition.** Consent to a treatment in general is not consent to a specific exposure, and the consent must be renewed as the treatment proceeds, because the nature of the exposure changes as the hierarchy ascends. A young person who consented to sitting in the corridor may not have consented to a group of peers being assembled in front of them, and the difference matters.

**The capacity to withdraw.** A young person must be able to stop the exposure, and must be able to do so without the withdrawal being treated as a failure of treatment or as evidence about them. A practitioner who takes personally a young person's request to stop will find that they get more, not fewer, completed exposures, because the young person knows that the option is real.

**The proportionality condition.** The intensity of the exposure must be proportionate to the impairment. A young person with a mild school anxiety and a young person unable to leave the house are not receiving the same treatment, and the second case usually requires a more careful, more gradual, and more supported approach than the first.

**The non-maleficence condition.** The exposure must not produce harm. Where the feared outcome is possible, where the young person has a vulnerability that the exposure would activate, or where the exposure would exceed the young person's developmental capacity, the procedure must be modified or abandoned.

**The right to a second opinion.** A young person, or a family, who has serious concerns about an exposure programme should be able to obtain one, and the practitioner should be able to name a colleague who could provide it.

### 12.3 Specific ethical considerations with young people

**The parent's role in the decision.** Where the parents are the ones who are refusing the treatment on the young person's behalf, the ethical question is usually not about the parent but about what is at stake for them, and it is worth exploring what the parent's own fear or expectation is.

**Withdrawal in a school setting.** A young person who withdraws from an exposure in school needs the withdrawal to be handled in a way that does not create a public event, and the school needs a written plan for this in advance, because it will happen.

**Cultural and religious considerations.** Where the young person's family has a practice that the exposure programme would disrupt — a diet, a fasting period, a practice of avoidance that has religious significance, a gender-related expectation about contact with opposite-sex people — the programme must be designed around it. This is a practical matter as much as an ethical one, and the practitioner should ask.

**Documentation.** The exposure hierarchy, the young person's consent, the agreed stopping points, and the review points should be documented, because the programme is unusual enough that a careful record is a professional necessity, and because a record is the young person's protection as much as the practitioner's.

## 13. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Exposure** — The planned, systematic presentation of a feared stimulus, in conditions in which the feared outcome does not occur, for the purpose of producing new learning about the relationship between the stimulus and the outcome.
- **In vivo exposure** — Exposure conducted in the actual physical context in which the fear occurs; the default modality for most specific phobias with young people.
- **Imaginal exposure** — Exposure using constructed images, scenes, or narratives, used where the real stimulus is unavailable.
- **Interoceptive exposure** — The deliberate induction of feared bodily sensations, used with panic and somatic presentations.
- **Virtual reality exposure** — Exposure conducted in an immersive virtual environment, used where the real stimulus is dangerous, scarce, or expensive.
- **Exposure hierarchy** — A collaboratively constructed, individually specific, and graded list of feared situations with subjective distress ratings, ordered from most to least tolerable.
- **First-day design** — The practice of beginning treatment with a single, small, planned, and bounded exposure to the feared stimulus, chosen to succeed, before substantial assessment or homework.
- **The law of effect applied to fear** — The principle that avoidance is negatively reinforced by the relief it produces, and that the avoidance therefore maintains the fear.
- **Inhibitory learning** — The account of exposure as the learning that the feared outcome does not occur, which supersedes rather than erases the original learning.
- **CS–US association** — The learned association between the conditioned stimulus and the aversive unconditioned stimulus, the interruption of which is the aim of exposure.
- **Safety signal** — A stimulus that reliably accompanies the feared situation and thereby signals safety; its use is helpful in the early phase and must be faded.
- **Safety-seeking behaviour** — Behaviour that reduces the perceived danger at the cost of maintaining the fear; a primary target of exposure.
- **Avoidance** — The behaviour of escaping or postponing a feared situation, negatively reinforced by the relief produced; the principal maintaining factor in anxiety disorders.
- **Behavioural experiment** — A collaborative test of a specific prediction, designed so that the belief is genuinely at risk of disconfirmation.
- **Prediction–outcome discrepancy** — The comparison of the young person's expected outcome with the observed outcome; the mechanism by which the learning occurs.
- **Exposure protocol** — A manualised specification of the elements and rules of an exposure treatment, with defined sessions, homework, and review points.
- **Prolonged exposure** — A structured protocol for the treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder, with a focus on the emotional processing of the traumatic memory.
- **Exposure and response prevention** — The behavioural treatment of obsessive-compulsive disorder, combining exposure to the obsession with the prevention of the compulsion.
- **Grade of exposure** — The degree of difficulty assigned to a particular exposure, calibrated so that it is challenging but achievable.
- **Retrieval work** — Repeated re-exposure across time and contexts, designed to protect the learning against spontaneous recovery and relapse.
- **Modelling** — The demonstration of a behaviour by another person, through which the observer acquires or performs the behaviour.
- **Participant modelling** — A graded procedure in which the therapist models the feared behaviour and then guides the young person through it with progressively less assistance.
- **Co-participant modelling** — Modelling in which the model is a peer or a group member rather than the therapist.
- **Vicarious reinforcement** — The increase in the observer's performance of a behaviour following the observation of a model being reinforced for it.
- **Observational learning** — Bandura's account of learning through attention, retention, and reproduction, without direct reinforcement of the observer.
- **Behavioural rehearsal** — The graded practice of a behaviour in a low-risk setting, with feedback, before performance in the natural setting.
- **Instruction** — The explicit teaching of a target behaviour, its rationale, and often a script; the first of the four components of skills training.
- **Behavioural skills training** — A structured four-component method for teaching a specific interpersonal or self-management skill: instruction, modelling, rehearsal, and homework.
- **Homework** — The planned practice of a target behaviour in the natural setting between sessions; the generalisation step of skills training and of exposure.
- **Target behaviour** — The specific, observable behaviour that a treatment is intended to produce, defined in operationally countable terms.
- **Avoidance of the exposure** — The premature termination of an exposure, which is the commonest form of treatment failure and which is reinforced by the relief it produces.
- **Agitated response** — A pattern in which distress escalates to a degree that the session cannot continue, requiring a lower dose, more modelling, and a slower grade.
- **Parent-delivered exposure** — Exposure in which the parent is coached to conduct the hierarchy, which generalises the learning into the family environment.
- **Parental accommodation** — Sustained parental modification of family routine and rules in response to the child's anxiety, which is now understood as a maintaining factor.
- **School refusal** — A pattern of persistent non-attendance at school that requires functional analysis before treatment, because at least four distinct maintaining patterns are recognised.
- **Relapse prevention** — The set of procedures designed to maintain gains after the end of treatment, including booster sessions, retrieval work, and written plans.
- **Booster session** — A planned follow-up session after the end of treatment, offered to consolidate gains and to address early signs of return.
- **Proportionality** — The ethical requirement that the intensity of an intervention be proportionate to the severity of the impairment it addresses.
- **Informed assent** — The young person's own agreement to a specific procedure, given with the capacity to decline and to withdraw.
:::

## 14. Chapter Summary

Exposure is the most successful behavioural intervention in the history of the profession, and it emerged from a theoretical dispute whose resolution was methodological as much as theoretical. The psychoanalytic account held that a phobia was a symbol; the learning account held that it was an acquired and maintained fear. The comparative evidence showed that the treatment that followed from the learning account worked and the treatment that followed from the symbolic account did not, and the field took from this the lesson that a treatment which does not relieve the symptom does not work, whatever its theory.

The five principles of exposure were stated in this chapter: the feared stimulus must be encountered; the feared outcome must not occur; avoidance must be prevented; the exposure must be repeated; and the young person must do it themselves. Each was examined in relation to the developmental context, and the chapter developed the specific adaptations that follow from the cognitive capacity of the young person, the developmental gradient of the fear, the gradient of the young person's control over the exposure, the role of the parent, and the school context.

The varieties of exposure — in vivo, imaginal, interoceptive, and virtual — were described with their indications and limitations, and the hierarchy and the first-day design were set out in detail, because the details are what make the treatment safe. The rules of exposure practice were specified, and the single most important of them is the prevention of avoidance, since the premature termination of an exposure is the commonest treatment failure and is itself reinforced.

Modelling was described through Bandura's mechanisms of attention, retention, and reproduction, and its adaptation for young people was set out with the emphasis on credibility, multiple models, and the demonstration of discrepancy between prediction and outcome. Behavioural rehearsal and behavioural skills training were described in their four components, and the chapter traced the components to their theoretical origins, making explicit that skills training is where the behavioural and the cognitive traditions are most completely integrated and that it does not deal with motivation, relationship, or developmental context.

The case of Tomas illustrated the separation of a referral question about defiance from a presenting problem of avoidance, the construction of a graded exposure with a planned check of the feared outcome, the work on the self-image belief, and the necessity of a school plan that included a decision not to pursue the punctuality target, since its pursuit would have provided a strong reinforcement for the avoidance.

The evidence is among the strongest in the literature for the specific phobias, for panic disorder, for social anxiety disorder, and for obsessive-compulsive disorder, and is substantial and growing for the anxiety disorders of childhood and adolescence. The component studies are more instructive than the outcome studies, and they identify repeated exposure, homework, the prediction–outcome check, and attention to predictions as the elements that make the difference.

The ethical analysis is the richest in the book, because the treatment deliberately produces distress. The conditions of defensibility are the necessity of the distress, the consent to the specific procedure, the expectation of benefit, and the absence of institutional convenience. The cultural analysis adds the point that some fears are collectively held and some fears are real, and that an exposure programme that confronts a real danger without a safety assessment is a serious ethical failure.

22. Summarise the component findings about repeated exposure, homework, checking the feared outcome, and the prediction–outcome comparison.
23. State the four limitations of the exposure evidence base.
24. State the four conditions under which deliberately inducing distress in a young person is defensible.
25. Explain the two cultural considerations that may make an exposure plan inappropriate or require its modification.

## 16. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 25.1 — Building a First-Day Exposure (75 minutes, individual)

Choose a young person you know or a case from placement who is avoiding something. Build the smallest possible first-day exposure: what it is, how long it lasts, who is present, what is said beforehand, what the young person is told they will feel, what the stopping point is, and what the young person does immediately afterwards. Then write the sentence you would say to the young person to introduce it. The purpose is to discover how hard it is to make the exposure small enough, and most students find that their first design is much larger than the one they end up writing.

### Activity 25.2 — The Prediction–Outcome Table (60 minutes, individual)

Take a fear of your own — a genuine one, however small — and complete ten rows of a table with the columns: situation, predicted outcome, predicted distress (0–100), actual outcome, actual distress, and the discrepancy. Do not complete the table from memory at the end; fill in each row immediately after the event. Then write what you would now believe if you were to design a similar table for a young person. The exercise demonstrates the mechanism, and it also demonstrates how much of the anticipatory dread is produced by the absence of a record.

### Activity 25.3 — Skills Training Rehearsal (90 minutes, small group, three roles)

In groups of three, one student plays a fourteen-year-old who is afraid of raising a problem in front of a teacher, one plays the teacher, and one plays the skills trainer. The trainer must run the four components — instruction, modelling, rehearsal, homework — on the target behaviour of asking a question in a lesson. Then swap roles so that each student plays the young person. The role of the young person is the instructive one, because it exposes the difference between a behaviour that has been rehearsed and a behaviour that has been modelled, and the difference between a young person who is coached and a young person who is instructed.

### Activity 25.4 — The Refusal and the Withdrawal (60 minutes, pairs, discussion)

In pairs, one student plays a parent who is convinced that their thirteen-year-old's refusal to attend school is a deliberate act of defiance and that the treatment should involve consequences, and the other plays the counsellor. The counsellor's task is to establish the maintaining contingencies without dismissing the parent's account, and to explain the parental accommodation literature without implying that the parent has been doing the wrong thing. Then reverse roles. The exercise is the best preparation in the book for the first family meeting in a school refusal case.
:::

## 17. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the treatment.** Read Wolpe (1958) for the origin and Bennett-Levy and colleagues (2016) for the modern protocol. The distance between the two is the history of the field in one comparison.

**For the evidence.** Read the meta-analyses of exposure with children and adolescents and read the component reviews on homework and on the prediction–outcome check. The component reviews are more useful clinically than the outcome reviews, because they tell you which parts of the protocol to protect when time is short.

**For the child-specific adaptations.** Read O'Connell (2009) in full. It is the most practically usable of the child-focused exposure texts and it addresses the first-day design, the school-based work, and the parental role in a form that can be used directly.

**For the case conceptualisation.** Read the chapter on anxiety disorders in the child-focused clinical texts and read the school refusal literature, which contains the most useful functional analyses available in the applied literature.

**For the ethics.** Read the ethical guidelines for the behavioural approaches and the critical literature on exposure with minors, and read the material on parental accommodation, which has implications well beyond exposure therapy.
