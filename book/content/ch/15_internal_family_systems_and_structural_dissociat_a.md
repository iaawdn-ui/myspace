# Internal Family Systems and Structural Dissociation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the development of internal family systems therapy and the influence of structural dissociation theory on it.
- State the three levels of experience in the internal family systems model and distinguish the Self from the parts.
- Describe the five categories of internal parts — Exiles, Managers, Firefighters, Self, and the ubiquitous Outsiders — and the function of each.
- Explain the concepts of blending, unblending, the 8 Cs of Self, and their clinical use.
- Describe the six-step therapeutic process and the role of the practitioner in it.
- Apply internal family systems thinking to a case involving a young person, and specify the developmental modifications required.
- Evaluate the evidence for internal family systems therapy, and state the current status of the randomised controlled trial evidence.
- Explain the ethical requirements that the model generates, particularly with respect to the young person's autonomy and to the treatment of difficult behaviours.

## Chapter Overview

Internal family systems therapy was developed by Richard Schwartz in the 1980s, initially as a way of working with the eating disorders and substance abuse that Schwartz encountered in his clinical work, and it has since developed into one of the most widely used integrative therapies in the field. Its central claim is that the mind is naturally multiple — that each of us is made up of a number of distinct subpersonalities, or parts, each with its own perspective, its own strategy, and its own history — and that the symptom is the result of a conflict among the parts rather than a failure of a single unified self.

The theory is unusual in the profession in that it is explicitly and deliberately religious in its origins and has subsequently developed a secular, non-spiritual version for clinical use, and in that the two versions have been somewhat confusingly run together in the literature. The model described in this chapter is the secular clinical model, and a student should be aware that the "Self" in the model is a specific clinical construct with a specified set of attributes and should not be confused with any metaphysical entity. Chapter 48 returns to this in the context of the profession's general avoidance of metaphysical claims.

The theory's relevance to work with young people is considerable and is not always exploited. The parts model provides a language for the young person's internal conflicts that is immediately comprehensible, that does not require the young person to be verbally sophisticated, and that locates the symptom as a solution. The developmental application is in its early stages and the evidence base for it is thin, and both of these facts should be stated by the student rather than glossed.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The clinical problem that produced the theory

Schwartz's original clinical work was with women with eating disorders and with substance abusers, and the observation that produced the theory was that these patients were not internally consistent. They were, on the contrary, in a state of conflict between powerful internal forces that were pulling in different directions, and the eating disorder and the substance abuse were, on this reading, the products of a conflict rather than the expression of a single disordered state.

This observation was strongly reminiscent of a specific body of theory that Schwartz encountered in the clinical literature, and that connection is the second origin of the model.

### 1.2 Structural dissociation

The theory of **structural dissociation**, developed principally by the Dutch trauma researchers Jan van der Hart, Antonio Onno van der Hart, Sandra Aeltsma and their colleagues, and drawing on Pierre Janet's nineteenth-century work, proposes that a person exposed to overwhelming trauma is unable to integrate the traumatic experience into the whole of the personality. What happens instead is a division of the personality into two or more relatively autonomous "action systems": the **apparently normal part**, which continues to function in the ordinary world and has no access to the traumatic memories and the associated affect, and the **emotional part**, which is fixated in the trauma, is dominated by the traumatic affect, and is temporally located in the moment of the trauma.

The theory's account of the symptom is that the two systems are in conflict, and that the symptom arises from the conflict and from the burdens carried by the emotional part. The account of the treatment is that the work is to enable the integration of the two systems, which requires establishing a relationship with the emotional part and helping it complete its unfinished action.

The theory is important to this chapter for two reasons. It provided Schwartz with a theoretical account of the internal multiplicity he was observing, and it has provided the wider field with a rigorous and increasingly influential account of the mechanism of dissociation that underlies many of the presentations that come to youth services. Chapter 38 develops the structural dissociation material in the context of trauma, and Chapter 40 develops it in the context of stabilisation.

### 1.3 The development of the model

Schwartz's initial formulation described three categories of part — Exiles, Managers, and Firefighters — and was derived from his clinical work with eating disorder and substance abuse patients. He later added the **Self** as a distinct entity with its own attributes, and the model was further developed by the addition of the category of Outsiders, by the development of the six-step process, and by the subsequent work on the treatment of families and schools, on the online delivery of the therapy, and on the treatment of psychosis.

## 2. The Three Levels of Experience

The model is described in terms of three levels of experience, and a student should be able to describe the relationship between them precisely.

**The unblended Self** is the core of the model. It is a distinct level of experience that is not a part and that has its own qualities and its own developmental trajectory. It is the level from which the practitioner works, and the goal of therapy is that the client develop more of it.

**Parts** are discrete aspects of the person's experience, each with its own perspective, its own emotional style, its own agenda, and its own history. A part is not a symptom, and it is not a disorder; it is a subpersonality with a legitimate adaptive function and a strategy that was once appropriate.

**Blending** is the condition in which a part's perspective is running the show, so that the person is *in* a part rather than *has* a part. Blending is a normal and necessary condition of daily life; the clinical problem is the degree of blending and the fact that the person has lost access to the Self while it is occurring.

### 2.1 The Self and the 8 Cs

The Self is described in terms of eight qualities, and the 8 Cs is the practical handle on it. The Self is **curious**, **calm**, **compassionate**, **confident**, **connected**, **courageous**, **clear**, and **committed**.

The 8 Cs are not aspirational virtues; they are descriptive of a state of experience that the practitioner can learn to recognise in the client and to cultivate in themselves. The Self is characterised by **emotional curiosity** — a genuine interest in the parts and in what they are protecting — by **emotional calm** — the capacity to be present with intense affect without being overwhelmed by it — and by **emotional compassion**, which is directed at the parts rather than at the behaviour. The three emotional Cs are what the practitioner works with most directly, and the working definition of the therapeutic stance in the model is "a curious, calm, and compassionate relationship with a part."

The Self is not a metaphysical entity and should not be presented as one. A secular reading of the Self is as the person's own adult, reflective, perspective-taking capacity, operating within the developmental and relational context in which it was formed — a reading that is close to the account of the self as a process in the person-centred tradition and to the account of reflective functioning in the mentalisation-based tradition discussed in Chapter 42.

## 3. The Categories of Part

### 3.1 Exiles

**Exiles** are the parts that carry the young person's wounds: the experiences of hurt, fear, shame, abandonment, and injustice that were inflicted on them and that they were not able to process at the time. Exiles are the youngest parts, and they are the parts most vulnerable to the external environment. They are often hidden, and the young person may have no conscious awareness of them until the therapeutic relationship provides sufficient safety for them to emerge.

An Exile in a young person carries the emotion of a specific past experience. A sixteen-year-old who was repeatedly humiliated at school may carry an Exile that feels like the humiliation itself, and the part may be as young as the child who experienced it, and it may have a correspondingly young vocabulary.

### 3.2 Managers

**Managers** are the parts whose function is to prevent the Exiles from being triggered, and their strategies are proactive. A Manager may be the part that plans, that organises, that controls, that achieves, that analyses, that works, that keeps the peace, that suppresses, or that withdraws. The Manager's function is protective and is aimed outward, at the prevention of the Exile's activation.

In a young person, the Manager strategies are the strategies of a child who has had to manage a difficult environment: the pleasing, the high achievement, the silence, the compliance, the caretaking of the parent, the use of humour, the withdrawal. The Manager is the part of the young person that has learned to be impressive, and the developmental cost of that is the loss of the parts that were not impressive.

### 3.3 Firefighters

**Firefighters** are the parts whose function is to extinguish the fire of the Exile's activation, and their strategies are reactive. The Firefighter may be the part that dissociates, that numbs, that bingeing or purging, that drinking or using, that raging, that suiciding, that cutting, that zoning out, that acting out, or that over-eating. A Firefighter's strategy is urgent, disproportionate, and aimed at the immediate reduction of the Exile's affect.

The distinction between a Manager and a Firefighter is a matter of timing and of direction, and it is the most useful single distinction in the model for work with young people: a Manager prevents; a Firefighter reacts. A young person who is a perfectionist at school and who bingees at night is displaying both, and the two are in a relationship in which the Manager's success generates the conditions for the Firefighter's activation.

### 3.4 Outsiders

**Outsiders** are the parts that maintain the appearance of things, and they include the part that criticises the system, the part that is the sceptic, the part that wants to make sure the therapy is right, and the part that is depressed by the work. The Outsider's function is to keep the system safe from the intensity of the work, and in clinical practice the Outsiders appear as the young person's persistent scepticism about the therapy, the part that says "this won't work", the part that intellectualises the whole thing, and the part that wants to quit.

The Outsiders are the least discussed category in the clinical literature and the most practically useful with adolescents, because the adolescent's scepticism about therapy is often a legitimate Outsider rather than a resistance, and a practitioner who treats it as resistance will fail.

### 3.5 The Symptom as the Solution

The model's account of the symptom is the one that makes it clinically powerful: **the symptom is the solution of a conflict among the parts.** The symptom is what the system produces in order to resolve the conflict, and it is a solution in the only sense that matters clinically — it works. It reduces the conflict, and the young person can function. The price is the continuing conflict and the continuing expenditure of the young person's resources, and the price is often paid in development, in relationships, and in the body.

The clinical corollary is important and is frequently mishandled: the symptom should not be attacked, and the parts that produce the symptom should not be treated as the enemy. They are protectors, they are doing their jobs, and the work is to understand what each is protecting against and to find a way for the system to be reorganised so that the protection is no longer required.

## 4. The Therapeutic Process

### 4.1 The six steps

**Step 1: Mapping.** The practitioner and the young person work out the internal world of the young person — the parts, their roles, their relationships, and the conflicts among them. The mapping is done through the young person's own language and their own images and feelings, and the practitioner does not impose a structure. The visual mapping can be used with young people, and the use of drawing is an important development in the child and adolescent applications.

**Step 2: Connecting with the Self.** The practitioner helps the young person find, and to be in, the Self, and the working is through the 8 Cs. In practice this is done by attending to curiosity, calm, and compassion, by asking questions that invite the young person's own perspective on the parts, and by noticing when the practitioner or the young person has slipped into a part.

**Step 3: Understanding the protectors.** The practitioner works with the Manager and Firefighter parts, understanding what they are protecting the Exile from, and what they believe is necessary. The work with protectors is respectful and curious, and it is the part of the process that most requires the practitioner to be free of a part's own perspective — the protective part will describe the young person as a defective or dangerous one, and the practitioner must hear that description without accepting it.

**Step 4: Healing the Exile.** When the Self is sufficiently established, the practitioner helps the young person connect with the Exile, and the Exile is allowed to bring its story, its emotions, and its memory. This is the most affectively intense step, and it is the step at which the model's account of dissociation becomes clinically critical, because in a young person with a trauma history the Exile is a part of a dissociated system, and the connection must be made with great care and at a pace the young person can tolerate.

**Step 5: Retrieving.** The Exile's burden is retrieved and the Exile is reoriented towards its original caretakers or sources of the pain. The idea is that the pain belongs to those who caused it, and the Exile can be released from it. The procedure involves allowing the Exile to give back what it was given, and to be shown, in the therapeutic relationship, a different outcome from the one it has been expecting.

**Step 6: Reworking.** The parts are reorganised so that the conflicts are resolved and the old strategies are no longer required. The young person is left with the Self, with access to the parts, and with the capacity to choose among them.

### 4.2 Unblending

The concept of **unblending** is the model's core technical idea and it is what makes the approach more than a re-description. Blending is a normal condition and the goal is not to eliminate it but to increase the young person's capacity to notice it and to step out of it. The practitioner works with the young person to develop a "distance" from each part: to experience the part's feelings and thoughts while also experiencing themselves as the one who has them.

Practically, this is done by attunement to the shifts in the young person's state, and by naming what is happening: "It sounds like the part that has to keep everything under control is here right now. Can you hear yourself saying that?" The naming gives the young person a choice that they did not have in the moment of blending, and the capacity to exercise that choice is the mechanism of change in the model.

### 4.3 The practitioner's position

The practitioner's position in the model is a specific one, and it is different from the person-centred position. The practitioner works from the Self, and the practitioner is a *real* part of the client's system, which means that the practitioner can become blended with a part of the client's system. A practitioner who is caught up in the young person's Firefighter may become the protector who is being sought, and a practitioner who is caught up in the young person's Manager may become the achievement-oriented adult who colludes with the over-functioning. The model's contribution to the therapist's self-awareness here is real and it is one of the more practically valuable things in the theory.

## 5. Internal Family Systems with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The developmental fit

The developmental fit of the model with young people is good, and there are three specific reasons.

**It externalises the internal.** For a young person, the most difficult thing about their own mind is that it is a jumble and that they cannot get any distance from it. The parts model provides a structure in which the young person can have a view of their own mind from the outside, and the use of drawing and of externalised representation makes the structure available to a young person who cannot hold it verbally.

**It is non-blaming in a way that young people can hear.** The young person who has been told they are "attention-seeking" or "defiant" or "in denial" can recognise, in the model, that the part that is doing these things is a protector with a function, and the recognition is often the first moment in which the young person feels understood.

**It is compatible with the developmental reality of the self.** The model's account of the Self as a capacity that develops and that can be temporarily lost is developmentally congruent, and it maps well onto the developmental literature on the adolescent's developing reflective functioning.

### 5.2 The developmental modifications

**With younger children**, the work is conducted through play, drawing, and image, and the parts are represented as characters in the young person's own imagery. The child who has a "worried one" and a "brave one" is already doing parts work, and the practitioner joins the child's metaphor rather than translating it into adult language.

**With adolescents**, the model is used in a more explicit form, and the specific development is the use of visual mapping. The adolescent who can draw a system of parts and see the relationships among them has made a significant step, and the practitioner can then work with the drawing as the shared object.

**With young people in crisis**, the model has a specific and important contribution: the crisis is understood as a Firefighter in action, and the first task is to connect with the Self, which is the part of the young person that is not in crisis. A young person in acute crisis is in a part, and the practitioner works to find the Self that is not — which is a different and more tractable task than trying to talk to the young person out of the crisis.

### 5.3 The specific cautions

**The Exile work.** The step in which the young person connects with the Exile and tells the trauma story is the highest-risk intervention in the model with a young person, and the developmental and ethical requirements are strict: adequate stabilisation, adequate capacity to remain in contact, a young person who can consent meaningfully to a procedure they understand, a practitioner competent in the work, and a support structure that will be in place afterwards. The structural dissociation literature is explicit that approaching the emotional part of a dissociated system without adequate stabilisation can retraumatise.

**The risk of the model being experienced as a past-life or possession framework.** A young person who is religious, or who has a spiritual framework, may hear the parts language in a way the practitioner does not intend, and the secular model should be explained carefully. Conversely, a young person who has been told by a family member that they are "possessed" or "demonic" may find the parts language a relief. The practitioner should ask rather than assume.

**The risk of over-intellectualisation.** The model is a framework for understanding, and a young person with a good intellectual grasp can learn the vocabulary of parts and use it to talk about their difficulties without ever being in contact with them. The Practitioner must watch for this, and the clue is whether the young person's affect changes in the session.

## 6. Modern Developments

The theory has developed in several directions that are relevant to a graduate student.

**Internal family systems for families.** The model has been extended to work with the whole family system, in which each family member has a set of parts and the family's symptoms are understood as the product of the interaction of the parts across the members. This is a considerably more complex application than the individual model, and it is developing rapidly.

**Internal family systems in schools.** A school-based model, "school-based internal family systems," has been developed and trialled, and it is the development most directly relevant to the student in this course, because it adapts the model to a brief, time-limited, educational setting with a young person who may have limited trust and limited continuity.

**Online internal family systems.** The delivery of the therapy online, developed in response to the pandemic, has produced a substantial research literature, and the findings are relevant to Chapter 55.

**Internal family systems for psychosis.** The model has been applied to the treatment of psychosis, and this application is significant because it is the clearest instance in the contemporary field of a therapy organised explicitly around the premise that the person's experience is not a set of symptoms to be eliminated but a set of parts in conflict, and because it engages directly with the experience of the person with psychosis rather than with the symptoms.
