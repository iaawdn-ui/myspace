# Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain Bowlby's account of attachment, including the behavioural control systems, the function of attachment, and the conditions of its formation.
- Describe the research tradition that established the attachment patterns, including the strange situation and the Adult Attachment Interview.
- State the four attachment categories in children and the three in adults, and describe the developmental implications of each.
- Explain the construct of the internal working model and describe its developmental trajectory.
- Describe the principles of attachment-based therapy and its main variants, including the Child and Family Traumatic Stress Intervention and the dyadic developmental psychotherapy.
- Apply the attachment framework to a case involving a young person in a family with a history of loss or maltreatment.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of attachment-based work, including the questions of the cultural specificity of the attachment categories and the status of the caregiver's own attachment history.

## Chapter Overview

Attachment theory is the developmental foundation on which much of the practice of this book rests, and it is the theory with the most robust empirical base in the developmental sciences. It is also, in the form most practitioners encounter it, the theory that has been most simplified and most misused.

The theory's central claim is that attachment is a **behavioural system** — a set of evolved, functional, and biologically prepared systems that the infant uses to regulate affect, to explore, and to form a model of the world and of the availability of others. It is not, in the form in which it is often described, a claim about love, and it is not a claim about dependency, and the practitioner who treats it as either will be working with the wrong construct.

The claim that matters most for this book is that the attachment relationship is the organising context of the child's behaviour, and that the internal working model built from that relationship — the expectations about the availability of others, about their trustworthiness, and about the self as worthy or unworthy of care — is carried forward into the child's understanding of other people and of themselves. The adolescent who is unable to trust, who expects to be abandoned, who cannot accept comfort, and who believes that needing anyone is dangerous, is presenting a working model, and the working model was built somewhere.

The theory has also been the source of a serious scientific dispute, and the dispute should be known: the question of whether attachment patterns are categorical or dimensional, the question of the cross-cultural universality of the patterns, the question of the relationship between attachment and the individual-differences measures, and the question of whether the adult attachment interview measures the same construct as the strange situation. The discipline's handling of these disputes is itself a lesson in how a field should treat its own instruments.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 From psychoanalysis to ethology

John Bowlby trained in psychoanalysis and was, for most of his career, in substantial opposition to the psychoanalytic tradition. His central objection was methodological: psychoanalysis proposed a set of developmental stages and a set of intrapsychic mechanisms that were, in his phrase, "unknowable" and therefore untestable, and the claim that the mother was the sole and exclusive object of the infant's attachment was, in his view, supported by evidence that was of a piece with the phrenology of the nineteenth century.

Bowlby's answer was to turn to ethology — the study of animal behaviour — and to look at the actual behaviour of mammals. What he found was a set of behavioural systems that were universal across the species and that had clear functions: **attachment**, which provided a safe base from which to explore and a refuge from threat; **feeding**, which was also the physiological system for the growth of the body; and **avoidance and exploration**, which constituted the other half of the human behavioural repertoire.

The functional argument was that the attachment system is **selected for** because of its consequences: infants attached to their mothers survived. The claim of the 1960s papers was that the attachment relationship was a **primary** system, a species-typical system present from birth, biologically prepared, and not a derivative of feeding.

### 1.2 The critical period and the monotropy hypothesis

Bowlby's developmental claims were the ones that caused the most controversy. He proposed a **critical period** in the first eighteen months to two years during which the attachment relationship is formed, and a **sensitive period** thereafter in which the relationship can still be formed but more rigidly. And he proposed **monotropy** — that the infant forms a single, primary, exclusive attachment, normally to the mother, and that other relationships are secondary.

The critical period claim was attacked on the grounds that it implied that separation after two years was catastrophic, and this was in turn attacked on the grounds of the effects of the orphanage studies of the 1940s and 1950s, which showed that children raised in institutions without a consistent caregiver developed profound difficulties. Bowlby's rejoinder was that the institution's failure was not the timing but the absence of a consistent figure, and the distinction matters.

### 1.3 The controversies

Three controversies dominated the field and all three are still live.

**The nature of the attachment categories.** Whether attachment is categorical — a small number of qualitatively distinct patterns, as in the strange situation — or dimensional — a continuum of scores, as in most of the self-report measures — remains unresolved, and the resolution is a matter of the measurement instrument rather than of the theory.

**The cross-cultural universality of the patterns.** The strange situation was developed in a specific social context, and its cross-cultural use produced a well-known problem: the pattern that looked "avoidant" in one setting was the pattern that looked "secure" in another, in which the caregiver's response to a stranger was appropriate. The resolution was the recognition that the strange situation measures the *child's* response to the reunion, and that the *caregiver's* response must be coded separately, and that the classification is of the child, and that the categories do not have the same meaning in every setting. This is an important methodological lesson and it is directly relevant to the cultural analysis in Section 11.

**The relationship between the child and the adult measures.** The Adult Attachment Interview measures a quite different construct from the strange situation: it measures the discourse of an adult about their own attachment history, and the categories it produces (secure, preoccupied, dismissing, and the unresolved category) have a rather different meaning from the child's categories, and the correlation between the two is modest. Practitioners who treat the two as interchangeable are making an error.

### 1.4 The move to the clinic

Attachment research moved into clinical practice through several paths. The **attachment-based family therapy** developed by the Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up (ABC) group, and the **Child and Family Traumatic Stress Intervention (CFTSI)** of the same group, translated the research into parent-focused interventions for young children who have been maltreated. The **dyadic developmental psychotherapy (DDP)** of Linehan's group, described in Chapter 42 in relation to mentalization, translated the attachment findings into a treatment for young people with attachment disruption, including those with a borderline personality disorder. And the **Mentalization-Based Treatment** of Fonagy and Bateman, described in Chapter 42, was built on the attachment research and has become the most substantial evidence-based treatment for adolescent borderline personality disorder.

## 2. The Core Theory

### 2.1 Attachment as a behavioural system

**Attachment** is a behavioural system organised around the child, activated by the child's need for protection, and functioning to provide a secure base from which to explore and a refuge from threat. The system's functions are:

**Protection.** The attachment figure provides a refuge in the face of threat, and the child's proximity to the figure reduces the physiological arousal associated with threat.

**Proximity maintenance.** The system is activated by separation and by threat, and it produces the child's seeking of the figure.

**A secure base.** The system promotes exploration from the point of security, and the exploratory behaviour is more extensive and more effective when the secure base is available.

**An affect regulation system.** The child's capacity to manage negative affect is a function of the availability of the attachment figure, and the internalisation of that capacity over the first years is the developmental foundation of the regulation of affect throughout life.

**A basis for the self.** Bowlby and his colleagues proposed that the attachment relationship is the setting in which the sense of self is constructed, and the two are complementary: the child's model of the other as a reliable or an unreliable person and the child's model of the self as worthy or unworthy of care are developed together.

### 2.2 The conditions of attachment

Three conditions are necessary for the formation of an attachment, and all three are necessary and none is sufficient.

**Availability.** The caregiver must be available and responsive.

**Sensitivity.** The caregiver must be sensitive to the child's signals, and the sensitivity must be accurate rather than perfect, because a caregiver who responds to every signal in a synchronised way produces a child with no capacity to manage the states that arise when the caregiver is unavailable.

**Affective investment.** The caregiver must be affectively invested in the child, and the child must be able to recognise that the caregiver is invested in them.

The three conditions can be separated empirically, and the separation is clinically important. A caregiver can be available and insensitive; a caregiver can be sensitive and not invested; a caregiver can be invested and unavailable. Each produces a different pattern, and each requires a different intervention.

### 2.3 Attachment and the other behavioural systems

Attachment operates in relation to the other systems and is not the only influence on behaviour.

**The exploratory system** is inhibited by attachment activation, and the child who is constantly anxious will not explore, and the exploration deficit is a common presentation.

**The caregiving system** is activated by the young person's cues and is suppressed by the young person's anger and by the caregiver's own unresolved loss. A parent whose own attachment history is unresolved may be unable to provide the caregiving that the young person cues for, and the young person's angry protest is exactly the signal that suppresses the caregiving system.

**The sexual system** develops through puberty and is not a derivative of the attachment system, and its interaction with the attachment system in adolescence produces the specific difficulties of the sexuality conflict described in Chapter 33.

## 3. The Internal Working Model

### 3.1 The construct

The **internal working model** is the set of expectations, drawn from the primary relationship, about the availability and the trustworthiness of others, about the response of others to one's own needs, and about the worthiness of the self. It is a cognitive schema that operates largely outside awareness, and it is both an interpreter of new experience and a generator of behaviour.

The three components are as follows.

**A model of the attachment figure** — as available or unavailable, as responsive or unresponsive, as trustworthy or not.

**A model of the self** — as worthy of care or not, as able to elicit care or not, as having a right to need or not.

**A model of the interaction** — whether the seeking of comfort works, whether the protest produces response, and what happens when the figure is unavailable.

### 3.2 The developmental trajectory

The working model is built in the first two to three years, and it is then **revised** rather than replaced.

**From birth to three years**, the model is built from the primary relationship. It is a relatively simple, affective, and non-verbal schema, and it is formed by the repeated experience of the infant's signals being answered.

**From three to six years**, the model is elaborated. The child can now hold a mental representation of the caregiver, can anticipate responses, can begin to use it to plan behaviour, and can develop the fantasy and the reality as separate systems.

**From six to eleven years**, the model becomes increasingly deliberate and increasingly capable of being reflected upon, and the child can be argued with, and this is a direct consequence of the cognitive development of the period.

**From eleven onwards**, the model becomes a more organised and more differentiated system, capable of multiple relationships, of ambivalence, and of reflection, and the adolescent's task is the *revision* of the model rather than its formation, and the revision is what the developmental literature calls the *secondary revision* of the working model.

### 3.3 The revision in adolescence

Two processes operate in adolescence and both are clinically important.

**The revision of the working model.** The adolescent's developmental task includes moving from the primary relationship to a wider world of relationships, and this requires a revision of the working model that permits multiple relationships of different kinds. The adolescent who has a working model in which all relationships are of the same kind and carry the same expectations cannot make the move, and the young person who remains in a pattern of intense and exclusive relationships is often presenting this.

**The acquisition of the capacity to sustain a relationship through a period of developmental change.** The working model of the childhood is a model of a relationship in which the other is stable. The adolescent's relationships are with people who are also changing, and the working model must be revised to accommodate the mutability of the other.

The clinical relevance is that an adolescent with an insecure working model will be at their most vulnerable precisely at the developmental point at which the working model is under revision, and a practitioner who interprets the adolescent's relationship difficulties as a regression is making a serious error.

## 4. The Attachment Patterns

### 4.1 The child categories

The categories derive from the **strange situation**, a laboratory procedure in which the child's behaviour is observed across a series of eight episodes in which the caregiver and a stranger are present or absent.

**Secure.** The child explores readily, is distressed on separation, is readily comforted on reunion, and is not markedly avoidant of the caregiver.

**Avoidant (insecure-avoidant).** The child does not distinguish between the caregiver and the stranger, explores readily even during separation, shows little distress at separation, and avoids the caregiver on reunion. The avoidant pattern is a defensive strategy: the child's experience of the caregiver's availability is unpredictable or the caregiver's responses are rejecting, and the child has learned that the reliable response to needing is to not need.

**Ambivalent or resistant (insecure-ambivalent).** The child is preoccupied with the caregiver, explores little, is highly distressed at separation, is angry on reunion, and is difficult to comfort. The ambivalent pattern reflects a history of caregiver behaviour that is unpredictable and inconsistently responsive, so that the child is angry about the separation and is also unable to use the caregiver on reunion.

**Disorganized.** The child shows contradictory, frozen, or dazed behaviour in the caregiver's presence, and the behaviour of the caregiver is the key to understanding it. The disorganized pattern is the one associated with maltreatment and with the loss of a caregiver, and it is described in the clinical literature as a **fright without solution**: the child approaches a caregiver who frightens the child, and the child has no organised strategy for this.

### 4.2 The adult categories

The adult categories derive from the **Adult Attachment Interview**, which is a semi-structured interview about the adult's own childhood and its significance, and which is scored for the quality of the discourse rather than for its content. The categories are:

**Secure (autonomous).** The narrative is coherent, the account of the difficulties is not accompanied by a defensive dismissal, and the person can describe the effects of their history without being overwhelmed.

**Preoccupied (anxious).** The narrative is detailed and often vivid, and the account of the past is marked by unresolved conflict, by a sense of the parent's own needs intruding, and by a difficulty in moving on from the past.

**Dismissing.** The account is minimised, the difficulties are attributed to the child or to the circumstances, and the person presents as self-sufficient and uninterested in the discussion.

**Unresolved (or preoccupied/unresolved).** The discourse shows lapses in monitoring, disorganised or contradictory accounts, and unusual responses to the losses in the person's history, and this category is associated with unresolved grief and with trauma.

The critical point for the practitioner is that these categories are *not* the adult versions of the child's categories, that they are derived from a different instrument measuring a different construct, and that the two are only modestly correlated.

### 4.3 The attachment matrix

A useful clinical instrument combines the two: the **attachment matrix** plots the young person's behaviour in the session against the attachment signals the practitioner observes in the caregiver, and the two are read against each other.

![The attachment matrix, plotting the young person's behaviour in the session against the attachment signals shown by the caregiver, with the four quadrants labelled.](fig55_attachment_matrix.png)

The reading is as follows. A young person who is **avoidant in the session** with a caregiver who is **preoccupied** is a young person who has learned to manage the caregiver's anxiety, and the intervention is with the caregiver. A young person who is **preoccupied** with an **avoidant** caregiver is a young person whose bid is being met by withdrawal, and the intervention is to introduce a consistent and responsive source of care, which is frequently a professional. A young person who is **disorganised** with a caregiver who is **unresolved** is the classic maltreatment presentation, and the intervention is the most difficult in the field.

## 5. Attachment-Based Therapy

### 5.1 The principles

The attachment-based therapies share five principles, and a student should be able to state them.

**The relationship is the treatment.** The therapy is directed at the development of a relationship in which the young person can experience the caregiver as available and responsive, and the therapeutic relationship itself is the vehicle through which the working model is revised.

**The caregiver is the target of the intervention.** The work is with the caregiver's responsiveness, because the young person's working model is built from the primary relationship, and a change in the caregiver's availability is a change in the material from which the model is built.

**The developmental task is respected.** The work does not treat the young person's behaviour as a problem to be extinguished but as an attempt to manage a situation that has been overwhelming, and the developmental appropriateness of the intervention is the requirement.

**The intervention is the creation of conditions in which the attachment system can be used.** In practice this means the removal of the conditions that suppress the attachment system — the anger that suppresses the caregiving system, the inconsistent response that teaches the child to manage the caregiver's anxiety, the maternal behaviour that teaches the child to self-soothe.

**The treatment of the caregiver's own attachment history is a component.** A parent with an unresolved attachment history will reproduce the pattern, and the work with the parent's history is part of the work with the child.

### 5.2 The variants

**Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up (ABC)** is a parent-mediated intervention for infants and toddlers who have experienced maltreatment or disrupted caregiving. The core procedure is the video-feedback intervention: the parent is filmed interacting with the child and then watches the recording with the practitioner, who helps the parent to see the child's bids and their own responses. The evidence base is good, and the mechanism is understood.

**Child and Family Traumatic Stress Intervention (CFTSI)** extends the ABC work to the treatment of trauma in children aged three to six and to their caregivers, combining the video-feedback with the teaching of specific skills: the recognition of the child's trauma-related behaviour, the reduction of the child's avoidance, the parent's own regulation, and the development of the child's attachment behaviours.

**Dyadic developmental psychotherapy (DDP)** is a parent-child attachment intervention for young people with attachment trauma and for those with emerging or established borderline personality disorder. Its distinguishing feature is the **PACE** stance — Playfulness, Acceptance, Curiosity, and Empathy — and its technique is the "follow your lead" responsive interaction in which the practitioner enters the child's world and reflects back the child's own intentions with delight. The evidence base is good and it is the most substantial of the attachment-based treatments for the adolescent population.

**Mentalization-Based Treatment** is described in Chapter 42 and is the largest evidence base of all the attachment-based approaches for adolescent borderline personality disorder.

**The general principles applied without a protocol** — the practitioner working with the caregiver's responsiveness, with the recognition of the young person's bids, with the repair of ruptures, and with the parent's own history — are the practice that most practitioners in this book will actually need, and they are what the rest of this chapter describes.

### 5.3 The practices

**The recognition of the bid.** A bid is a small, usually nonverbal, signal that the young person is seeking proximity, contact, or reassurance. The practitioner's task in the first sessions is to learn to see them, and the technique is the same as the technique used in the ABC video-feedback: the practitioner observes, names what the young person did, and names what it meant.

**The response.** The response must be prompt, and it must be congruent, and it must be about the bid and not about the behaviour. A young person who is angry at being told what to do is not making a bid and should not be responded to as one.

**The repair.** The most important single practice. When the practitioner misses a bid — and the practitioner will, and the young person will know — the repair is the opportunity. The practitioner names what happened, acknowledges the young person's response, and does not require the young person to reassure them. A practitioner who can repair a rupture with a sixteen-year-old has demonstrated more than a practitioner who has never missed one.

**The prohibition on the use of the therapeutic relationship to produce compliance.** This is a critical ethical point in the attachment-based work and it is stated explicitly in the CFTSI and the DDP protocols: the practitioner does not use the attachment relationship to secure the young person's cooperation, and the practitioner does not withhold to secure it. The attachment figure must be available precisely when it is not needed for the practitioner's purposes.

**The consistency of the response.** The response must be the same every time, and the "every time" includes the times when the practitioner is tired, and this is why the work is best done in a team.

**The work with the caregiver's history.** The parent's unresolved grief, the parent's own experience of being cared for or not, and the parent's own unresolved losses are worked with, usually in a separate strand, and the practitioner should expect that a parent whose own history is unresolved will struggle to respond consistently.
