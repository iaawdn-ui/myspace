# Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the historical development of cognitive therapy, including the role of Aaron Beck's clinical research and the relationship of the theory to Kelly's personal construct theory.
- State the cognitive model in detail, including the levels of processing, automatic thoughts, intermediate and core beliefs, and the hot spots.
- Explain Beck's cognitive conceptualisation and describe the five components of a complete formulation.
- Describe the principal techniques of cognitive therapy and state the indications and cautions for each.
- Explain the structure of a cognitive therapy session and its rationale.
- Evaluate the evidence for cognitive therapy and for its efficacy in specific conditions, and state the limits of that evidence.
- Critically evaluate the cognitive model, including the treatment of the body, of emotion, of development, and of social and structural context.
- Apply the cognitive model to a case involving a child or adolescent.

## Chapter Overview

Cognitive therapy was developed by Aaron Beck in the 1960s, initially in the treatment of depressed outpatients at the Philadelphia Veterans Administration Hospital, and it was the first treatment in the history of the profession to be specified in a manual, to be tested in a controlled trial, and to be exported for use in other countries and other conditions. It is the approach against which most of the modern evidence-based practice movement was measured, and the "cognitive model" that it proposes is the most widely used explanatory framework in the profession.

The theory's central claim is that the way a person construes a situation determines the emotional and behavioural response to it, and that a great many of the difficulties that present for treatment are maintained by the way the person thinks about themselves, their situation, and their future. The therapeutic task is to identify the dysfunctional beliefs and to test them collaboratively, in a process that Beck called collaborative empiricism, in which the practitioner and the client are co-investigators in a set of propositions about which both can be, in principle, persuaded.

The chapter presents the model in detail, works through the cognitive conceptualisation and the technique repertoire, describes the session structure, and then addresses the evidence and the criticisms. The final section is a sustained critical appraisal, because the cognitive model's virtues are well documented and its weaknesses are, in the main, well documented too, and a graduate student should be able to state both.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 Beck's research and the session model

Aaron Beck was trained in psychiatry and psychoanalytic psychotherapy, and he was in practice as a psychoanalytic therapist when he noticed something that disturbed him. His depressed patients, when asked to record their thoughts, produced material that was uniformly negative in tone — and, more strikingly, the material was *specific and plausible*, not the vague, conventional self-criticism of the psychoanalytic literature. His patients said things like "I am no good," "I am a burden," "I will always fail," and they said them with conviction and with a logic that was internally coherent.

Beck's decision to shift his attention from the content of the transference to the content of the patient's thoughts in the session was the founding move of cognitive therapy, and the method he developed to elicit it — the **session model** — is the direct ancestor of the thought record and of every subsequent cognitive technique.

The method is worth describing precisely, because it is a technique for gaining access to the process rather than the content. Beck asked his patients, in the middle of a session, to report what was going through their minds — not what they were thinking about, but the thoughts themselves, in the form in which they occurred, including the images and the sensations. He then asked them to rate each thought on a scale of believability, and he asked them what emotional response each thought produced. He then asked them to consider what the evidence for and against each thought was.

Two findings followed. The first was that the negative thoughts were in fact **available to consciousness** — they were not buried, and they were not unconscious, and they did not require an hour of free association to surface. This was a significant challenge to the psychoanalytic account of the relationship between the presenting symptom and the underlying conflict, and it was a finding that the practice of cognitive therapy was designed to exploit. The second was that the beliefs underlying the negative thoughts were **widely held and held with conviction**, and that they were not in the first instance hypotheses to be tested but convictions to be examined. Beck called them "cognitive derivatives" and later "core beliefs."

### 1.2 Kelly and the personal constructs

The theoretical foundation of cognitive therapy is George Kelly's **personal construct theory**, published in 1955, and the debt is larger than is often acknowledged.

Kelly's claim was that each person constructs a personal theory of the world through a process of bipolar alternatives, and that a person is defined not by the content of these constructs but by the *way in which they construe events*. Two features of Kelly's theory are directly important to cognitive therapy.

The first is the **fixed-role therapy** approach: the client is invited to explore alternative construings of an event, and the exploration is treated as a laboratory in which the client's construing can be tested. This is the direct ancestor of the behavioural experiment.

The second is the emphasis on the **correlational context** of a construct: a personal construct is not a proposition in the abstract but a hypothesis about a correlation, and it is therefore testable. Kelly's formulation of a personal construct as a bipolar pair — "strong–weak," "responsible–irresponsible" — with a subordinate corollate, is the direct ancestor of the way cognitive therapists work with beliefs, and it is the reason that cognitive therapy uses the *form* of the belief rather than its content.

### 1.3 The naming of the model

The term "cognitive therapy" was coined by Beck and his colleagues, partly to distinguish the approach from "rational therapy" and partly to signal a break with the psychoanalytic tradition. The name was successful and the tradition grew quickly. By the 1980s, cognitive therapy was the most widely researched psychotherapy in the world, and by the 1990s the manualised treatments derived from it — including the treatments for anxiety and for depression — were the most widely used evidence-based treatments in the world.

## 2. The Cognitive Model

### 2.1 Levels of processing

Beck's model describes three levels at which cognition may be analysed.

**The conscious level** consists of the thoughts that the person can report and that are available to reflection. It is the level at which most cognitive therapy is conducted, and it is the level at which the young person's own verbal capacity most directly constrains what is possible.

**The automatic level** consists of the rapid, habitual, and often unconscious appraisals that occur without the person's awareness and that generate the immediate emotional and behavioural response. Beck's "hot spots" are the specific automatic thoughts that are associated with the strongest emotional responses, and identifying them is the first technical task of a cognitive therapy session.

**The underlying level** consists of the more general and more deeply held beliefs, both intermediate and core, that give the automatic thoughts their structure and their power.

### 2.2 Automatic thoughts

An **automatic thought** is a self-directed internal statement that arises spontaneously in a situation, is experienced as true in the moment, and produces an emotional response. Automatic thoughts are not beliefs in the strict sense; they are situationally bound, momentary, and often adaptively functional. The young person who thinks "they are all watching me" on entering a room is having an automatic thought, not holding a belief, and the clinical point is that the difference matters: automatic thoughts are accessible, they can be examined, and they change.

The properties of automatic thoughts that matter clinically are that they are quick, that they feel true, that they are not usually noticed before the emotion, that they are often adaptive in the situations in which they arose, and that they are accessible to awareness once the practitioner points to them.

### 2.3 Beliefs

**Intermediate beliefs** are the conditional rules and assumptions that the person holds about situations, about themselves, and about other people, and that are derived from the core beliefs. They typically take the form of "if... then..." statements, and they are the level at which the young person's difficulties in specific situations are usually generated. The developmental literature is useful here: a young person's intermediate beliefs about school, about friends, and about their body are formed in the middle years and are highly specific to their contexts.

**Core beliefs** are the most general, most deeply held, and most difficult to change of the cognitive structures. Beck's classical categories are the three that have become the most familiar: **unlovable**, **worthless**, and **helpless**, with the addition of **inadequate** and **unfavourable-to-others** in later formulations. The categories are often presented as a list and the list is often used too readily. What matters is the specific content of the belief for a particular young person, and a core belief label is a shorthand for a richer clinical description that the practitioner must supply.

The properties of core beliefs are that they are global, that they are held as truths, that they are self-maintaining, that they are highly resistant to change, and that they are often held in a form the young person cannot articulate without assistance.

### 2.4 The cognitive triad

Beck's original formulation of the model was the **cognitive triad**: the belief that "I am worthless," the belief that "The world is unkind," and the belief that "My future is hopeless." The triad was the model as presented to the profession in the 1970s, and it has been substantially revised since, for three reasons: it is too simple for the complexity of the cases it is applied to; it does not describe the physiology of the response; and it does not accommodate the developmental and social context.

The revised formulation, in the contemporary model, is a hierarchical structure in which the core beliefs generate intermediate beliefs, which generate situation-specific automatic thoughts, which generate the emotional, behavioural, physiological, and cognitive responses. The three components of the older formulation are retained as "the heart, the core, and the key" of the model, but the model is no longer a triangle.

### 2.5 The hot spots

The **hot spot** is the specific situation in which a particular automatic thought produces the strongest emotional reaction, and identifying hot spots is the first technical task of the assessment and the organising structure of the therapy. The clinician's task is to find the situations in which the young person's automatic thoughts are most powerful, and to work with the thoughts in those situations, because it is in the hot spot that the cognitive system is most accessible to change.

## 3. The Cognitive Conceptualisation

Beck's **cognitive conceptualisation** is the systematic account of a case in the cognitive model, and it has five components. A student should be able to produce all five for a case.

**The core belief.** The most general belief the young person holds about themselves, their situation, or their future, and the evidence for it.

**The intermediate beliefs.** The rules and assumptions derived from the core belief, in the form of "if... then...", and the specific situations in which they operate.

**The automatic thoughts and hot spots.** The situation-specific thoughts, the emotional responses, and the point at which the emotional response is most intense.

**The behavioural and physiological responses.** What the young person does, and what happens in the body, in the hot spots.

**The coping behaviours.** The young person's strategies for managing the emotional response, and the consequences of these strategies for the maintenance of the difficulty.

The conceptualisation is not a static document. Beck's formulation is that the conceptualisation is a hypothesis that is tested in the session, and that the clinician should expect to revise it, and that a conceptualisation which does not change over the course of the therapy is a sign that the clinician has stopped attending.

### 3.1 The process of the conceptualisation

Three questions are asked in the session, and the answers constitute the conceptualisation.

**What are the words the young person uses to describe the situation?** The wording is diagnostic, because the young person's own vocabulary for their difficulty contains their cognitive structure. A young person who says "I can't" is describing a perceived incapacity; one who says "I won't" is describing a decision; one who says "I must" is describing a rule.

**What does the young person believe is going on?** The core belief is often most easily approached by asking what the young person would have to believe in order for their current situation to be what it is, and by asking what they would have to believe in order for things to be different.

**What are the consequences?** The formulation must specify the consequences of the belief in the domains of emotion, behaviour, and physiology, because the intervention is targeted at the consequences as much as at the belief.

## 4. The Technique Repertoire

### 4.1 The thought record

The thought record is the central technique, and it is worth describing with precision because the student will be expected to use it.

**The situation.** The specific situation, described in concrete, sensory terms and located in a particular place and time. The instruction to the young person is to describe the situation "as if you were watching it on film" or as if they were standing outside looking in, which draws attention to the observable and away from the interpretive.

**The emotions.** The emotions the young person felt, with their intensity rated, and the location of the emotion in the body.

**The automatic thoughts.** The thoughts that went through the young person's mind at the time, in the form in which they occurred, including images.

**The evidence for.** What supports the thoughts? This is a genuinely open question, and the practitioner should ask it as though the answer might support the young person's belief.

**The evidence against.** What does not support the thoughts? The difficulty here is that the young person frequently cannot generate the evidence against, or generates it and immediately neutralises it. The practical technique is to return to the actual situation and to ask what the young person would have expected to happen and what actually happened.

**Alternative thoughts.** What else could have been thought? The instruction is deliberately open: not "what is the rational thought?" but "what else could you have said to yourself?"

**The consequences of the alternative.** What would be different if the young person had held the alternative thought? This is a crucial step, because it converts a cognitive exercise into a motivational one: an alternative that makes no difference is not worth holding.

**The effect.** Rate the belief in the original thought now, and the belief in the alternative now.

The thought record is not a test of the young person's intelligence, and a practitioner who uses it as such will generate compliance rather than learning. The development of the technique with young people is the subject of Chapter 17.

### 4.2 Behavioural experiments

A **behavioural experiment** is a planned test of a prediction derived from a belief, conducted in the world, with the result observed. The structure is: the prediction, the test, and the outcome, with the young person's actual experience of the outcome as the data.

The technical requirement is that the experiment be a genuine test — that it be capable of disconfirming the belief. An experiment designed to confirm the belief is not an experiment. This is the point at which the cognitive therapy practitioner most often goes wrong, and the discipline is to design the test with the young person and to ask explicitly, before the experiment, "what would we see if this belief turned out to be wrong?"

The developmental adaptation of the behavioural experiment is discussed in Chapter 17, and the two points to note here are that the experiment must be small and concrete for a young person, and that the outcome must be examined in detail rather than summarised, because a partial disconfirmation is more instructive than a complete one.

### 4.3 Agenda setting, mood monitoring, and the session structure

**Agenda setting.** The beginning of each cognitive therapy session is spent on the young person's agenda — what they want to work on. The rationale is that it establishes the young person's ownership of the work and it produces a co-constructed direction that the practitioner can then pursue. The practitioner's own agenda is deferred.

**Daily mood monitoring.** The young person records their mood across a number of activities each day, which provides the data for the between-sessions work and, importantly, gives the young person direct experience of the relationship between activity and mood, which is the empirical basis of behavioural activation.

**The session structure.** A cognitive therapy session has a recognisable structure: agenda setting, mood check-in, the work on the agreed agenda, homework assignment, and a summary. The structure is not bureaucratic; it is the mechanism by which the young person learns the shape of the work and by which the between-session transfer is secured.

### 4.4 Imagery rescripting and related techniques

**Imagery rescripting** is the modification of a mental image to change the emotional response to it, and it is used particularly with young people who have intrusive images of a frightening or humiliating event. **Decentring** is a technique in which the young person is taught to consider the full range of evidence and perspectives, so that the thought is held as a thought rather than as a truth. **The continuum of change** is a technique in which the young person is helped to see a belief as a continuum rather than as an absolute, and is used particularly with the rigid, dichotomous thinking that accompanies many of the presentations in youth services.

### 4.5 The exposure of core beliefs

The most advanced technique in cognitive therapy is the work with core beliefs, and it is done through a series of behavioural experiments and through the examination of the "person's data" — the evidence that the young person has about themselves. The procedure is to identify the core belief, to specify the predictions it generates, to test those predictions, and to construct a new, more qualified belief that accommodates the disconfirming evidence without discarding the adaptive core.

## 5. The Session Structure and the Working Alliance

The cognitive therapy session has a structure, and the structure is one of the reasons the treatment is deliverable in a brief or time-limited form. The session is agenda-led, which means that the young person determines the content, and the clinician's role is to bring the cognitive model to bear on whatever the young person has brought.

The **alliance** in cognitive therapy has been studied in detail, and the findings are consistent with the general alliance literature: the alliance predicts outcome in cognitive therapy as it does in other therapies, and the alliance mediates the delivery of the specific techniques. Two features of cognitive therapy that are relevant to the alliance are worth noting.

The first is the **collaborative stance**, which is the defining interpersonal feature of the approach. The clinician does not tell the young person what to think; they examine the evidence together, and they are as prepared to be persuaded as the young person is. Beck's formulation of this is that the clinician and the young person are co-investigators, and that the therapeutic relationship should feel like a scientific inquiry.

The second is the **guided discovery**, which is the method by which the collaborative stance is enacted. The clinician asks questions, and the questions are designed to help the young person find the answer rather than to deliver it. The question "what evidence do you have for that?" is the prototype. The risk of guided discovery is that it becomes a way of avoiding a direct statement that the young person needs, and a clinician who has mastered the technique can be almost impossible to argue with, which is a problem with a young person who has a legitimate grievance about the clinician.

## 6. Cognitive Therapy in Other Conditions

The cognitive model was developed for depression, and it was applied to anxiety within a few years, with the result being the manualised cognitive-behavioural treatments for generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social phobia, and specific phobia, and later for PTSD, for eating disorders, for psychosis, for depression in young people, and for a range of developmental and health conditions.

Two developments are worth noting for a student in this course. The first is the development of the **transdiagnostic** model, in which the same cognitive mechanisms — overestimation of threat, overestimation of uncontrollability, and overestimation of the unfavourability of the self, and the corresponding attentional biases — are proposed to operate across disorders, which has led to a unified treatment for a broad range of conditions. The second is the development of the **third wave** approaches discussed in Chapters 20 to 22, which reformulate the cognitive model to emphasise the function of thoughts rather than their content.
