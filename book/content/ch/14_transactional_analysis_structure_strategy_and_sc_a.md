# Transactional Analysis: Structure, Strategy, and Script

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the origin of transactional analysis in Eric Berne's work with group therapy, and explain the four school divisions that followed.
- Explain the three levels of the personality model — Parent, Adult, and Child — and the characteristics of each ego state.
- Explain the concepts of transaction, transactional analysis, and script, and give a clinical example of each.
- Describe the three-level model of the person as it applies to structural analysis, strategic analysis, and script analysis.
- Apply the concepts of script, script matrix, and racket system to a case.
- Explain the concept of redecision and the redecision process, and state its clinical value.
- Apply transactional analysis to work with children and adolescents, and identify the developmental modifications required.
- Evaluate the evidence for transactional analysis, including the controversy over its scientific status.

## Chapter Overview

Transactional analysis was developed in the 1950s by Eric Berne, a physician and psychoanalyst who had trained in Vienna and who worked with group patients, many of them soldiers. Berne's founding observation was that his patients' accounts of their lives had a repetitive and dramatic structure, and that the structure could be described in a vocabulary of social roles, of exchanges, and of a life story. He published *Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy* in 1964 and the books that popularised the field, and the theory then split into four schools — the classical, redecisional, cathexis, and integrative — each of which has a different relationship to the underlying theory and a different evidential status.

The theory is attractive to a student because it is legible. Its vocabulary is concrete, its diagrams are clear, and it offers an account of a young person's difficulty that is easy to formulate and easy to discuss. It is attractive in a second, more troubling way, and a student should be able to name it: the theory's language of Parent, Adult, and Child maps uncomfortably well onto the categories through which adults routinely think about young people, and a naive application of transactional analysis to an adolescent can reproduce the very adultism it purports to criticise. The most common error in the clinical use of this theory with young people is the treatment of the adolescent's behaviour as a "Child" state to be corrected, and the therapeutic framework in which the adult is the "Adult" doing the correcting.

The chapter presents the theory in its historical context, works through the three-level model, the concept of script, and the redecision process, and then gives the developmental and ethical attention that this theory particularly requires.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 Berne and the group

Eric Berne's career is unusual. He trained as a psychiatrist in Vienna and came to the United States in the 1930s, and he worked in a variety of institutional settings, including a veterans' hospital where, in the early 1950s, he began to run group therapy with what he called "the blackout" — the group members' apparent forgetting of what had happened between sessions, which he attributed to a dissociation common to the group.

The observation that the "blackout" was really a failure to integrate the material from one session into the next, and that this was best understood as a "double layer" of the personality, was the founding insight. The social behaviour layer, Berne claimed, operated on a different level from the intrapsychic one, and each was observable in a person's speech. The "Parent", "Adult", and "Child" states were named in *Transactions in Group* (1955), and the theory of games followed in 1964.

### 1.2 The four schools

The split into schools is not merely a matter of emphasis; the schools have different theories and different statuses.

**The classical school**, Berne and his followers including Eric Berne's own formulations, Doyle and Weisz, and Harris. It retains the structural model of the three ego states, the analysis of transactions and games, and the script concept, and it tends toward a developmental model in which the Child ego state is the location of the script and the Adult is the location of the cure. The classical school's characteristic intervention is the **redecision**, drawn from Berne's 1972 *What Do You Say After You Say Hello?*, in which the client is helped to make a new decision in the area in which the old script was written.

**The redecisional school**, associated with Mary Lee and Eric Berne, and with the Ego State Institute, developed the redecision process into a systematic and directive method, with a specific protocol and a specific set of interventions. It is the most operationally specified variant and is the one most used in clinical practice.

**The cathexis school**, associated with Robert M. Young, Douglas Mosley, and Erskine, and with the Journal of theTransactional Analysis Association in its earlier form, treats the script as a set of emotional energies and payoffs, and its clinical work is with the emotional core of the script rather than with the decision. The cathexis school's contribution is the most substantial contemporary addition to the theory, and it is the school that has engaged most seriously with the attachment and the relational literature.

**The integrative school**, associated with James R. Allen, Leslie Fehmi, and the group around the International Transactional Analysis Association in its more recent form, integrates transactional analysis with attachment theory, with psychodynamic and relational theory, and with contemporary developmental and systemic thinking. The integrative school is where the theory currently has the most vitality and the most relevance to work with young people.

### 1.3 The popularity and the reputation

Transactional analysis is unusually popular outside the profession, and this has had a double effect. It has given the theory a public visibility that most clinical theories do not have, and it has produced a diffuse and sometimes quite inaccurate popular version in which "Adult" is a synonym for "healthy" and "Child" is a synonym for "spoiled." A student who has only met the popular version will be poorly prepared for the actual theory, and a student who uses the popular version will do harm.

## 2. The Three-Level Model

### 2.1 Ego states

The personality is described as organised into three ego states, each of which is a set of feelings, thoughts, and behaviours that are phenomenologically distinct and that are, in principle, available to awareness.

**The Parent.** The Parent ego state is an internalised representation of the parents or other early authority figures, and it operates by injunction and by permission. It provides a set of prohibitions ("you must not") and a set of encouragements ("you may"), and it is the source of the internal voice that judges the person. Crucially, the Parent is not the same as the actual parents, and the internalised Parent is frequently a composite of many authorities and is frequently out of date.

**The Adult.** The Adult is the component that observes, gathers data, organises it, and estimates the likelihood of the outcomes of various courses of action. It operates on the principle of reality, and it is the part of the personality that is neither feeling the feelings of the Child nor accepting the dictates of the Parent. Berne described the Adult as a data-processing function, and a student should note the striking similarity between this description and the information-processing account of the cognitive therapies.

**The Child.** The Child is the part of the person that feels, remembers, wants, and acts from the feelings. It has two divisions: the **Natural Child**, which is the healthy, spontaneous, joyful, and creative part of the young person, and the **Adapted Child**, which is the part that has learned to respond to the world in ways that are inappropriate, and which is where the hurts, the fears, the anger, and the compliance are stored.

The distinction between the Natural and the Adapted Child is the feature that most distinguishes transactional analysis from the popular version, and it is also the feature that makes it most useful with young people, because it locates the young person's vitality and their difficulties in the same place, rather than treating all of the Child as the problem.

### 2.2 The three schools of the person

The structural analysis of the three ego states yields three ways of analysing a person.

**Structural analysis** describes the composition of the personality: which ego states are present, which are excluded, and which are contaminated by which. A person whose Adult is contaminated by the Parent is a person who reasons in order to justify a position already taken; a person whose Adult is contaminated by the Child is a person who reasons from a need. A person whose Parent is excluded is a person with no internal standard, and a person whose Child is excluded is a person who is markedly inhibited and "proper."

**Strategic analysis** describes the person's characteristic way of operating: the recurrent patterns of behaviour in which the person organises their transactions with other people. It is concerned with the *how* rather than the *what*.

**Script analysis** describes the person's life story: the unconscious, preconscious, and conscious plans that the person has been living out, which were formed early, which are reinforced by parental injunctions and permissions, and which are played out over a lifetime, often with a predictable and self-defeating conclusion.

### 2.3 Transaction and analysis

A **transaction** is the exchange of a stimulus and a response between two ego states, in which the stimulus has a vector: social, psychological, or operational. A **transactional analysis** is the identification of the ego states involved in a transaction, and the analysis of the person's characteristic pattern of transactions.

The three types of transaction are:

**Complementary.** The response is at the same level as the stimulus and is of the same type, and the exchange is satisfactory. Two Adults exchanging information; a Parent caring for a Child.

**Crossed.** The response comes from a different level than the stimulus, and the exchange is confused and unsatisfying. A Parent giving an instruction to an Adult who responds as a Child; an Adult asking a question that is answered by a Parent's judgement.

**Ultraparallel.** The response is at the same level and of the same type but addresses a different topic, and the two people appear to be having a satisfactory exchange that is in fact taking place on a different subject. The clinical significance of the ultraparallel is that it is the most common form of covert communication in families, and it is a major mechanism in the maintenance of family patterns.

### 2.4 Games and scripts

A **game** is a recurring, structured pattern of exchange with a predictable payoff, which is not consciously planned and which is not in the person's best interests. Berne's account of games in general is now largely superseded by the more rigorous and more useful account of **script** that the cathexis and integrative schools have developed, and a student should learn the script account and treat the games material as historical.

A **script** is a person's unconscious life plan, formed in early childhood, reinforced by parental injunctions and permissions, and expressed in a predictable set of behaviours with a predictable conclusion. The script is not simply a set of symptoms; it is the organising story of the person's life, and the therapeutic task is to change the ending.

### 2.5 The script matrix and the racket system

The **script matrix** is the account of the three levels of the script matrix system: the underlying beliefs or injunctions (the "don't" and the "be"), the corresponding script beliefs or positions, and the corresponding behaviours. The **racket analysis** is the account of a person's characteristic "racket" — the set of covert, illegitimate, and unhealthy means by which the person seeks to get what the script promises, as distinct from the legitimate means by which the same goal might be achieved. The distinction is useful: the goal may be legitimate and the means may not be, and the means are what the therapy addresses.

### 2.6 The three areas of the script matrix

The script matrix is a three-by-three grid in which the rows are the levels of the script system and the columns are the three areas of the person's life: what the person thinks, what the person does, and what the person feels. The matrix provides a systematic way of mapping a case: what the person believes about themselves and others, what they do about it, and what they feel about it.

## 3. Script, and Its Developmental Form

### 3.1 The origins of script

Script is formed in the first years of life, in the period when the child is dependent, and it is formed out of two things: the child's own early adaptations to the environment, and the parental injunctions and permissions, which are themselves usually responses to the child's behaviour rather than independent statements. A child who is met with a consistent "you may" develops a script of permission; a child who is met with a consistent "you must not" develops a script of prohibition. Most children develop a mix, and the "mixed script" is the most common and the most difficult to work with.

### 3.2 The script and the parent's script

The developmental account of script that has emerged from the integrative school is that **the child's script is an adaptation to the parent's script**, and that the child's difficulties are frequently the result of the parent's unresolved issues rather than the child's. A parent whose script includes "I must be needed" will raise a child who is needed, and the child's symptom will be the solution to the parent's script. This account is extremely consequential for practice with young people, and it is the aspect of transactional analysis most consistent with the systemic and relational traditions.

### 3.3 The script payoff and the script belief

The modern formulation of the script has three elements, and they should be distinguished carefully. The **script belief** is the unconscious conviction about the world and the self, formed in early childhood and not available to conscious examination. The **script payoff** is the benefit, however small or however costly, that the script is designed to produce. The **script system** is the network of beliefs and behaviours that sustains it. The therapist's work is to locate the payoff — because the payoff is what the script is for — and to make it available to the young person in a form they can consider.

## 4. Redecision and the Redecision Process

### 4.1 The redecision

The redecision is the therapeutic intervention at the heart of the redecisional school, and it is the theory's most distinctive contribution. A redecision is a change in the decision, belief, or behaviour by which the person learned to survive, made now, by an adult, in the full knowledge of what the old decision purchased and what it costs.

Berne's original formulation asked the client to construct and to state a new decision in a specified area of their life, and the redecisional school has developed the protocol in detail. The protocol has six steps.

**Step 1: Establish the script belief.** The client is helped to identify the old decision, in the form "I decided that..." or "I concluded that..." — for example, "I decided that I was not the kind of person who gets what they want."

**Step 2: Trace the script belief to its origins.** The origins are located, characteristically in early childhood, and the point is to establish that the decision was a rational response to a situation in which the young person had very little choice. This step is critical: the redecision is not a criticism of the old decision, which was an intelligent adaptation.

**Step 3: Identify the payoff.** What did the old decision buy? A redecision that is not connected to its payoff is unlikely to hold, and the identification of the payoff is often the most revealing part of the work. "I decided not to try because when I tried, I was humiliated in front of everyone, and I decided the humiliation was worse than the not-trying."

**Step 4: Identify the script belief about the self.** The decision is traced to a belief about who the person is, and this is the belief that the redecision must address. "If I don't try, then I am safe — and I am the one who never puts themselves in a position to be rejected."

**Step 5: Re-decide.** The client is invited to make a new decision, and the classical school's formulation is that the redecision is *mindful* — that is, it is made now, in the present, by an adult who has options the child did not have.

**Step 6: Test the redecision in action.** The new decision is tested in behaviour, and the testing is the point at which the old script is reasserted and has to be reworked.

### 4.2 The value of the redecision

The redecision is a clear, teachable, and time-limited intervention with a specific structure, and it maps reasonably onto the behavioural activation and cognitive restructuring work in other traditions. Its distinctive contribution is the emphasis on the *payoff*: the therapist's question is not "is this belief true?" but "what does this belief buy you, and what does it cost?" This is an unusually well-developed therapeutic stance, and it works very well with young people, for whom the question "what does this get you?" is often considerably more useful than the question "do you believe this?"

### 4.3 The cautions

The redecision protocol requires care in three respects. The step in which the origins of the decision are traced can become a re-traumatisation if the practitioner is not competent with the material, and the tracing should be done at a pace the young person can tolerate. The formulation of a new decision can become coercive if the practitioner has a particular decision in mind, and the classical school's own literature on this is extensive and worth reading. And a redecision made in the consulting room may be made to please the practitioner, and the testing step is where the practitioner finds out.

## 5. Transactional Analysis with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The developmental fit

Transactional analysis offers three genuine developmental contributions.

**It provides a language for the young person's experience that is not pathologising.** The concepts of the Adapted Child and the script allow a young person's difficult behaviour to be described as an adaptation with a history rather than as a character defect, and a young person who has been called defiant for six years will recognise the description of an Adapted Child differently.

**The script account locates the difficulty in the early relationship.** The developmental literature is unambiguous that early relationships are formative, and the script account provides a way of working with a young person that is developmentally congruent and that is not dependent on the young person's verbal capacity.

**The account of the Parent and the child's internalised Parent is directly relevant to the adolescent's developmental task.** The adolescent's difficulty in becoming a separate individual is a difficulty in the negotiation of the internalised Parent, and the theory's account of the injunction and the permission is a way of working with that.

### 5.2 The specific danger

The specific danger of transactional analysis with young people is that the framework reproduces the adult's view of the young person.

The technical vocabulary of the theory — the Child ego state, the Adapted Child, the Natural Child — maps onto the ordinary adult discourse about young people, and the popular version of the theory reinforces the mapping: children are Childish, they need to be moved into the Adult, and the Adult is the person who knows. A practitioner who adopts the framework without the critical awareness will find that it validates the very attitude to the young person that the young person came to therapy to escape. The chapter's central clinical warning is this, and it should be stated in the seminar:

**In work with a young person, the Practitioner is not the Adult.** The Practitioner is one more person in a field, with their own Parent and Child states, with their own script, and with their own history. The young person's "Child" state is not the problem; it is the most alive part of them. The work is not to move the young person into the Adult but to find the Young Person's own decision, which is not the practitioner's decision and cannot be.

This is a real and specific issue, and the integrative school's development of the theory is largely an effort to address it. Erskine's work on the transactional analysis of the relationship and on the therapist's own redecision, and the integrative school's emphasis on the mutual influence of the practitioner and the young person in the here and now, are the direct responses to it.

### 5.3 The developmental modifications

**With young children**, the work is conducted through play and through the identification of the injunction, and the young child's "script" is usually observable in the repetitive play themes.

**With adolescents**, the redecision protocol is usable and is often the most direct of the interventions in this book for a young person who is stuck. The developmental modification is that the redecision must be *the young person's* and not the practitioner's, and the practitioner must be explicit about this.

**With young people in crisis**, the redecision is contraindicated in the acute phase, and the developmental and ethical judgment is that a young person in crisis has no available bandwidth for a redecision, and that the redecision can be experienced in crisis as a demand to perform a recovery that is not real.

## 6. Evidence and Research Support

**The scientific status.** Transactional analysis has had a contested standing in the scientific community since the 1980s, and the criticism has two distinct components that should be distinguished.

The first is the claim that the concepts of Parent, Adult, and Child do not correspond to anything that can be objectively measured, and that the theory is therefore not scientific. The counter-argument is that the concept of an ego state is a phenomenological description of a state of mind, and that the theory's hypotheses are about the *behavioural* consequences of the state, which is observable, and that the theory's research tradition, particularly in the integrative school, has generated testable propositions about the relationship between script characteristics and outcomes.

The second is a specific and well-documented controversy concerning the validation of the Transactional Analysis Personality Scale and the Tulley standards for the recognition of a theory of psychotherapy, and the award of the Transactional Analysis Research and Training Center's recognition to the theory in the 1990s. The dispute concerns the adequacy of the psychometric properties of the instruments and the adequacy of the process of review, and it has been conducted largely through the journals of the professional association. A student should know that the dispute exists, should read the primary sources rather than a summary of the dispute, and should not treat the controversy as evidence about the clinical usefulness of the method.

**The outcome evidence.** The outcome literature is small and mixed. A number of controlled studies, mostly in the 1980s and 1990s, have reported favourable outcomes for transactional analysis, particularly in relation to the specific outcome measures that the theory generates — script-related constructs, decisional change, and the redecision — and a smaller number have reported outcomes comparable to other bona fide therapies. A systematic review of the evidence for transactional analysis rated the evidence as limited. The integrative school's work, which is the most methodologically careful, has produced a more substantial body of process and outcome research than the classical school's, and the student who is evaluating the theory should begin there.

**The "proof" of the redecision.** The theory's own claim is that the redecision is the mechanism of change, and there is a body of research that reports associations between the completion of a redecision and favourable outcomes. The methodological difficulties in this literature are substantial: the measures are largely self-report, the redecision is assessed in ways that overlap heavily with the outcome measures, and the samples are small. The honest statement is that the redecision is a coherent intervention with a plausible mechanism, some supporting evidence, and a much weaker evidentiary base than its popularity would suggest.

**The relational findings.** As in every other humanistic theory, the support for the theory's relational propositions comes from the general alliance literature rather than from the theory's own studies.
