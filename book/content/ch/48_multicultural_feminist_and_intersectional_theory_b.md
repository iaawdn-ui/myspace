
## 7. Broaching and Cultural Formulation

Broaching is the counsellor's intentional invitation to discuss culture, identity, power, and difference. Silence does not create neutrality; it often tells the young person that these topics are unsafe or irrelevant.

A developmentally appropriate broach might be: “People's experiences at school can be affected by race, language, disability, gender, family income, or being new to a place. I don't want to assume which, if any, matter for you. Are any of those part of what you're dealing with?”

Cultural formulation explores:

- the young person's and family's explanation of the problem;
- salient identities and communities;
- migration, language, and intergenerational differences;
- discrimination and institutional experiences;
- family roles, authority, privacy, and decision-making;
- strengths, cultural resources, and trusted helpers;
- differences between client and counsellor;
- barriers to services and previous experiences of help.

The DSM-5 Cultural Formulation Interview provides useful prompts, though it should be adapted for adolescents and not treated as an interrogation.

## 8. Assessment, Diagnosis, and Testing

Cultural bias can enter through language, norms, item content, examiner behaviour, and interpretation. A quiet adolescent may be respectful, frightened, dissociating, neurodivergent, unfamiliar with the language of assessment, or uninterested. A parent who challenges a professional may be advocating within a system that has previously ignored them.

Before diagnosing, ask whether behaviour is developmentally expected, culturally congruent, situationally adaptive, or produced by unmet access needs. Use multiple informants and settings, validated translations, interpreters, and dynamic assessment where appropriate. Document uncertainty.

School data require disaggregation and scepticism. If one group receives more exclusions, restraint, lower grades for similar work, or fewer referrals to support, individual counselling cannot be the sole response. The institution becomes part of the formulation.

## 9. Case Illustration: “Defiant” in Two Languages

**Presentation.** Samira, fourteen, recently arrived in a new country, is referred for refusing to speak in class and arguing with her mother at home. School staff suggest selective mutism and oppositional behaviour.

**Cultural formulation.** Samira speaks three languages but is still acquiring academic English. In class she fears pronunciation errors after students imitated her accent. At home she interprets for her mother at appointments, giving her adult responsibility but little authority. Her mother, once a pharmacist, works nights cleaning and feels grief and status loss. Arguments occur when Samira refuses further interpreting for complex medical letters.

**Intersectional reading.** Gender, migration, language, class change, age, and parentification interact. Samira is simultaneously privileged by adults as competent and burdened by that competence. Her silence protects dignity at school; her anger at home protests an unsustainable role.

**Intervention.** The school provides language support, private ways to answer, anti-bullying action, and a bilingual staff contact. The service uses professional interpreters and translated letters so Samira is not the family's administrative bridge. Counselling validates grief and status changes, helps mother and daughter negotiate interpreting boundaries, and builds Samira's confidence through small speaking successes. The counsellor does not diagnose defiance from behaviour that had communicative and structural logic.

## 10. Feminist and Anti-Oppressive Practice in Sessions

Practical commitments include:

- explain counsellor power, records, confidentiality, and choices in accessible language;
- invite correction and treat disagreement as information;
- avoid mother blaming and include fathers, kin, and chosen supports where safe;
- notice whose time, body, privacy, and labour are expected to adjust;
- make goals with the young person, not merely for institutional convenience;
- use interpreters and accessible formats;
- challenge discriminatory policies through consultation and governance;
- evaluate outcomes by client-defined wellbeing and participation, not compliance alone.

Self-reflection must lead somewhere. A counsellor who recognises bias but continues using the same referral thresholds, language, and punitive partnerships has insight without action.

## 11. Evidence and Limitations

Evidence supports culturally adapted interventions for some populations and outcomes, though adaptation is heterogeneous. Cultural humility is associated with stronger alliance and client perceptions of counsellor competence. Feminist and empowerment-oriented approaches have supportive but variable evidence depending on population and outcome.

No competency framework can guarantee responsive practice. Training can become performative if institutions do not change staffing, language access, data monitoring, discipline patterns, and decision-making power. Intersectionality can also be diluted into a slogan. Its test is whether formulation and action change.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Cultural humility** — Lifelong self-critique, openness, and accountability in cross-cultural relationships.
- **Broaching** — Intentionally inviting discussion of culture, identity, difference, and power.
- **Intersectionality** — Analysis of interacting social identities and systems of power.
- **Minority stress** — Stress arising from stigma, discrimination, concealment, and expectation of rejection.
- **Structural formulation** — Case conceptualisation that includes material and institutional conditions.
- **Parentification** — A child assuming adult practical or emotional responsibilities.
- **Anti-oppressive practice** — Counselling that identifies and acts against power relations producing harm.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Multicultural, feminist, and intersectional theories change the counsellor's questions. Distress is still personal, but never only personal. Identity, language, power, discrimination, family roles, and institutional design shape both suffering and recovery. Ethical practice therefore combines humility in the room with action beyond it.
