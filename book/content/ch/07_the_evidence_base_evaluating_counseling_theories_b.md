## 10. Reading a Meta-Analysis: A Worked Procedure

The procedure below is used in the seminar. It takes about forty minutes with a single review and produces a written appraisal of about 800 words, which is the format the annotated bibliography assignment requires.

### Step 1: Read the abstract, the conclusions, and the tables (10 minutes)

Read the abstract and the conclusions first, and then the characteristics table and the risk-of-bias figures. If the paper's headline claim does not survive the reading of the tables, that is the finding.

### Step 2: Find the question (3 minutes)

Write the PICO question in your own words: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. If the paper does not state a question that can be rendered in this form, note that the review is a narrative review and that the pooled estimate is decorative.

### Step 3: Trace the flow of studies (5 minutes)

How many records were identified, how many screened, how many included, and how many contributed data to each analysis? A large discrepancy between the number of studies included and the number contributing to the pooled estimate usually indicates that the pooled estimate rests on a small subset, and you should find out which.

### Step 4: Assess the risk of bias (8 minutes)

For each of the two or three included studies you examine closely, note the randomisation procedure, whether the allocation was concealed, whether assessors were blind, the attrition rate and its direction, and whether the protocol was delivered with fidelity. Then ask the question that matters: are these flaws likely to inflate the effect? High attrition with a differential rate between conditions inflates. Unblinded outcome assessment inflates. Poor fidelity dilutes, and dilution is a different problem with a different implication.

### Step 5: Examine the heterogeneity (5 minutes)

Find the I² statistic and the forest plot. If I² is high, identify the moderators that the authors propose and check whether they are plausible. A very high I² with no exploration of the sources is a serious defect, because it means the pooled number has no interpretation.

### Step 6: Assess the effect and its clinical importance (5 minutes)

Report the pooled effect with its confidence interval, in the units the authors used. Then translate: what does this mean for a young person with the severity described in the sample? Compare it with the natural course of the problem and with the consequences of no treatment. An effect that is small relative to no treatment may still be the best available, and that judgement requires the comparison the authors will often have made.

### Step 7: Consider the publication bias diagnostics (4 minutes)

Look at the funnel plot and at what the authors say about it. Note whether the small-study literature in this field is dominated by positive results — it is, in most areas, and the authors of a good review will say so.

### Step 8: Apply the ecological validity test (5 minutes)

Write down the answer to the question: *would this evidence apply to the young person in front of me?* List the similarities and the differences. This is the step that determines whether the review will change your practice, and it is the step that the profession's own literature most often omits.

### Step 9: Write the appraisal (5 minutes)

A useful structure is four short paragraphs: the question and the search; the quality of the included studies; the size and meaning of the effect; and the applicability to your practice, including a specific recommendation.

## 11. Case Illustration: A Treatment That Is Not Working, and How to Know

**The situation.** A student counsellor in a university placement is working with Jordan, a sixteen-year-old referred for "low mood and school refusal." Jordan's sessions are going well in the sense that Jordan attends, talks, and has a good relationship with the student counsellor. The student has been delivering a structured behavioural activation and problem-solving approach, adapted from a well-known manual, for six sessions. Jordan reports feeling "a bit better" on the weekly rating form, but has not returned to school and describes the sessions as "not really helping."

**The measurement.** The placement requires a Session Rating Scale and a standardised depression measure at intake, at session four, and at discharge. The intake score was in the moderate range; the session-four score is in the mild range; the young person's own ratings have moved from 2 to 5 on a ten-point scale.

**The supervision conversation.** The supervisor asks three questions.

*What did you expect, and by when?* The student answers: "I thought the mood would lift and he'd start going back in week two or three." The supervisor asks what the manual predicts, and the student finds the relevant section: the manual specifies that a substantial increase in activity is expected by session four and that the majority of participants show a reduction in depressive symptoms by session six, with some delay in the return of school attendance.

*What is the functional relationship between the school refusal and the mood?* The student has not examined it. The supervisor asks whether the school refusal began before or after the low mood, and whether there is anything about the school, the timetable, a particular class, a particular teacher, or the journey that precedes the avoidance. The student reviews the notes and finds that the refusal began after a fall in mathematics and after a comment by a teacher that the student had described as "humiliating" — a fact recorded in the intake notes in the first session and not returned to.

*What do you know about Jordan's life outside the sessions?* The student answers that Jordan lives with his mother, who works shifts, and that there is a younger sister. The supervisor asks about the father's involvement, and the student discovers that this was not asked. The notes record only that the "parent" attended the first session.

**What the supervision produces.** Three conclusions.

First, on the evidence, the treatment is not yet disconfirmed. The self-report ratings have improved and the standardised measure has moved. The manual's own expected trajectory has not been exceeded, and the manual specifies a longer period before the school attendance change.

Second, the formulation is inadequate regardless of the manual. The manual was designed for a population whose depression was maintained by inactivity and whose functional deterioration followed it. Jordan's school refusal preceded the low mood and appears to be maintained by a specific avoided situation, and a behavioural activation approach that does not address the avoidance will produce exactly the pattern observed: improved mood, no change in school attendance. A manual applied to a case whose maintaining process is different from the manual's is a different intervention, whatever the fidelity.

Third, there is a missing piece of the assessment. The student's intake was incomplete on the family and developmental history, and the omission has had a specific consequence: the case has been formulated as a case of a withdrawn adolescent with depression rather than as a case of a boy whose school refusal is maintained by a feared situation, in a family with an unexamined structure, and possibly in the context of a separation.

**The revised plan.** The student counsellor and the supervisor agree on three things: a specific functional analysis of the school refusal, using the framework in Chapter 24; an examination of the mathematics situation, in which the teacher is involved with consent, and which is the first step of an exposure hierarchy; and a full family and developmental history, obtained in the next session, with attention to the father's absence or presence and its effect on the family structure. The behavioural activation continues, because it is helping with the mood and there is no reason to stop a helpful intervention; but the student now understands that it is treating a secondary problem and that the primary problem is untouched.

**The ethical dimension.** The supervisor raises a question the student had not considered. If the treatment had been allowed to run to its manualised conclusion and then discharged with the note "improved mood, low mood resolved, attendance unchanged," the outcome would have been recorded as a successful episode and Jordan would have left the service with the same problem with which he arrived. This is the most common form of harm in a service that measures only what it is treating: the young person's actual presentation is not the measure, and the measure becomes the goal. The requirement to measure the target of the intervention, and to review against it, is an ethical requirement and not merely a methodological nicety.

## 12. Evidence and Research Support

**Well supported.** That specific, competently delivered treatments produce superior outcomes to no treatment for many conditions. That some interventions have larger effects than others for particular conditions, with the clearest examples in anxiety, in conduct problems, and in eating disorders. That practitioner competence and the quality of the alliance account for a substantial proportion of outcome variance in routine services. That structured feedback improves outcomes. That the majority of the outcome variance in routine services is attributable to the provider rather than to the model.

**Supported with qualifications.** That the outcome of treatment is predictable from diagnosis; the prognostic power of diagnosis is modest, and the prognostic power of comorbidity, family functioning, and the severity of impairment is higher. That structured manualised treatment is superior to usual care; the superiority is small in most routine-service comparisons and depends on the fidelity of the delivery and on the quality of the comparator. That evidence-based practices are superior to usual care; the effect is real but modest, and the greatest gains come from the implementation supports rather than from the training alone.

**Weakly supported or contested.** That a specific theory is superior to another in general. That specific effects are negligible; the component literature does not support this. That the "specific effect" and "common effect" can be cleanly separated; the current methodological position is that they cannot be estimated independently in the same way, and that the alliance is a mediator rather than a competitor.

**Methodologically weak or absent.** Evidence for the treatment of specific populations of young people — including those with neurodevelopmental conditions, those who are out of care, those who are justice-involved, those who are experiencing poverty, and those who are young people of colour — with the partial and encouraging exception of the mindfulness, ACT, DBT-A, and multisystemic literatures. Evidence on the differential effectiveness of approaches for young people who differ by culture, by gender, or by neurotype. Evidence on the long-term outcomes of counseling. Evidence on the cost-effectiveness of counseling compared with other services, which is sparse and methodologically weak.

## 13. Strengths and Limitations of the Evidence Base

**Strengths.** The counseling research literature is, for a clinical field, large, methodologically sophisticated in places, and consistently transparent about its limitations. The therapy-outcome literature has produced genuinely important findings about alliance, about components, about the limits of the efficacy trials, and about the centrality of the practitioner. The practice-based research literature has done more than any other to keep the field honest about the conditions in which its work is actually done. And the field's measurement instruments, developed for routine clinical use, are among the field's most practically valuable contributions.

**Limitations.** The population problem is severe and systematic. The methodological quality of much of the literature is low by the standards of evidence-based medicine, and the field's own reviews consistently report high rates of uncontrolled designs and high attrition. Publication bias is substantial and is not adequately addressed by the standard diagnostics. The outcome measures are often poorly matched to what matters to young people. The treatment literature is overwhelmingly concerned with a small number of well-studied conditions, and the young people most likely to be referred to a school or community service are the ones least represented in it. And the field's public rhetoric about "evidence-based practice" is considerably more confident than its evidence warrants.

## 14. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**Culture and the evidence base.** The evidence base is dominated by studies conducted in the United States, Western Europe, and Australia, with samples that are disproportionately white, disproportionately affluent, and disproportionately drawn from the intact nuclear family. The construct validity of the standard measures in a population with different assumptions about selfhood, family obligation, emotional expression, or the appropriateness of disclosing distress to an adult is a genuine methodological question, and the field's treatment of it is largely by assumption rather than by evidence. Chapter 8 develops this argument and Chapter 48 develops its ethical implications.

**The ethical requirement of measurement.** The codes of the profession require that practitioners work with "the most current and relevant evidence" and that they evaluate the effectiveness of their work. These two requirements together make outcome measurement an ethical obligation rather than an administrative one, and a practitioner who declines to measure on the grounds that measurement is bureaucratic is declining a professional requirement.

But the requirement carries a condition: the measurement must include the young person's own appraisal, and the measurement must be used in the service of the young person's work rather than in the service of the institution's audit. A young person whose only measured outcomes are produced by a clinician-rated scale, and whose own account of change is never collected, is being treated in a system that has decided not to listen to them.

**The ethical requirement of honesty about uncertainty.** The codes require that practitioners communicate with clients in a manner that is accurate and that does not misrepresent the effectiveness of the services provided. In a field where practitioners are under pressure to present confident rationales for their interventions, this requirement is more demanding than it appears. A practitioner who tells a fifteen-year-old "this approach has a very good track record with young people who are avoiding school" when the relevant evidence base is thin, indirect, and largely about a different population has misrepresented the effectiveness of the service.

**Equity as an evidentiary requirement.** A service that delivers the best-supported treatment to the young people who can access it most easily has not delivered evidence-based practice; it has delivered evidence-based practice to a selected population. Chapter 50 treats the distributive question, and Chapter 48 treats the power differential that determines it.

## 15. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Evidence-based practice** — The use, in clinical decisions, of the best available research evidence together with clinical expertise and the client's circumstances and preferences.
- **Empirically supported treatment** — A treatment that has met specified criteria for research adequacy, including replication in independent studies with comparison groups, defined populations, and manualised delivery; a claim about the state of the research rather than about superiority.
- **Efficacy** — The capacity of an intervention to produce better outcomes than a comparison condition under ideal, research conditions.
- **Effectiveness** — The capacity of an intervention to produce better outcomes than it would otherwise have in the routine conditions of ordinary services.
- **Implementation** — The process by which an intervention is adopted, delivered with fidelity, and sustained in a real service; the stage at which most of the loss of benefit occurs.
- **Efficacy–effectiveness gap** — The difference in average outcome between trials and routine services, attributable to case mix, practitioner competence, time pressure, comorbidity, engagement, and fidelity.
- **Pragmatic trial** — A randomised study conducted in routine care with the resources and constraints of routine care, which estimates effectiveness rather than efficacy.
- **Practice-based evidence** — Evidence generated by routine outcome monitoring in real services and analysed with quasi-experimental methods.
- **Learning health system** — A service in which routine data collection and comparison are used as the primary mechanism of quality improvement.
- **Randomised controlled trial** — A design in which allocation to conditions is randomised and outcomes are compared; the strongest available design for causal inference.
- **Control condition** — The comparison condition in a trial; its composition determines what the trial can estimate.
- **Credible control** — A control condition designed to reproduce the common elements of treatment while omitting the specific technique, so that the specific technique's contribution can be estimated.
- **Wait-list control** — A control condition providing no treatment elements; an inadequate basis for attributing outcome to a specific technique.
- **Attrition bias** — Systematic error arising when participants who leave a study differ from those who remain, typically in ways correlated with outcome.
- **Selective reporting** — The publication of only those outcomes that reached significance, inflating apparent effects.
- **Publication bias** — The systematic under-publication of null or negative findings, producing inflated pooled estimates.
- **Funnel plot** — A diagnostic plot of effect size against sample size, used to detect publication bias; unreliable with few studies.
- **Heterogeneity** — The degree to which the results of studies within a meta-analysis differ beyond what chance would explain; measured by I².
- **Ecological validity** — The extent to which the conditions of a study resemble the conditions of the practice in which the findings would be applied.
- **Outcome measure** — The instrument used to assess change; the choice determines what is detected.
- **Session Rating Scale** — A four-item measure of session quality used both as an outcome measure and as routine feedback.
- **Natural course** — The trajectory a problem would have followed without treatment; the benchmark against which the clinical importance of an effect should be judged.
:::

## 16. Chapter Summary

"Evidence-based practice" in counseling means combining the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the client's circumstances, and the field's tendency to use the term to mean only "use a treatment that has been tested" is a source of confusion. The empirically supported treatment movement contributed a valuable methodological standard and a misleading label, and the implementation movement corrected the second problem by distinguishing the state of the research from the state of the practice.

The hierarchy of evidence organises the literature by internal validity, and the counseling literature occupies an unusual position within it, with its strengths in single-case designs, alliance research, and practice-based research, and its weaknesses in the population, the control condition, and the outcome measure.

The efficacy–effectiveness–implementation gap is the field's most important methodological insight. Trials show that treatments can work; routine-service research shows that they work less well, with large provider variation, under ordinary conditions. Practice-based evidence is the compass that tracks this.

For a profession that intends to work with children and adolescents, the population problem is the most serious: the adolescent evidence base is narrower than the adult one, the component literature is almost entirely adult, drop-out is high and non-random, and the field's habit of transferring adult findings without comment is an unacknowledged extrapolation.

A forty-minute appraisal framework, a worked case in which a helpful treatment is shown to be treating the wrong target, and the ethical requirement to measure the young person's own appraisal and to state uncertainty honestly together constitute the practical content of this chapter.

## 18. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 7.1 — The Forty-Minute Appraisal (3 hours, individual, submitted)

Obtain a recent systematic review of a psychological or behavioural intervention for adolescents, using the library databases, and produce the 800-word appraisal described in Section 10. Submit it with the review's reference. The seminar will compare appraisals of the same review, and the differences in judgement between students are the most instructive part of the exercise.

### Activity 7.2 — The Evidence Map for a Presenting Problem (2 hours, groups)

Each group takes one common adolescent presenting problem — school refusal, panic, self-harm, family conflict, social withdrawal, substance use — and maps the evidence: what is well supported, what is supported with qualifications, what is contested, what is absent. Use the PICO format to structure the search, and report on the quality of what you find. The groups present in ten minutes, and the map becomes the group's reference for the formulation assignment later in the course.

### Activity 7.3 — The Unmeasurable Session (60 minutes, pairs, reflective)

Practise a session in which the young person reports a small improvement that the practitioner does not believe, and the practitioner must explore the discrepancy without dismissing the young person's account and without inflating it. The pair then reflects on what it feels like to hold both, and on the pressure to produce a satisfying session. The exercise is aimed at the difference between measurement and its corruption, which is the most common way that a service's data become useless.
:::

## 19. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the frameworks.** Read the evidence-based practice framework literature, and read the consensus statement on the evidence base for psychotherapy. Note the difference in tone between the two; the consensus statement is written by people who have to be accurate about a field they work in, and its careful hedging is a model for how to write about one's own evidence.

**For the pragmatic and practice-based literature.** Read the large pragmatic trials and the practice-based research reports. These are the studies that changed what the field believes, and they are also the studies with the most interesting internal disagreements.

**For the adolescent specifics.** Read the child and adolescent psychotherapy handbooks, which summarise the state of the sub-field, and then check the summaries against the primary studies. The summaries are considerably more confident than the studies support.

**For the critical stance.** Read the methodological critiques of the psychotherapy research literature — the papers on publication bias, on the exclusion of dropouts, on the equivalence-trial design, and on the overstatement of the evidence base in public policy. A student who has read only the favourable literature will over-trust it.
