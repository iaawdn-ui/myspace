# Culturally Responsive Theory and the Cultural Lens

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the three generations of multicultural counseling theory and the assumption that separates them.
- Distinguish the concepts of culture, ethnicity, race, social identity, and acculturation, and use them accurately.
- Summarise the empirical findings on the relationship between cultural factors and mental health outcomes, and identify where the evidence is strong and where it is thin.
- Apply the cultural lens to a case, moving between the individual, interactional, family, community, and societal levels of analysis.
- Describe the structure and use of the cultural formulation interview, and explain its ethical function.
- Evaluate the evidence base for the three models of cultural identity development.
- Analyse how cultural factors modify the operation of a specific counseling theory, and identify which theories have been most and least developed for cultural responsiveness.
- Apply the principles of cultural humility to your own practice, including the assessment of your own assumptions.

## Chapter Overview

Almost everything in this book assumes a particular kind of young person. The assumption is rarely made explicit, and it is rarely examined: the young person is an individual; the family is a context rather than a client; the significant adults in the young person's life are the parents; the distress is located inside the person; the resources for change are the person's own; the culture is a variable that may modify the presentation of the problem but not the existence of it; and the professional's own values, categories, and authority are the appropriate frame for understanding all of it.

This chapter takes that assumption apart. It describes the development of multicultural counseling theory through three generations, sets out the vocabulary with which the field discusses culture, and reviews the evidence on culture and mental health, which is more substantial than is often acknowledged and less supportive of simple conclusions than is frequently claimed. It then develops the cultural lens as a practical instrument: a way of analysing a case at five levels, and of asking the question that a culturally responsive formulation must answer — *what is happening in this young person's life, and what would the difficulty look like if it were not a problem?*

The chapter also addresses the practitioner. Cultural responsiveness is not a set of techniques for working with young people from minority backgrounds; it is a self-examination of the framework in which the practitioner has been trained, and a set of disciplines for examining the categories, assumptions, and authority that the framework carries. A practitioner who can work skilfully with a young person from a different background and who has never examined the assumptions of their own training has learned a technique, not a discipline.

## 1. Three Generations of Multicultural Counseling Theory

### 1.1 The first generation: cultural sensitivity and cultural competence

The first generation, dominant from the 1970s through the 1980s, was built on two propositions. The first was that the mainstream theories and the mainstream practitioner were **anthropocentric** — that they took the norms, values, and worldviews of the dominant culture as universal, and treated deviation from them as pathology. The second was that the remedy was **cultural competence**: the practitioner should acquire knowledge of the beliefs, values, customs, and communication styles of the cultural groups they were likely to work with, and modify their practice accordingly.

The first generation made an important contribution. It named the anthropocentrism, and it was largely responsible for the curricular reforms that made culture a required element of counselor training. Its assumptions, however, are now generally regarded as inadequate in two respects.

The first is that **culture is treated as a static attribute of a group**. The "culture" of a manual or a workshop is presented as a set of facts about a population, homogeneous within itself and fixed over time. The research on cultural identity development, on biculturalism, on acculturation stress, and on intra-group and inter-group variation demonstrates that this is false, and the practitioner who has learned a cultural profile will encounter young people who do not fit it.

The second is that **the practitioner is positioned as the expert and the cultural group as the object of study**. A model in which the practitioner learns about a group in order to work more effectively with it reproduces the asymmetry of the clinical encounter, and it does so at the level of knowledge. It is not enough for the practitioner to know a great deal about a young person's background; the question is what the practitioner does with that knowledge, and a practitioner who has been given a great deal of information is at risk of using it to explain the young person rather than to understand them.

### 1.2 The second generation: cultural awareness and cultural humility

The second generation, emerging from the late 1980s, shifted the emphasis from the culture of the client to the culture of the practitioner. Its central concepts are **cultural awareness** — the recognition that the practitioner has been socialised into a particular cultural framework and carries assumptions that are not visible from within it — and **cultural humility**, which adds the requirement that the practitioner remain continuously open to the possibility that their framework is partial, and that the people they work with hold knowledge they have not been taught.

Cultural humility differs from cultural competence in an important respect: it is not a state to be achieved and then certified. It is a lifelong practice, and the second generation's own literature has become markedly more willing to say that no practitioner ever arrives at a state of cultural competence, and that the goal is rather a stance of informed humility in which the practitioner is always prepared to be corrected by the people they work with.

### 1.3 The third generation: cultural responsiveness and the social justice commitment

The third generation, which has become dominant in the twenty-first century, reframes the problem in structural terms. Its two central commitments are:

**Cultural responsiveness** — the proposition that no intervention is culturally neutral, that every theory, every technique, and every assessment instrument carries assumptions about the person and about what constitutes a problem, and that the practitioner must identify those assumptions in the specific case and determine whether they hold for this young person.

**Social justice and critical consciousness** — the proposition that individual distress is frequently produced by systemic conditions such as racism, poverty, colonisation, gender inequality, ableism, and the criminalisation of young people, and that a practice that locates all difficulty in the individual and all resources in the individual is a practice that will fail with the young people for whom the systemic conditions are the main cause.

The third generation also brings a distinctive ethical and epistemological stance, best described as **critical**: the practitioner asks not only whether the intervention is helping this young person but also whether the framework of the intervention reproduces the conditions that produce the young person's distress, and whether the young person's own account of their situation is itself constrained by a discourse that positions them as the problem.

Chapter 48 develops the third-generation position in full, and Chapter 49 applies it to a specific population. This chapter establishes the vocabulary and the evidence on which the later chapters depend.

## 2. The Vocabulary of Culture

The field's vocabulary has grown unwieldy, and much of the confusion in the professional literature comes from the imprecision of its terms. The following distinctions are the ones a student must be able to make.

**Culture** refers to the shared and transmitted patterns of meaning, values, norms, and practices that distinguish a group. Two cautions are necessary. The first is that culture is not a static possession of a person; people participate in multiple cultures — familial, regional, religious or secular, national, ethnic, racial, class, professional, gender, generational — and the configurations change over a life. The second is that the boundaries of a "culture" are themselves a matter of politics, and the classification of people into cultural groups has historically been a tool of administration and exclusion.

**Ethnicity** refers to shared heritage — a shared history, language, or ancestral origin — and is generally a matter of self-identification and of social ascription. The relevant clinical point is that ethnic identity may be central to a young person's identity, that it may be contested within the family, and that the level of identification is a developmental and contextual variable rather than a fixed attribute.

**Race** is a social construction with real material consequences. Treating race as a biological fact is a scientific error; treating it as socially meaningless is a political error that obscures the mechanisms by which race operates in health, in the justice system, in schooling, and in everyday interaction. The clinical consequence is that race operates as a predictor of the young person's exposure to stress, of the quality of the responses they receive from institutions, and of the probability that their distress will be misread. Chapter 48 develops this argument in detail.

**Social identity** refers to the intersection of multiple categories — race, gender, class, disability, sexual orientation, religion, and others — in the constitution of a person's position in the world. The concept of intersectionality, developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw in the context of the law and later applied to psychology, holds that the experiences produced by these categories cannot be understood by examining each separately, and that the structures of power that operate through them intersect in ways that produce distinct experiences that are often invisible to the dominant account.

**Acculturation** refers to the process of change that follows contact between cultures, involving shifts in behaviour, identity, and social organisation. The widely used model of Berry describes four strategies — integration, separation, marginalisation, and assimilation — and the literature suggests that the integration strategy, in which the young person maintains both the heritage and the receiving culture, is associated with the best adjustment outcomes, while marginalisation is associated with the worst. The clinical relevance is that a young person of immigrant parents in a third generation may be moreaccurately described as having a bicultural identity than as being acculturating, and the therapeutic implication — that a bicultural young person may need help with the *negotiation* of two value systems rather than with the resolution of a conflict between them — is often missed.

**Microaggression, subtle discrimination, and cultural taxation** are the contemporary concepts that describe the cumulative effect of low-level discriminatory incidents on a member of a marginalised group, and the additional burden borne by members of such groups who are also responsible for representing their group to the dominant culture. These concepts matter clinically because the cumulative effect of repeated microaggressions on a young person's mental health is substantial and is frequently misattributed to individual psychopathology.

## 3. Culture and Mental Health: What the Evidence Shows

The evidence on culture and adolescent mental health is more substantial than the profession's ambivalence about it usually suggests, and it points in a consistent direction.

### 3.1 The relationship between racial and ethnic discrimination and mental health

The research on discrimination and mental health among adolescents is now extensive and consistent. Minority stress — the theory that the experience of chronic discrimination and of the threat of discrimination constitutes a chronic stressor with predictable mental health consequences — is supported by a substantial literature in adolescents, in adults, and across racial and ethnic groups, and the effects are detectable in both symptoms and in the objective markers of physiological stress. The relationship is dose-dependent, is stronger for discrimination that is chronic rather than episodic, and is stronger for discrimination that occurs in the domains in which the young person is most exposed to institutions — school, policing, healthcare.

Two findings are especially relevant to the practitioner. The first is that **the effect of discrimination on mental health is mediated substantially by the young person's appraisal of the discrimination as attributable to themselves**, and this is a point of direct theoretical interest: the mechanism is cognitive appraisal, which means the cognitive therapies may have something to offer here, provided they are applied to the appraisal of discrimination rather than to a self-blame that the discrimination has produced. The second is that **discrimination is associated with increased risk in the opposite direction to the one the field often assumes**: it is associated with higher rates of internalising symptoms, and the externalising and conduct problems that are more visible to the system are also elevated, which is a point that should temper any assumption that an adolescent's disruptive behaviour is not connected to their social position.

### 3.2 The protective role of ethnic and racial identity

The research on ethnic identity is consistent in a way that is important for practice: **a strong, positive, explored ethnic identity is associated with better mental health, better academic outcomes, and greater resilience**, and the effect is present across groups and across age. The protective effect is strongest where the young person's family and community reinforce the identity, where the young person has a sense of positive belonging, and where the wider society is not actively undermining it. Where the wider society is undermining it, the identity can be a source of strain as well as of strength, and the strain appears particularly at moments of racial incident or of public scrutiny.

The clinical implication is that the ethnic identity of a young person is not a marginal matter to be addressed at the end of an assessment but a central resource whose development may need active support, and that a practice that treats a young person's cultural identity as a source of risk to be managed has misunderstood the literature.

### 3.3 The role of the family and the community

The research on the protective role of the family, extended family, and community is strong across cultures, and it is the single most important finding for the practitioner working with an adolescent. The presence of at least one stable, reliable adult in the young person's life is one of the most consistent predictors of good outcomes across every problem domain and every population. For young people facing racism, poverty, displacement, or conflict, the presence of a connected and supportive adult is a stronger protective factor than almost anything else that a service can provide.

This finding has a direct theoretical implication for counseling, and it is the point on which the cultural critique of the individual framework is most concretely grounded: **the most effective intervention for many of the young people who present with the most severe difficulties is the repair of their social relationships**, not the alteration of their internal states. This is the point at which the cultural critique and the systemic tradition converge, and it is examined in Chapter 50.

### 3.4 Where the evidence is thin

The evidence is thin in several important places, and a student should know where. It is thin on the effectiveness of culturally adapted interventions as compared with cultural adaptations of the same intervention — the question of whether cultural adaptation improves outcomes is real but unresolved, and the field's claim that cultural adaptation is superior is asserted more often than demonstrated. It is thin on the evidence for particular cultural groups, because the research is organised around the categories of the dominant culture and the sample sizes for particular groups are frequently very small. It is thin on the relationship between cultural identity development and outcome in a way that is sufficiently controlled to guide practice. And it is thin on the long-term consequences of cultural identity, where the developmental and the political literatures have not been well integrated.

## 4. The Cultural Lens: Levels of Analysis

The cultural lens is a practical instrument for the formulation, and it works by requiring the practitioner to move explicitly between five levels of analysis. The formulation of a culturally responsive case should specify what is happening at each level and how the levels are connected.

![The cultural lens, showing the five levels of analysis in the cultural formulation of a case.](fig09_cultural_lens.png)

**The individual level.** What is the young person's internal state? What are their appraisals, their emotions, their self-evaluations, their physiological state? The individual level is where the clinical tradition locates the problem, and the cultural critique does not deny that it matters; it denies that it is sufficient.

**The interactional level.** What happens between people in the young person's immediate environment? How does the young person communicate, defer, resist, negotiate? What are the norms of emotional expression, of directness, of eye contact, of silence, of disagreement, and of physical contact in the young person's family and community? A young person who is described as "non-compliant" in a school may be performing a norm of respect for adult authority that is entirely appropriate in the family, and the school has read it as defiance.

**The family level.** What is the structure of the family, and what are the family's own cultural resources? Family structure varies enormously across cultures — the extended family, the multi-generational household, the separated and reconstituted family, the kin-centred network — and a formulation that assumes a nuclear family with two parents will misdescribe a great many cases. Chapter 33 addresses the specific issues of working with parents and adolescents across cultural contexts.

**The community level.** What institutions surround the young person — school, faith or secular community, sports club, extended kin network, neighbourhood — and what is the quality of their connection to them? What is the young person's reputation, and how does it operate within the community? In some contexts the community is the young person's primary source of identity and support; in others, it is the source of pressure and of the demand for conformity that the young person is trying to escape.

**The societal level.** What is the young person's position with respect to structural conditions — the labour market, the housing market, the immigration status of the family, the criminal justice system, the welfare system, the education system, the presence of racism? This is the level at which the third-generation critique locates the primary causes of many presentations, and it is the level that the individual clinical tradition most consistently omits.

### 4.1 The question that the lens generates

The cultural lens produces one question that no individual level analysis can produce:

> **If this young person's difficulty were entirely a product of their culture, their family, their community, and their position in society — with no individual pathology at all — what would we see?**

The purpose of the question is diagnostic. If the answer is "we would see exactly what we see," then the difficulty is not primarily a product of the social context, and the intervention should be directed primarily at the individual level. If the answer is "we would see something quite different," then the social context is a substantial part of the picture, and an intervention directed only at the young person's internal states will fail.

This is a hard question, and it is deliberately discomposing, because the answer is often that the young person's symptoms are entirely explicable as responses to conditions that no one in the room is going to change. The correct professional response in that case is not to abandon the young person and not to explain away the difficulty, but to work on the individual level as well as on the social level, and to be honest with the young person about what is and is not available.

## 5. The Cultural Formulation Interview

The cultural formulation interview is a structured set of questions, developed in the DSM tradition and now widely used in clinical and research settings, whose purpose is to elicit the narrative and the cultural meaning of the presenting problem from the perspective of the young person and, with permission, of the family. It is reproduced in full in Appendix A.

Its structure is worth understanding, because the structure embodies a set of commitments.

**The identity question** asks the young person to describe themselves, with the explicit framing that the question is interested in the identities that matter to them and in the ones that do not. The ordering matters: the question comes first, before the problem, so that the practitioner hears who the young person is before hearing what is wrong with them.

**The explanatory models question** asks what the young person and their family think is causing the problem, what they think has helped in the past, and what they expect from treatment. This question is the most clinically useful in the whole set, and it is the one most often omitted. The practitioner who does not know what the family believes is about the cause of the difficulty does not know whether their explanation is shared, and an explanation that is not shared is an intervention applied to a person who does not believe in it.

**The psychosocial stressors and supports question** covers the domains of the cultural formulation: the young person's key social relationships, the disruptions, the transitions, and the losses; the social aspects of the young person's functioning; the cultural elements of the young person's environment; and the differences between the young person and the significant others in their views of the problem.

**The cultural elements of the clinician–patient relationship** asks how the young person expects to be treated, whether there are differences in gender, age, or other factors between them and the practitioner that matter, whether there are aspects of the relationship that the practitioner should change, and whether there are any other aspects of the relationship that the young person wishes to have addressed.

**The overall assessment** asks the practitioner to summarise the cultural and social formulation, the cultural and social stressors and supports, and the cultural aspects of the clinician–patient relationship.

The function of the interview is threefold, and all three are relevant to the ethical requirements of the profession. It is a **clinical instrument**, in that the explanatory models and the family views frequently change the treatment plan. It is a **quality instrument**, in that it surfaces mismatches between the practitioner's assumptions and the young person's, which is the most common source of treatment failure with young people from minority backgrounds. And it is an **ethical instrument**, in that it establishes the young person's own account as a legitimate and necessary input to the formulation, and it does so in a form that can be documented, reviewed in supervision, and audited.

## 6. Models of Cultural Identity Development

Three models of ethnic identity development are standard in the literature, and the course requires students to be able to describe and compare them.

**Phinney's multistage model** describes ethnic identity development as a progression through a **pre-identity** stage, in which the young person has not thought much about ethnicity and may have internalised negative stereotypes; an **identity exploration** stage, in which the young person is actively questioning and learning about their own and others' ethnic identities; and an **identity achievement** stage, in which the young person has a secure, positive, and well-integrated ethnic identity. The model is empirically well supported, and the finding that the stage is associated with outcomes — with better psychological functioning, better academic engagement, and better family relationships — is one of the more reliable findings in the multicultural literature. Its clinical use is to predict that a young person in the pre-identity stage is unlikely to respond to an intervention that requires them to work explicitly with cultural meaning, and that the exploratory stage is the appropriate time to support that work.

**Helms' model** describes the movement from **unexamined identity** through **identity crisis** to **identity integration**, and emphasises the different developmental tasks and coping mechanisms characteristic of each stage. Its particular contribution is to the development of an explicit model for the multicultural person, and to the observation that the stages of majority and minority group identity development are parallel but not identical.

**DeChick's model** adapts Phinney's model to the specific case of the biracial and bicultural individual, and is the model to consult for a young person with a mixed heritage who is navigating multiple communities.

All three models are heuristic, all were developed on limited samples, and all are used in the field somewhat more confidently than the evidence warrants. Their value to a student is that they provide a developmental language for a clinical observation, and that they make it possible to distinguish a young person's developmental task from a pathology.

## 7. The Limits of the Multicultural Framework

Three limits should be named.

**The limits of the "culture" as a unit.** The multicultural framework is organised around culture as a category of population, and the categories available are those produced by the census, the immigration system, and the academic interest of the dominant culture. A young person whose identity is organised around a sexuality, a disability, a class position, a subculture, a fandom, a faith practice, or a political commitment is not well served by a framework whose central variable is ethnicity. Chapter 49 treats this directly.

**The limits of the "competence" framing.** Even in its third generation, the framework retains the structure of the practitioner being the competent one and the cultural group being the object of understanding. The critical and postcolonial literature in counselling, psychology, and education has been clear that this structure reproduces the epistemology of the colonial encounter, and that a genuinely responsive practice requires the practitioner to hold their own framework as one framework among several, and to be genuinely willing to be organised by the young person's account of the situation rather than by the professional's categories.

**The limits of the evidence.** As Section 3 indicated, the research base for culturally adapted interventions is limited, the research base for particular cultural groups is thin, and the field's confidence outruns its evidence. The honest position is that cultural responsiveness is ethically required, theoretically indicated, and empirically under-supported — and that the ethical requirement stands even where the evidence does not, because the alternative is to deliver an intervention whose assumptions have not been checked against the young person they will be delivered to.
