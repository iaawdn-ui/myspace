# Emotion-Focused Therapy

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of emotion-focused therapy and describe the clinical problems that produced it.
- State the principles of the emotion-focused model, including the position that emotion is the primary datum in therapy.
- Describe the model of emotion as a response to a need, and the categories of emotion the model identifies.
- Explain the concept of the change point and the two components of the process.
- Describe the emotional processing steps and the in-session emotion coaching.
- Apply the emotion-focused framework to a case involving an adolescent in emotional distress.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of emotion-focused work, including the questions of the cultural status of emotional expression and of the relationship between emotion and need.

## Chapter Overview

Emotion-focused therapy, in the form Leslie Greenberg and colleagues developed it, is unusual in this book in making the client's **emotional experience** rather than the client's thoughts, behaviour, or relationships the primary object of the work. Its founding observation was clinical and unremarkable: **clients change when they are able to identify, experience, and express their core emotions, and they do not change when they do the other things first.**

That observation was in part a reaction to a specific and widely felt failure of the cognitive therapies. Some clients arrive in cognitive therapy able to dispute their thoughts perfectly and leave unchanged, and the reason is that the problem is not in the thoughts. The client believes "I am worthless" and disputes it accurately — "I have got a job, I passed my exam, my aunt says I'm fine" — and the dispute changes nothing, because the feeling of worthlessness is not generated by the belief and is not removed by its refutation. The feeling is generated by an unmet need, and the needs are the clinical target.

The model has two further features that make it valuable with young people. The first is that it is **brief and focused**: the treatment has a limited number of sessions, a specific target emotion, and a specific procedure, and a young person who has been offered years of weekly sessions that produce no change is often well served by an approach that names a target and a timeframe. The second is that it is **developmentally consonant with the adolescent's task**: the adolescent's central developmental work involves the construction of a new self, and the emotions of that period — the shame, the anger, the grief, the anxiety — are the raw material of the work of becoming an adult, and the model treats them as such rather than as symptoms to be eliminated.

The chapter presents the model, the theory of emotion, the change point, the processing steps, the developmental adaptations, and the critical analysis, which includes the question of whether the model's account of emotion is culturally universal, and it is not.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The two foundations

The model has two intellectual sources, and both are in this book already.

**The client-centred tradition of Chapter 10.** The empathic attunement, the congruence, and the understanding of the client's frame of reference, and the finding that the conditions of the relationship are themselves therapeutic.

**The cognitive tradition of Chapter 16.** The technique of disputing maladaptive thoughts, which Greenberg did not abandon but repurposed: the cognitive disputation becomes one of several ways of responding to an emotion, and it is the *least* effective of them for most clients.

### 1.2 The development

Greenberg and colleagues began the work in the late 1970s at the University of British Columbia, initially as a research programme comparing a structured, emotion-focused treatment with a structured cognitive treatment for depression. The design was unusual in asking the researchers to code the process of therapy in detail, and the coding produced the finding that changed the field: **emotional change, rather than cognitive change, was the best predictor of improvement**, and the improvement in emotion in the session was the best single predictor of improvement in the session.

The programme then developed into a series of controlled studies that established the specific procedures, and the model was extended from depression to a wide range of presentations, including the anxiety disorders, the eating disorders, the interpersonal problems, the trauma-related presentations, and the problems of couples, and it was adapted for young people.

### 1.3 The place in the field

Emotion-focused therapy is not a mainstream school, and its position is unusual: it has a good research base, a well-specified protocol, and relatively limited clinical adoption. A student should be aware of the reasons, and they are instructive.

**The model requires a level of emotional processing that not every client can reach.** The model's procedures depend on a client who can access, identify, and stay with a core emotion. A young person with a very restricted emotional range, with a very high level of dissociation, or with a very limited capacity for reflection may not be able to do what the model asks, and the field's own position is that these presentations need a longer course of preparatory work.

**The model requires a trained practitioner.** The procedures look simple and are not, and a badly delivered emotion-focused session in which the practitioner pushes the client toward a feeling the client cannot bear is not a neutral event.

**The model's account of emotion is under-specified.** The theory of emotion as a response to a need is elegant and is not a theory of emotion in the sense that the affective science would recognise, and a student who wants a developmental account of the emotional system should read the affective science as well as Greenberg.

## 2. The Core Principles

### 2.1 Emotion as the primary datum

The model's foundational claim is that **emotion is the primary datum in therapy**, and that the other elements — cognition, behaviour, and the relationship — are in the service of emotion.

The claim is not that cognition is unimportant. It is that the cognitive intervention is subordinate: a belief is examined because it is generating or blocking an emotion, and a behaviour is examined because it is serving an emotional function, and the alliance is examined because a rupture in it will block the emotional work.

### 2.2 The nature of emotion in the model

Greenberg's account of emotion has four components, and a student should be able to state them.

**Emotion as a response.** Emotion is a response to a need — the need for a secure attachment, the need for autonomy, the need for control, the need for approval, the need for a sense of worth, the need for a connection with others, the need for a sense of identity. The primary emotions are organised in relation to these needs, and the specific emotion reflects the specific need and the specific way in which it is being frustrated.

**Emotion as adaptive.** Every emotion serves a purpose. Anger mobilises against a violation; shame mobilises against exposure; sadness mobilises against loss; fear mobilises against threat. The problematic emotions are not the ones that are wrong in themselves but the ones that are misdirected, excessive, or aimed at the wrong target.

**Emotion as tolerable and changeable.** Healthy emotions are felt, expressed, and then resolved. A primary emotion that is adequately processed ends; the problem arises when a secondary emotion — the feeling that arises in the reaction to the primary one — blocks it. Guilt is a secondary emotion that typically arises in the reaction to anger, and it blocks the anger and the anger's work.

**Emotion as transcending.** The most developed part of the model's account is the proposal that the highest form of emotional life is a form of transcendence in which the self is expanded beyond the narrowness of the immediate situation, and the "solution" emotions in the model — pride, confidence, calm, and a sense of the other as a potential ally — are understood as the forms of emotional transcendence.

### 2.3 The categories of emotion

The model's categories of emotion are clinically useful and a student should be able to use them.

**Primary emotions** are the adaptive responses to the frustration of a need: anger, sadness, fear, shame, joy, and the physical sensations that accompany them. They are "maladaptive" only when they are misdirected, excessive, or persistent.

**Secondary emotions** are the feelings that arise in the reaction to the primary ones, and they function to block the primary emotion's processing: guilt typically blocks anger, depression blocks anger and grief, shame blocks pride, and anxiety blocks the approach that the underlying need is demanding. The clinical target is very often the secondary emotion, because it is the secondary emotion that is generating the presenting complaint.

**Emotion schemes** are a generalisation from a specific emotional response, and they are the level at which the model locates the enduring vulnerability: "if I am not in control, I will be helpless"; "if I am rejected, I am worthless"; "if I fail, I am a failure".

**Emotional responses and their variations** — the way in which a particular emotion is expressed and experienced, which is a matter of the person's habitual response rather than of the emotion itself.

### 2.4 The position on the relation between emotion and cognition

The model is clear that **cognition and emotion are mutually determining** and that the direction of determination varies from person to person and from moment to moment. Some clients change when they think differently; others change when they feel differently; and many need both.

The clinical implication is diagnostic rather than theoretical: the practitioner must determine, for this client and at this point, which is the bottleneck. Greenberg's own phrasing is that the practitioner should ask whether the client's problem is primarily a thinking problem, a feeling problem, or a doing problem, and the answer determines the intervention.

## 3. The Change Point

### 3.1 The concept

The **change point** is the specific point in the processing of a core emotion at which the client is most likely to change, and it is the most distinctive technical contribution of the model. The concept is derived from the observation that clients are most amenable to change at a particular moment in the emotional process, and that practitioners who are not aware of the moment will either press too hard too early or press too softly too late.

**The change point is the moment when the client is experiencing the core emotion at its most, and is simultaneously able to remain present with it.** Before the change point, the client is either not in the emotion or is being protected from it by a secondary emotion. After the change point, the emotion has been resolved or has been abandoned in favour of a secondary emotion or a defence.

The change point is thus a narrow window, and the practitioner's task is to find it and to work in it.

### 3.2 The two components

The change point has two components, and a practitioner who recognises only one of them will miss the moment.

**The emotional component** is the client's experiencing of the core emotion at its peak, with the associated physiological arousal, the facial expression, the voice quality, and the body posture.

**The cognitive component** is the client's accessing of the core appraisal — the meaning of the situation for the core need. The appraisal is what determines the emotion, and a client who is feeling the emotion at its peak but who has not accessed the appraisal is in a state of arousal without a meaning, and the state will be resolved by a secondary emotion or a defence rather than by a reorganisation.

The two components occur together in the moment of the change point, and a practitioner who produces the emotional component without the cognitive component has produced a discharge rather than a change.

### 3.3 The practitioner's task

The task is a sequence, and it is worth stating it as a set of steps.

**Identify the core emotion.** The work begins in the assessment, and the identification of the core emotion is one of the deliverables of the assessment phase.

**Find the blockage.** Almost always a secondary emotion, and the task is to identify it and to name it for the client.

**Work through the blockage.** The practitioner does this with warmth and with some assertiveness, and the standard move is to say what the practitioner thinks the client is feeling and to invite the client to consider it. This is where the "empty chair" and the "chair work" of the process stage becomes relevant.

**Achieve the change point.** The client is then helped to stay with the core emotion, and the practitioner supports the arousal, and the practitioner's own arousal and warmth are the instruments.

**Facilitate the resolution.** The resolution is not the end of the session; it is the beginning of the new emotional state, and the practitioner helps the client experience the solution emotion.

### 3.4 The emotional processing steps

The standard procedure for the processing stage has five steps, and a student should be able to name them.

1. **Attune and focus.** The practitioner attends to the core emotion, usually by reflecting it, and sometimes by exaggerating it, and the exaggeration helps to bring the emotion into focus.
2. **Confirm and clarify.** The practitioner confirms the emotion, sometimes by naming it directly, and the naming is a key moment, because a client who has never had their core emotion named by another person is being given a new experience.
3. **Congruence and the empathic stance.** The practitioner is present with the emotion rather than managing it, and this is the "being with" stance, and it is a demanding stance to hold.
4. **Facilitating the expression.** The practitioner encourages the expression of the core emotion, and the expression is the vehicle of the processing, and the practitioner matches the arousal.
5. **Reaching the resolution.** The emotion resolves, and the practitioner helps the client notice the new emotional state, and the noticing is essential, because the new state is the thing that will be available to them in the future.

## 4. The In-Session Emotion Coaching

The second major procedure, and the one most applicable to work with young people, is the **emotion coaching**, and it comes from a different research tradition — the work on parental emotion regulation with young children, and the body of research on the "emotion socialization" of children.

### 4.1 The procedures

**The empathic statement.** The practitioner reflects the child's or young person's emotional state in a statement, and the statement is a hypothesis that the young person can correct, and the correction is itself data.

**The emotion labelling.** The practitioner names the emotion, using a label from a list that is introduced gradually and is not pejorative. "Frustrated" is a safer first label than "furious", and "disappointed" is safer than "sad".

**The validation.** The practitioner validates the emotion by stating its legitimacy in the situation. The validation is of the emotion, not of the behaviour, and the distinction matters, and a practitioner who validates the behaviour has made an error.

**The problem-solving and the choices.** The practitioner helps the young person identify the problem, and then the choices. The order is important: the emotion is processed first and the problem-solving second, and reversing the order produces a young person who solves the problem without ever having been with the feeling that generated it.

### 4.2 The emotion coaching with parents

The most widely used application of the emotion coaching is with the parents of young children, and it is effective enough that a student should know it even if the population served is older. The structure is a hierarchy of responses to a child's distress: first, the parent is taught to **empathise** and to name the emotion; second, to **validate** the emotion; third, to **label** the emotion and set a limit on the behaviour if necessary; and fourth, to **problem-solve**.

The teaching is done with video-feedback where possible, and it is the first and the third steps that parents most often skip, and they are the steps that matter most.

### 4.3 The emotion coaching with adolescents

With an adolescent, the same procedure is used with two modifications, and the modifications are what make it usable.

**The developmental modification.** The adolescent is not told what the emotion is and is not told what to do; the practitioner labels and validates and then asks. The adolescent's own label is preferred, and the adolescent's own solution is required, because a solution supplied by an adult is a solution the adolescent will not use.

**The relational modification.** The emotion coaching is often best delivered through the parent, because the adolescent's capacity to be with their own emotions is being undermined by the parent's response to them. A young person whose mother's response to her distress is to become more distressed cannot learn to self-soothe, and the work with the mother is the prerequisite.
