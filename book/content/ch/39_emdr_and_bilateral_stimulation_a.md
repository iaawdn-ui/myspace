# EMDR and Bilateral Stimulation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the history of the eye movement desensitisation procedure and the development of EMDR, and state the evidence that led to the method's acceptance.
- State the adaptive information processing model and explain how it accounts for the efficacy of a procedure that was originally thought to be effective because of the eye movements.
- Describe the eight phases of the EMDR protocol and the bilateral stimulation methods.
- Explain the mechanisms proposed for the effect of bilateral stimulation, and evaluate the evidence for each.
- Describe the adaptations of EMDR for children and adolescents, including the specific modifications and the evidence for them.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of EMDR practice, including the questions of the acceptability of the procedure and of the treatment of the memory.

## Chapter Overview

In 1987, a psychotherapist working with a woman who was suffering from a disturbing memory noticed that she reported a reduction in the distress associated with the memory while moving her eyes from side to side. The observation was made with one patient, it was not a controlled experiment, and it was made in a context in which the prevailing assumption was that eye movements had no psychological effect whatsoever.

Twenty years later, the procedure was a first-line treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder in adults, it was endorsed in the guidelines of the major international bodies, and the underlying mechanism was the subject of one of the more consequential scientific controversies in the history of psychotherapy.

The story is worth telling carefully, because it illustrates a methodological point that the practitioner should take seriously: the eye movements were almost certainly not doing the therapeutic work, and the treatment is effective anyway. The contemporary understanding is that the bilateral stimulation is a **provocative technique** — an apparatus that reliably elicits and sustains the dual attention and the internal association that the treatment requires, and that the same work can be achieved by other means. The evidence for this has been gathered by a series of well-controlled studies comparing eye movements with other forms of bilateral stimulation and with a matched control condition, and the results are more consistent than the field's earlier polemics suggested.

For work with young people, the most important practical facts are these. The procedure with children is markedly different from the adult procedure, and the differences are not cosmetic. The **assessment and preparation phases are longer**, the young person is taught the technique themselves, the clinician is far more active, the **parent is in the room**, and the **body and the regulation work of Chapter 40 comes first**. The evidence for the paediatric protocol is good, and it is better than the evidence for most of the treatments in this book in the specific population with a specific disorder.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The origin

Francine Shapiro was working with a client who described a memory of an assault that she found unutterably distressing. In the course of the session, the client spontaneously moved her eyes from side to side, and reported, with some surprise, that the memory had become less vivid and less disturbing. Shapiro asked the client to do the eye movements deliberately, and observed a repeat of the effect.

Shapiro then developed the procedure as a systematic treatment, and the early papers described it in terms of a new kind of "information processing" in which the eye movements appeared to facilitate the "unblocking" of traumatic memories. The early formulations were not well specified, and the early trials were criticised, sometimes severely, for their methodology.

### 1.2 The controversy

The controversy was of a familiar and familiar-seeming kind. The critics' arguments were that the eye movements had no established physiological effect, that the method had been given a mystified theoretical justification, and that the early trials had used inappropriate controls. The proponents' arguments were that the empirical results were better than those of the competing treatments and that a mechanism was not a precondition of efficacy.

The field resolved the controversy in a way that is instructive. A series of well-controlled studies established several things: the **efficacy** of the procedure, which was not seriously in dispute even at the height of the controversy; the **specificity** of the bilateral stimulation, in that the procedure is more effective than a matched control that omits the eye movements; and the **absence of a specific mechanism**, in that the effect is found with several different forms of bilateral stimulation and with non-bilateral controls in some studies, which suggests that the stimulation functions as a device for sustaining a particular attentional state rather than as a treatment in its own right.

### 1.3 From eye movements to EMDR

The procedure was renamed and the theory was revised, and the shift is significant. The current model is the **adaptive information processing model**, which locates the pathology not in the memory but in the *failure to process* the memory, and the treatment in the *completion* of the processing. The bilateral stimulation is now understood as the element that elicits and sustains the associative processing while the memory is held in focus.

The renaming was not merely cosmetic. The change in the theory is a change in what the practitioner is doing: the clinician is not moving information out of the memory but is facilitating the patient's own processing of a memory that has not been completed.

### 1.4 The spread

The spread of EMDR into clinical practice has been remarkable, and it has been driven less by the mechanism debate than by three things: the treatment is brief, the guidelines recommend it, and it works. It is now used for post-traumatic stress disorder, for the phobias, for the obsessive-compulsive disorder, for the panic disorder, for the specific phobias, for the grief, and — increasingly, in the paediatric literature — for the specific procedural and accidental trauma of childhood, which is a considerably better fit for the paediatric protocol than the developmental trauma is.

## 2. The Adaptive Information Processing Model

### 2.1 The central claim

The model's claim is that **memory, once encoded and integrated into the general network of autobiographical knowledge, is permanently available for use and for change, and that many pathological conditions are the result of a failure of this processing**.

The implication is that the pathology is not in the content of the memory. The memory is accurate; it happened; the young person was in a car accident and the memory of the car accident is correct. The pathology is in the *storage* of the memory — in the fact that it has remained in a form in which it is experienced as a current event, in which it is accompanied by the physiological response that accompanied the original event, and in which it is not linked into the network of the rest of the person's life.

### 2.2 The components of the model

**The unprocessed memory.** A memory that has been encoded and stored in a form that remains vivid, that is experienced in the present tense, that is associated with the original autonomic and muscular response, and that is disconnected from the general narrative of the person's life.

**The dysfunction.** The symptoms are the consequence of the unprocessed memory: the intrusive images, the nightmares, the avoidance, the hyperarousal, the negative beliefs about the self and the world that the memory has generalised into.

**The link.** The missing element is the *link* between the unprocessed memory and the rest of the person's autobiographical knowledge. When the memory has been linked — when the young person can place it in a narrative of a life in which the accident is one event among many, in which it is over, and in which it is not the whole of who they are — the symptoms reduce.

**The bilateral stimulation.** The device that elicits and sustains the associative processing while the memory is held in focus.

### 2.3 The model and the treatment's logic

The treatment follows directly. The young person is asked to hold the image of the memory in focus while the bilateral stimulation is delivered, and to follow the eye movements wherever they lead, and to report whatever comes. Over the course of the processing, the memory changes: it becomes less vivid, the autonomic response diminishes, and the young person begins to report new associations, some of them painful and some of them neutral and some of them about the present rather than the past.

The final stage, the **installation** of the new associations, is the point at which the change becomes durable, and the installation is done by the clinician rather than by the young person, and it is a distinctive and often under-taught element of the procedure.

## 3. The Eight Phases

### 3.1 The phases

The protocol has eight phases, and a student should be able to name all eight and say what each is for.

**Phase one: the history and the stabilisation.** A full history, including the trauma history, the current symptoms, the medical and the psychiatric history, the developmental and the social history, the current resources, and the current level of functioning. The purpose is to establish that the young person is sufficiently stabilised to proceed and to establish the baseline against which the change will be assessed. A student should be able to state the criteria in Chapter 38 for this phase, because the EMDR history phase is a phase one in the phase model.

**Phase two: the preparation.** The preparation of the young person for the procedure itself: the explanation of what will happen, the establishment of the bilateral stimulation method, the teaching of the "internal focus of attention" and of the "external focus of attention" as cognitive anchors, the checking of the capacity to stay in the window of tolerance, and — for the paediatric protocol — the involvement of the parent.

**Phase three: the assessment.** The measurement of the subjective units of disturbance (the SUD), the body sensations, and the vividness of the image, for the selected memory, and the establishment of the baseline values that will be tracked through the processing. The clinician identifies the specific memory that will be worked on, and the choice of the memory is a clinical judgement and not a neutral matter.

**Phase four: the reprocessing.** The heart of the procedure. The young person holds the image in focus, follows the bilateral stimulation, and reports whatever comes. The clinician monitors the SUD and the body sensations, and adjusts the stimulation and the pacing, and periodically asks the young person what they are noticing.

**Phase five: the installation.** The new associations — the new images, the new beliefs, the new body sensations, the new links to the past and to the present — are explicitly installed, one at a time, with the bilateral stimulation. This phase is commonly the most useful and the most neglected, and a student should know that a practitioner who stops at the end of the reprocessing has delivered an incomplete treatment.

**Phase six: the body scan.** A bilateral stimulation period in which the young person attends to the body and reports any remaining sensations. The purpose is to complete the processing of the autonomic component.

**Phase seven: the completion.** A final bilateral stimulation period, and the young person is asked what they would like to address, and any remaining material is addressed briefly.

**Phase eight: the review.** The re-measurement of the SUD, the body sensations, and the vividness, and the review of the progress across the whole treatment.

### 3.2 The SUD and the other measures

The **subjective units of disturbance** are the young person's own rating of the distress associated with the memory, on a scale of zero to ten, or on the widely used pictorial scale, and they are the primary outcome measure within the protocol and the most important thing to chart.

The **body sensations** are the physical sensations associated with the memory, and the **vividness** is the rating of how vivid the image is. These three measures are recorded at the assessment, at the end of each reprocessing set, and at the completion, and the chart is a clinical document and should be part of the case record.

### 3.3 The bilateral stimulation methods

The methods are of three kinds, and the choice among them is made on the basis of the young person's preference, their comfort, and the evidence.

**Horizontal eye movements**, the original method, with the tracking of the therapist's hand moving from side to side.

**Bilateral auditory stimulation**, with alternating tones delivered through headphones, one ear at a time, so that the sound alternates from left to right.

**Bilateral tactile stimulation**, with the hands, or with the feet, or with the shoulders, tapped alternately.

**The butterfly variant**, in which the young person taps their own thighs alternately while tracking the therapist's moving hand with their eyes, and which is used with young children.

The most common finding in the comparative studies is that all of the effective methods work and that the choice is best made collaboratively.

## 4. The Mechanisms

### 4.1 The competing accounts

Three families of explanation have been proposed, and a student should be able to state them and the evidence for each.

**The ocular motor account.** The eye movements produce a specific effect on the autonomic nervous system, and in particular a reduction in the sympathetic arousal. The evidence for a specific autonomic effect of horizontal eye movements is weak and the findings are inconsistent, and the account does not explain why the auditory and the tactile methods work as well as they do.

**The attentional and the associational account.** The bilateral stimulation occupies the attention in a way that permits the free associative flow that the reprocessing requires, and that reduces the "avoidance" of the memory that characterises the unprocessed form. This is the account with the best support, and the support comes from the studies that found the effect with several stimulation methods and that found a reduced effect when the bilateral stimulation was replaced by a non-bilateral control.

**The "novelty" and the working-memory account.** The bilateral stimulation occupies a part of the working memory that is otherwise occupied by the avoidance of the memory, and the consequence is a displacement of the memory into a different representational system. This account has a plausible neurocognitive profile and some supportive findings, and it remains contested.

### 4.2 The consensus position

The field's current position is that the mechanism is not known, that the bilateral stimulation is a **provocative technique** rather than a treatment in its own right, and that the therapeutic work is the associative processing that the stimulation makes possible.

This position has an important clinical implication: **the practitioner is not required to believe the mechanism in order to deliver the treatment competently**, and a practitioner who is uncomfortable with the bilateral stimulation can deliver the same treatment with a different attention-holding method, and the protocol's clinical structure is what does the work.

## 5. The Paediatric Adaptations

### 5.1 The differences from the adult protocol

The paediatric protocol is a genuinely different protocol and a practitioner should know the differences.

**The assessment is longer and more play-based.** A young person of seven cannot give a detailed trauma narrative, and the clinician gathers the history through play, through the parent's account, through the drawings, and through the young person's own account in whatever form the young person can manage.

**The preparation is longer and more active.** The clinician teaches the technique explicitly, and the young person practises it, and the young person chooses the method, and the young person is taught the "safe place" and the "happy place" and the "calm place" in detail, and these are used throughout the procedure as the internal anchor.

**The parent is in the room.** The presence of a safe caregiver is the single most important element in the paediatric protocol, and the clinician actively coaches the parent during the procedure, and the parent's role is to be a secure base, to model calm, and to be available for comfort.

**The body work comes first.** The clinician spends a substantial amount of time in the preparation phase on the body's signals and on the young person's capacity to notice them, because the somatic component of the trauma memory is the last to change and the first to be triggered.

**The number of sets is small and the pace is slow.** A young person processes for a few minutes and then stops, and the sets are short, and the young person is given control over the stopping.

**The language is concrete and the images are drawn.** The young person's own drawings of the memory and of the safe place are used, and the drawings are worked with.

**The reprocessing is more affectively intense and the clinician intervenes more.** The clinician's "resourcing" interventions, in which the young person is taken back to the safe place and to the positive resource, are used frequently, and the protocol's guidance is that the clinician should not let the young person's distress exceed a certain level.

### 5.2 The evidence

The evidence for the paediatric EMDR protocol is good, and the studies are generally randomised controlled trials with standardised outcome measures, and the results are consistently positive, with large effect sizes.

The evidence is best for the **single-incident traumatic events** in childhood: the accidents, the disasters, the school shootings, the medicalprocedures, and the sexual abuse where the event is discrete. The evidence for the **developmental trauma** is much weaker, and the reason is methodological: the developmental trauma is not a discrete memory, the standard SUD measure does not apply well, and the protocol's structure assumes a specific and bounded target.

The comparative evidence shows the paediatric EMDR to be **comparable to the trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy** on outcome measures, and in some studies superior, and a recent network meta-analysis of the paediatric trauma treatments placed it among the first-line recommendations.
