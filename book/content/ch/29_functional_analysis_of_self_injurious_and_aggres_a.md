# Functional Analysis of Self-Injurious and Aggressive Behaviour

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the safety-first framework that governs work with self-injurious and aggressive behaviour.
- Describe the epidemiology, the range of presentation, and the differential diagnosis of self-injury and aggression in children and adolescents.
- Describe the functional analysis of self-injurious behaviour, including the assessment of the antecedents, the behaviours, and the medical factors.
- Apply the four functional classes and the setting demands to the analysis of self-injury and aggression.
- Describe the safety interventions for self-injury and aggression, including the physical, environmental, and relational components.
- Describe the treatment approaches for each function, and state the specific treatment of aggression maintained by escape and by attention.
- Explain the standards of care and the documentation required for work with this behaviour.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of work with dangerous behaviour, including the questions of restraint, of restrictive intervention, and of the young person's dignity.

## Chapter Overview

This chapter concerns behaviour that can kill or permanently injure a young person, and it is written with a different set of conventions from the rest of the book. There is no discussion here of whether the behaviour should change. The behaviour must change. The clinical questions are about what the behaviour is accomplishing, what the safest intervention is, and what treatment will be effective and safe in the long run.

Two behavioural presentations are treated together because they frequently co-occur, because the analysis is parallel, and because the young people who present with one frequently present with the other later. They are, however, distinct in their consequences, their risk profiles, and their treatment implications, and the chapter keeps them distinct.

The safety framework has four elements and it should be stated at the outset because it governs everything else in the chapter. **The safety of the young person comes first and is not contingent on the assessment being complete.** **The least restrictive effective procedure governs the choice of intervention.** **The treatment of the behaviour is not the treatment of the condition**, and a young person whose self-injury is a symptom of a disorder requires the treatment of the disorder, not only the management of the injury. And **the intervention must be accompanied by a plan for the young person's dignity**, because the interventions that protect a young person from harm are frequently experienced as punishments, and the young person who experiences them as punishments will not tell anyone when the next episode is building.

A working knowledge of this material is not optional for a practitioner who works with young people, and a working knowledge of the ethical framework is not optional either, because the interventions in this area are the ones in which a practitioner's decisions can be challenged and reviewed.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 From the asylum to the institution

Self-injurious behaviour has a long and grim history in the institutional treatment of people with learning disabilities and mental illness. The nineteenth-century practice of "moral treatment" included elaborate mechanical devices designed to prevent self-injury, and the twentieth century added the padded cell, the restraint, and the surgical intervention for the self-injurious behaviour of a small number of people with a severe genetic condition. The literature is a record of a systematic failure to understand the behaviour before trying to control it, and it is a useful reminder that an unexplained behaviour is usually treated harshly.

### 1.2 The functional analysis of self-injury

The decisive development was the work of Iwata and colleagues from the 1980s, and its central finding was methodological. Before that work, self-injury was treated on the assumption that it served an unknown purpose, and the intervention was a physical one. Iwata's group demonstrated that the behaviour could be analysed experimentally in the same way as any other behaviour: by manipulating the consequences across conditions and observing whether the behaviour changed.

The findings were published in 1982 and 1994 and they are the foundation of everything that followed. Self-injury in the population studied was overwhelmingly maintained by **automatic reinforcement** — the behaviour produced its own sensory consequence — and, in the remainder, by escape from demands and by attention. The proportion of cases in which the behaviour was maintained by automatic reinforcement was much higher than had been assumed, and this finding changed practice fundamentally, because it meant that a young person whose self-injury is automatically maintained requires a very different plan from one whose self-injury is escape-maintained.

The therapeutic consequence of the automatic reinforcement finding is the treatment of the sensory consequences themselves, through the pharmacological management of the underlying condition, through a programme of alternative sensory stimulation, and through the extinction of the behaviour — and the practical consequence is that the "treatment as usual" of self-injury, which was containment, is largely ineffective, because containment removes the behaviour but does not remove the maintaining contingency.

### 1.3 The function of aggression

The functional analysis of aggression developed in parallel, with the critical contribution of the studies of aggression maintained by **attention** and by **escape**, and the demonstration that a substantial proportion of physical aggression in young people with learning disabilities is escape-maintained, is a request for help, or is the only available form of communication, and not a desire to hurt.

This finding has had a profound effect on practice, because the response to a young person's aggression in most settings is escalation, and escalation is exactly what maintains the behaviour.

### 1.4 The regulatory history

Work with this behaviour in the United States is now subject to a dense regulatory framework, and a practitioner must know it before working in a setting where it applies. The relevant framework includes the federal protections for individuals with disabilities, including the requirement of a functional behavioural assessment before a change of placement and the manifestation determination procedure; the prohibitions on the use of seclusion and of physical restraint as written in the federal regulations, and the conditions under which they may be used; the requirements of the Americans with Disabilities Act in relation to the modification of policies and practices; and the state-level licensing standards for residential and inpatient settings, which vary considerably and are frequently more specific than the federal rules.

The practical requirement is straightforward and should be stated in the chapter's conclusions: a practitioner working with this behaviour in a regulated setting is implementing the plan of a team, under supervision, within a documented protocol, and every element of the plan that touches the young person's body, liberty, or dignity is subject to review.

## 2. Safety

### 2.1 The hierarchy

The safety hierarchy for work with dangerous behaviour is ordered, and the order is not negotiable.

**Level 1: Immediate physical safety.** The prevention of imminent harm to the young person or to others. This is the only consideration that overrides every other consideration, including the young person's assent, the family's preference, and the practitioner's own discomfort.

**Level 2: Medical assessment.** The determination that the injury or the aggression is not a medical emergency requiring other care. A young person who is self-injuring may have an undiagnosed condition contributing to it, and a young person who is aggressive may be in pain, or have a neurological condition, or be in a state of delirium, and in each of these cases the behavioural analysis is the wrong frame entirely.

**Level 3: The functional assessment.** The determination of what the behaviour is accomplishing. This cannot be conducted while the young person is in acute danger, and a plan that requires the assessment to be completed before the safety interventions are in place is a plan that will fail.

**Level 4: The treatment.** The long-term reduction of the behaviour and the increase of the young person's repertoire.

### 2.2 The safety plan for self-injury

A safety plan for self-injury has five components, and it is written down, shared with everyone who works with the young person, and rehearsed.

**The functional prediction.** The circumstances in which the behaviour is likely, stated specifically enough to be recognisable, and derived from the functional analysis if it has been conducted, and from the pattern of previous episodes if it has not.

**The response to the early indicators.** The physiological and behavioural precursors — a change in breathing, a change in the pattern of movement, the sudden quietness, the change of tone — and what the adult should do at that point. The early indicators are specific to the individual and are usually known only from careful observation, and they are the most valuable single piece of information in a safety plan.

**The response to the behaviour.** The procedure, specified in advance, and practised, and consistent across all adults. The procedure should specify the physical approach, the words used, the position of the adult, and what is done if the young person resists.

**The provision of the alternative.** The sensory input or the break or the help that the analysis indicates the young person is seeking, available at the moment of the early indicators and not only after the behaviour.

**The medical and the psychological response after an episode.** The physical care, the documentation, the review, and the decision about whether the episode changes the plan.

### 2.3 The safety plan for aggression

The safety plan for aggression has five components as well, and the first of them is different in kind.

**The identification of the target of the aggression.** Who is at risk: the young person, other young people, staff, family, or the wider community. The risk assessment should be explicit about the worst credible outcome, and the practitioner should be willing to state that a young person has a realistic potential to kill, because the risk assessment is useless if it understates the risk.

**The identification of the precursors**, as for self-injury, with the addition of the situational variables: the time of day, the staffing level, the noise level, the presence of a specific peer, the state of the young person's medication.

**The avoidance and the environmental modification.** The changes to the environment that reduce the likelihood of the episode — the change of route, the reduction of noise, the staffing adjustment, the removal of the trigger from the space.

**The response to the behaviour**, specified and rehearsed, and including the specification of what the adult does when the young person is escalating, which is usually the most important part of the plan and the part most often improvised.

**The plan for the aftermath**, including the repair of any relationship damaged by the episode, which is a clinical task and not a courtesy. A young person who has just attacked a member of staff and is then treated as a danger for a month has learned that violence is the only route to significance, and the plan for the aftermath is the part of the plan that prevents the next escalation.

### 2.4 The role of restrictive interventions

Restrictive interventions — physical restraint, seclusion, and the chemical equivalents — are the most fraught part of this work, and the professional position should be stated plainly.

They are **last-resort interventions**, used only when the young person poses an imminent danger to themselves or to others, and when less restrictive interventions have been tried and have failed, and when the intervention is used for the shortest possible time, and with the monitoring and the documentation and the review that the law requires.

They are **more likely to cause harm than to prevent it** in the cases in which they are used most often, because they are used with the young people who are most distressed and least responsive, and the restraint of a distressed young person is itself a trigger, and it frequently produces the aggression it is intended to prevent.

They are **subject to oversight**, and a practitioner who uses them without a documented protocol, without the training, and without the review is not practising within the professional standard, whatever the local law happens to permit.

And they are, in the clinical literature, associated with higher rates of injury to the staff, higher rates of injury to the young person, higher rates of subsequent aggression, and worse therapeutic outcomes. This is not an argument against their use in a genuine emergency; it is an argument for the seriousness with which they should be used, and for the institutional responsibility to design the environment so that they are needed less.

## 3. Presentation and Diagnosis

### 3.1 Self-injurious behaviour

Self-injury in children and adolescents presents in a wide range of forms and with a wide range of functions. The forms include: skin picking, scratching, and cutting; head banging and hitting; eye poking; hair pulling; and the more severe forms involving burning or the ingestion of objects. The frequency ranges from occasional and low harm to several times daily and severely injurious.

The differential diagnosis is wide and includes: the stereotyped movement disorders of the autism spectrum; the self-injury associated with specific genetic syndromes, notably Lesch–Nyhan syndrome, Smith–Magenis syndrome, and Cornelia de Lange syndrome; the self-injury associated with a psychotic disorder; the self-injury associated with a trauma history, including the self-harm behaviours of a young person who is being abused; the self-injury associated with a mood disorder; and the self-injury associated with a communication impairment or with an unmet need.

The differential has direct clinical consequences, because the treatment of the self-injury follows the diagnosis. Self-injury in a young person with a genetic syndrome with a characteristic neurochemical profile may be substantially responsive to pharmacological management, and the behavioural plan is then an adjunct rather than the treatment. Self-injury that is a symptom of psychosis requires the treatment of the psychosis. Self-injury that is a response to abuse requires the protection of the young person, not the extinction of the symptom.

### 3.2 Aggression

Aggression likewise presents in a range of forms: verbal aggression and threats; the destruction of property; physical aggression towards peers, towards adults, and towards animals; and the more severe forms of severe violence.

The differential diagnosis includes: the impulsivity and irritability of a mood disorder, including the irritability of a depressive episode, which is a presentation that is frequently missed in adolescents and which is often the correct diagnosis of a young person referred for aggression; the impulsivity of a neurodevelopmental condition; the psychosis, in which aggression may be a response to persecutory beliefs; the disinhibition of a substance; the dysregulation of a trauma response; the learned pattern of a young person who has been in an environment where aggression was the only effective response; and the communication of a young person who has no other way to express a need.

The diagnosis of the mood disorder is the one most often missed and it is worth dwelling on. An adolescent with a depressive episode, with the irritability that is its characteristic presentation in adolescence, is frequently referred to a behaviour service because the irritability presents as aggression. Treating the aggression behaviourally, without treating the depression, will fail, and it will fail in a way that increases the young person's shame.

### 3.3 The assessment

The assessment of dangerous behaviour is comprehensive, and its components are as follows.

**A full history**, including the developmental history, the medical history, the medication history, the trauma history, the family history, the educational history, and the history of previous interventions and their outcomes.

**A physical examination and a medical review**, including a review of the medication, a review of the pain, a review of the sleep, a review of the seizures, and a review of the sensory environment. A young person in pain is an aggressive young person, and this is not a figure of speech.

**The functional analysis**, which is the subject of Sections 4 and 5.

**A risk assessment**, which is a separate document from the functional analysis and has a separate structure: the identification of the risk, the likelihood, the severity, the protective factors, the current level of risk, and the review date. The risk assessment should be written in language that a court, a family, and a young person could each read and understand.

**A functional assessment of the environment**, which examines the antecedents in the physical setting: the noise, the crowding, the lighting, the temperature, the seating, the transitions, the staff-to-young-person ratios, the schedule.

**A developmental assessment**, because the meaning of the behaviour and the feasible alternatives are both developmental facts.

**An assessment of the young person's own view.** A young person who is aggressive has usually been asked about it many times and has usually found the question useless, and the practitioner should approach it differently, with attention to what the young person wants, what makes the behaviour difficult to stop, and what the young person has tried.

## 4. The Functional Analysis of Self-Injury

### 4.1 The stages

The functional analysis of self-injury proceeds in a defined order, and the order reflects the safety hierarchy.

**Stage 1: Safety and medical.** The safety plan is in place, the medical assessment is complete, and the physical management of the injury is available.

**Stage 2: Descriptive assessment.** A structured record of the episodes, their antecedents, their consequences, the settings, the times, the frequency, the severity, and the injury sustained. The descriptive assessment is the foundation of the experimental analysis, and it is also the point at which the pattern of the early indicators is discovered, which is the most clinically valuable output of the whole exercise.

**Stage 3: Experimental analysis.** The manipulation of the consequences across conditions.

**Stage 4: The treatment plan**, specified in terms of the function.

### 4.2 The experimental analysis

The analysis of self-injury uses a set of conditions, each of which is designed to isolate a particular consequence. The conditions run in blocks, typically five to fifteen minutes, in a treatment room or a ward, with the young person observed and the behaviour recorded.

**The alone condition** — the young person is alone in the room, with an observer. The behaviour that persists in this condition is maintained by its own consequences, that is, automatically.

**The attention condition** — the observer attends to the young person, and the attention is delivered in the way that the descriptive assessment suggests the young person finds reinforcing, which is usually the way in which the behaviour has previously obtained attention. The behaviour that increases in this condition is maintained by attention.

**The demand condition** — demands are presented, and the opportunity to escape or to take a break is contingent on the behaviour. The behaviour that increases under demands is escape-maintained.

**The play condition** — the environment is arranged to be engaging and the observer does not attend to the self-injury. The behaviour that decreases in this condition is, by elimination, automatically maintained.

**The control conditions** — the condition in which the consequence of interest is delivered non-contingently, which is the control for the possibility that the mere presence of the consequence is maintaining the behaviour.

**The social control condition** — the young person is observed with another person present who does not respond to the behaviour, which distinguishes the behaviour that requires an audience from the behaviour that does not.

The interpretation follows the logic of Chapter 24. The behaviour that is high in the alone and play conditions and low in the attention and demand conditions is automatically maintained. The behaviour that is high in the attention condition is attention-maintained. The behaviour that is high in the demand condition is escape-maintained. A great many cases show more than one pattern, and the analysis then describes a behaviour with more than one function, which requires a treatment plan for each function.

### 4.3 The interpretation

The interpretation of the analysis has three parts: the function, the setting demands, and the establishing and maintaining conditions.

**The function** is what the behaviour produces: sensory input, escape from a demand, escape from social interaction, or attention from others.

**The setting demands** are the conditions under which the behaviour occurs: automatic, attention, escape, or the combinations.

**The establishing conditions** are how the behaviour came to be performed, and they are usually historical — a period of illness in which the behaviour produced an analgesic effect, a period of abuse in which it produced a change in the perpetrator's behaviour, a period of communication impairment in which it was the only effective signal.

The establishing conditions matter because they are the reason the behaviour is "stuck", and because they indicate that the treatment must address something other than the current contingencies alone.

## 5. The Functional Analysis of Aggression

### 5.1 The procedure

The analysis of aggression follows the same structure, with three modifications.

**The analysis is conducted in the settings in which the aggression occurs**, whenever this is possible, because the contingencies in a ward and the contingencies in a classroom are different, and the behaviour is often maintained by different consequences in each.

**The observation is of the antecedents with particular care**, because the predictors of serious aggression in a setting are often specific and physical: the noise, the crowding, the time of day, the specific peer, the transition, the medication state, the pain.

**The consequences are examined with attention to the adult responses**, because the adult response to the escalation is usually the maintaining contingency. The analysis includes a record of what the adult did immediately after the aggression, and the analysis of the escalation sequence is usually the most informative single component.

### 5.2 The four patterns and their treatments

**Attention-maintained aggression.** The aggression reliably produces adult attention, and the treatment is differential reinforcement of the appropriate behaviour and the planned ignoring of the aggression, and — this is the difficult part — the systematic change of the adults' response so that the escalation no longer produces attention. The treatment also requires the adults to provide the attention at other times, so that the young person is not deprived of it altogether.

**Escape-maintained aggression.** The aggression reliably terminates a demand. The treatment is differential reinforcement of compliance, the planned ignoring of the escalation, the reduction of the demands, and the teaching of an alternative request for help or break. The treatment must include a clear plan for what the adults do when the escalation occurs, because the escalation is the behaviour that is working.

**Automatically maintained aggression.** The aggression produces its own sensory consequence, which is uncommon in aggression as distinct from self-injury, and it occurs most often in young people with a neurodevelopmental condition and a sensory processing difference. The treatment is the management of the sensory environment and the pharmacological treatment of the underlying condition.

**Multiple functions.** A great many young people present with aggression maintained by both escape and attention, and the treatment must address both, and the differential reinforcement of compliance with the extinction of escalation is the core of the plan in either case.

### 5.3 The treatment of the escape-maintained case in detail

Because the escape-maintained case is both the most common and the most reliably treated, it is worth setting out the procedure in the detail in which it should be implemented.

**Reduce the demands.** The demands that the young person is escaping are identified and broken down into smaller steps, and the young person is taught the steps. A young person who cannot do the task will escape the task, and the correct intervention is the teaching of the task.

**Reinforce compliance.** Every instance of compliance is reinforced, immediately, with something small and frequent. The reinforcement must be more immediate and more frequent than the escape it is intended to replace, and the schedule must be dense at the beginning.

**Do not respond to the escalation.** The escalation is ignored — genuinely ignored, with no eye contact, no speech, no raised voice, and no attempt to calm the young person. This is difficult for adults, and it is the part of the plan most likely to fail, and it is the part most in need of training and rehearsal.

**Provide the alternative.** A signal for help, a break card, a specified call to a staff member, which the young person can use, which is trained, and which is honoured immediately when used. The alternative must be available at the same time as the demand, and it must be a real alternative and not a slower route to the same escape, because a young person who can ask for a break in thirty seconds will use it.

**Expect the burst.** The behaviour will increase before it decreases, and the practitioner should tell the young person this in advance, and should not interpret the increase as a failure of the plan.

**Fidelity.** The adults' responses must be consistent, and the fidelity of the response to the escalation is the variable that determines the outcome.
