# Gestalt Therapy: Awareness, Contact, and the Here and Now

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Trace the origins of Gestalt therapy in the perceptual psychology of the Berlin School and the phenomenology of the Würzburg school, and describe the influence of Fritz Perls' own clinical and wartime experience on the theory.
- State the core propositions of Gestalt theory and explain the significance of the organism–environment field.
- Explain the three zones of awareness and their clinical use.
- Describe the cycle of experience, the concept of the interrupted gestalt, and the therapeutic significance of the concept of the "unfinished business".
- Explain the meaning of contact and the here-and-now principle, and apply them to a case.
- Describe the principal techniques — the empty chair, the hot seat, the gestalt prayer, and the dream work — and state the indications and cautions for each.
- Apply Gestalt theory to work with children and adolescents, and identify the developmental modifications required.
- Evaluate the evidence for Gestalt therapy and for the "experiential" methods generally.

## Chapter Overview

Gestalt therapy is the most direct clinical descendant of the perceptual psychology of the Berlin School, and the most misunderstood. The popular image — the empty chair, the shout, the encounter with the here and now — captures the techniques and omits the theory, and the omission matters, because the techniques of Gestalt therapy are derived from a specific and rather precise account of how a person functions, and they are much less useful when detached from it.

The theory is built on three propositions from the experimental psychology of the whole. The first is that perception is organised into wholes, and that the mind does not receive elements and assemble them but responds to organised configurations. The second is that a person is not a collection of parts but a dynamic field in which the person and the environment are mutually determining. The third is that the organising principle of the field is a tendency toward a coherent, completed, and differentiated whole — the actualisation of which is what the word *Gestalt* was borrowed to name.

The clinical implications follow with a directness that is unusual in the field. If a person is a field organised toward completion, then a symptom is an uncompleted gestalt, and the therapeutic task is to complete it. If a person is only knowable in the here and now of the relationship, then the most informative data available to the practitioner is the present moment. And if the person and the environment are mutually determining, then the practitioner's own presence is a constitutive part of the field, which makes the technique of confrontation — the deliberate disruption of the young person's habitual way of being — a legitimate and powerful intervention.

This chapter presents the theory in its intellectual context, works through its central constructs, describes the technique repertoire with its indications and cautions, and addresses the developmental and ethical questions.

## 1. Historical and Intellectual Context

### 1.1 The Berlin School and the laws of organisation

The experimental psychology of the Berlin School in the early twentieth century produced a series of findings that Gestalt therapy took as its foundation, and a student of the theory should know them, because the clinical constructs are direct translations of the perceptual findings.

**The law of Prägnanz.** Every stimulus pattern is perceived in the most simple, stable, and symmetrical form possible. The law states that the perceptual organisation tends toward the simplest, most stable, and most complete whole that the stimulus field permits.

**The law of good continuation.** Elements arranged in a line or curve are perceived as related, and the organisation tends to continue smoothly rather than to break.

**The law of closure.** The mind tends to fill in gaps and to perceive incomplete figures as complete.

**The law of figure and ground.** Every field is organised into a figure, which stands out and is attended to, and a ground, which recedes. The figure is the current focus of attention and of organisation, and the ground is what recedes from the foreground but remains in the field.

**The phi phenomenon and the apparent motion.** A sequence of still images perceived as motion; a demonstration that the whole is not the sum of its parts.

The clinical translation of the law of Prägnanz is the theory's central claim: there is a tendency in the organism toward an organised, coherent, and completed whole, and this tendency is the ground of growth. The clinical translation of figure and ground is the account of attention and of the way in which a person organises their experience: what is in the foreground, what has been pushed to the background, and what has been excluded from the field altogether.

### 1.2 The Würzburg school and the phenomenology of the act

The Würzburg school, working at the turn of the century on the phenomenology of thinking, established that thought involves an attitude of mind directed toward an object, that the object of thought determines the experience of thinking, and that there are properties of the act of thinking that are not reducible to its content. The contribution of the Würzburg school to Gestalt therapy is the notion that a person's experience is always experience *of* something, directed toward something, and that the direction of the attention is a clue to the state of the person.

### 1.3 Kurt Goldstein and the organism

Kurt Goldstein's work in the clinical neurology of the 1920s and 1930s, and his concept of the *Gestalt* as the form of the activity of an organism, supplied the biological grounding of the theory. Goldstein's studies of patients with brain injuries established that the organism, when it cannot complete an action, reorganises its whole activity around the incomplete action — and that the incomplete action persists as a drive that organises the total behaviour. This is the physiological counterpart of the clinical concept of the unfinished gestalt, and it is one of the reasons the theory has a certain explanatory power that its reputation for eccentricity sometimes obscures.

### 1.4 Perls: the biography and its consequences

Fritz Perls' path to Gestalt therapy was unusual and it shaped the theory in identifiable ways.

He trained in Vienna in the psychoanalysis of Freud and in the ego psychology of Anna Freud, and in 1930s Berlin he was a student at the Berlin Psychoanalytic Institute, where he was analysed by Kurt Goldstein and worked in Goldstein's neurological clinic. He moved to the United States in the 1930s, and there he encountered the behaviourism of the era as a challenge to the psychoanalytic method — and, in the "empty chair" experiment at the end of the 1940s, at a 1942 military hospital for what was then called "shell shock", he conducted group sessions in which he had one soldier sit in an empty chair and speak to the persona he most disliked.

The Second World War's contribution to the theory is direct and important, and it is the part of Gestalt therapy that most distinguishes it from the other humanistic theories. Perls' work at the military hospital produced the observation that many of the difficulties presented by the soldiers were not difficulties of feeling but difficulties of *contact* — that the person was cut off from the here and now, was living in a dissociated state, and that the re-establishment of contact with the immediate present moment was the treatment. The theory's emphasis on the here and now is therefore not a philosophical position; it is a clinical observation from a specific and very large sample of acutely disturbed people.

The New York school of the 1950s, in which Perls developed the theory with colleagues including Virginia Satir, Laura Perl, and Isadore From, produced the version of Gestalt therapy that is now most familiar, with the emphasis on the techniques and on confrontation. The Chicago school, and the Esalen and humanistic movements of the 1960s, produced a different version, with a much heavier emphasis on the "authentic" and "warm" dimensions and a corresponding softening of the confrontational techniques. The difference matters clinically, and a student should know which version they are reading.

## 2. The Core Propositions

### 2.1 The organism–environment field

The person is not an entity and the environment is not a separate entity; the person is a **field** in which the person and the environment are mutually determining. The word "field" is used in a specific sense: a bounded region of activity in which the parts are determined by their relations with each other and with the whole.

Three clinical consequences follow immediately.

**The practitioner's presence is constitutive.** The practitioner is a part of the young person's field. Whatever the practitioner does — asks, waits, challenges, stays silent — is a contribution to the field and is a possible stimulus for the young person's organisation. This is the ontological justification for the here-and-now principle and for the technique of confrontation.

**The symptom is a function of the field.** A symptom is not located in the person; it is the person's current mode of organising the field, and it arises out of a difficulty in the person's relation to the environment. A young person who is always the responsible one has found a way of organising the field in which they are not responsible for anything, and the symptom is the solution.

**The boundary is negotiable.** The boundary between the person and the environment is not fixed; it is drawn by the person, continuously, in the light of their needs. Symptoms are the drawing of a boundary that is too tight — an over-inclusion of what is not the person's own, an identification with the environment — or too loose. The account of identification and projection as disturbances of the boundary is one of the theory's most distinctive clinical concepts.

### 2.2 Here and now

The **here and now** is the total situation of the present moment in the field, including the practitioner. The here-and-now principle states that the most significant data available to the practitioner is the present moment of the encounter, and that the work is to bring into awareness what is occurring now.

The principle is more subtle than the popular version, and the subtlety is important. It does not mean that the past is unimportant — the past is present in the field, in the person's posture, in the tone of voice, in the way they arrange the chairs. It means that the past is available for examination only in the present, and that the practitioner's attention to the here and now is the most direct route to the material.

The clinical formulation of this is the **experiment**: the practitioner deliberately alters the field — in tone, in distance, in timing, in warmth, in challenge — in order to create, in the young person, an unfamiliar and disorganising experience, and then observes what the young person does with it. The therapeutic effect of the experiment lies in the difference between the young person's habitual mode of organising the field and their response to the disruption.

### 2.3 Awareness and the three zones

The state of **awareness** is a continuum with three zones, and the zones are used in Gestalt work to locate where a person is attending.

**The zone of awareness** contains what the person is aware of in the present moment, including their sensations, their feelings, their thoughts, their actions, and their interactions with the practitioner.

**The zone of pre-awareness** contains material that is accessible but not currently attended to — the background hum of the session, the physical tension in the shoulders, the half-formed thought, the thing the young person has been circling.

**The zone of unaware material** contains material that is not available to awareness at all, and which is held out of the field by the very process that manages the disruption. It is the material that the person's characteristic mode of organising the field excludes.

The clinical work is the expansion of the zone of awareness, and the technique for it is the **excitation**: the deliberate slight disruption of the field that brings the pre-aware material into focus. The precondition is that the young person has sufficient contact with the field to register the disruption at all, and the state of the person's contact is therefore the first thing to be assessed.

## 3. The Cycle of Experience and the Interrupted Gestalt

### 3.1 The cycle

The theory proposes a **cycle of experience** in which the organism moves through a sequence of phases: an approach toward a need or a contact situation; a heightened awareness at the point of contact; a full, satisfying, and complete contact; the integration of the new experience into the organism's structure; and a withdrawal to the organism's new equilibrium, in preparation for the next approach.

The theory's claim is that this cycle is completed many times a day in ordinary life, that the completion of each gestalt is what gives life its sense of movement and vitality, and that the organism's growth is the progressive capacity to complete more of these cycles and to complete them with greater differentiation.

### 3.2 The interrupted gestalt

The clinical account of difficulty follows. When the organism approaches a situation and the contact is *interrupted* — by a fear, by an environmental obstruction, by an internal prohibition — the organism cannot complete the gestalt, and the incomplete gestalt persists as an **unfinished business**, a drive that organises the person's behaviour around the incomplete contact.

The consequences of the incomplete gestalt are three.

**The figure persists.** The incomplete gestalt remains in the foreground of the person's attention, and the person continues to orient their behaviour towards completing it. A young person who has been unable to say goodbye to a parent who left will find the goodbye in every available relationship, and will organise their life around the attempt to complete it.

**The contact is interrupted by a substitute.** The incomplete energy is channelled into a substitute contact that cannot complete the gestalt either, and the behaviour that results is the symptom. A young person who was unable to assert and was therefore not met with an assertion has substituted an activity that accomplishes the same thing in a form that can be tolerated — and the substitution becomes the pattern.

**The energy is split.** The incomplete energy divides between the pursuit of the unattainable gestalt and the management of the rest of the field, and this is the source of the divided or split quality that is often characteristic of a young person's presentation. The therapy's aim is to allow the energy to be released and the cycle to be completed.

### 3.3 The therapeutic task

The therapeutic task is therefore: to help the young person become aware of the unfinished business, to bring it into the here and now, to allow the feeling to be felt to its completion, and to integrate the completed experience into the person's structure. The theory's language is that the practitioner helps the young person to "close" the gestalt, and the classical figures in the work are the Polar Bear and the Empty Chair: the completion of an unexpressed feeling toward a person who is not present.

The most important clinical point about this account is that it makes the symptom intelligible as an **attempted solution** rather than as a failure. The young person who is always the responsible one is not failing to be a child; they are completing a gestalt that could not be completed directly. The work is to find out what the attempted solution is for.

## 4. Contact and Its Interruptions

**Contact** is the boundary event in which the organism and the environment act upon one another. Good contact is characterised by three conditions: the **presentment**, in which the person is present in the moment and available to the interaction; the **focusing**, in which the person is aware of and engaged with a specific aspect of the field, the figure; and the **full contact**, in which the person is aware of the figure *and* of their own part in it.

Interruptions of contact take several forms, and a student should be able to name them in a case.

**Premature closure.** The person completes a gestalt before the contact is complete — a young person who agrees enthusiastically to everything, who ends the session before the work is done, who talks continuously to avoid the pause in which something might be felt. Premature closure is a form of the "should" of the character structure, and it is a very common presentation with young people who have been over-protected.

**Retroflection.** The person turns back on themselves a communication that should be directed outward. A young person who cannot say "I'm angry with you" and instead becomes depressed, apologetic, or anxious has retroflexed. Retroflexion is the most common form of contact interruption in clinical work with young people.

**Projection.** The person attributes to the environment what belongs to the self, and the attributed material is then perceived in the environment. A young person who insists that everyone in the household is out to get them may be experiencing their own exclusion.

**Confluence.** The person dissolves the boundary with the environment in order to avoid the anxiety of the boundary. The confluent young person is the one who merges with the practitioner, agrees with everything, and is in effect not present in the session at all. The theory holds that the movement from confluence toward differentiation is a central developmental task, and this claim has an obvious application to work with young people whose families have not permitted differentiation.

**Deflection.** The person turns away from the field — into abstraction, into questioning, into an activity, or out of the room. Deflection is the most benign of the interruptions and the one most easily mistaken for engagement.

## 5. The Technique Repertoire

### 5.1 The empty chair

The empty chair technique places an empty chair in the field, and the young person is invited to sit in it and speak to the chair as though it were occupied by the person to whom they have something unsaid. The Polar Bear variation involves the young person making the noise of the animal. The technique is derived directly from the theory: the empty chair represents the absent element of the field, and speaking to it completes the interrupted contact that could not be completed with the real person.

**Indications.** The technique is indicated where there is clearly unfinished business with an absent person, where the young person has difficulty expressing a feeling directly, where the feeling is one of the "unfinished business" type, and where the relationship to the absent person is not dangerous.

**Cautions, which are substantial.** The technique should not be used where the absent person is a source of danger, where the young person has a history of dissociation or of trauma that is not adequately prepared, or where the young person is likely to be overwhelmed. The technique can be experienced as a manoeuvre by the practitioner, particularly in its more dramatic forms, and a young person who is suspicious of adults will sometimes experience the invitation to speak to a chair as a trick. And the technique can put the young person in contact with very large feelings without the containing structure that they need, and the practitioner must be able to stay present with the flood and to help the young person complete it rather than to shut it down.

### 5.2 The hot seat

The hot seat places the young person in the position of the practitioner and the practitioner in the position of the young person, and the young person is invited to treat the practitioner as they have treated others. The technique is an experiment in the strict sense: it disrupts the young person's habitual position in the field and makes available the experience of being on the other side of the interaction.

**Indications and cautions.** It is indicated with young people whose habitual mode is highly compliant, passive, or deferential, because it gives them a position from which to act. It is contraindicated with young people who are fragile, who have been shamed, or who will experience the technique as a replication of a coercive encounter. The practitioner must be prepared to be attacked, and the preparation is not intellectual but attitudinal.

### 5.3 The gestalt prayer

The gestalt prayer is a personalisation exercise: the young person is asked to phrase a statement of a feeling in the first person singular, addressed to the person to whom it belongs — "I am angry with my father and I want him to know." The theory's account of the phenomenon is that the pronoun "I" is used in place of a more distal one because the person is identified with the feeling and with the projected material, and the shift to the first person is the de-identification that allows the feeling to be owned.

### 5.4 The dream work

In dream work, the young person reports a dream, and the practitioner helps them to construct a first-person narrative of the dream, working with each element in turn: what is the place like, what is the texture, what do the figures want, what do they do, what do you feel. The theory's account is that the dream is a gestalt in the process of completion, that every element is part of the young person's own psyche, and that the work of the session is the completion of the dream's unfinished action.

**Caution.** Dream work is contraindicated in young people with a history of dissociation or of nightmares, and with young people in acute crisis, and it is contraindicated in any situation in which the practitioner lacks the training to hold a young person's material without structuring it. It is also the technique most likely to be misused by an inexperienced practitioner, and the literature contains a number of documented cases of harm.

### 5.5 The confrontation of the here and now

The core intervention, and the one with the widest application, is the practitioner's direct statement of what is happening in the field. "Your shoulders have come up and your voice has got smaller since I asked about your father." "You have been answering my questions with questions." "You moved the chair away from me and I noticed you did it."

The intervention is powerful and it is the one that most often frightens students, because it feels like a lack of kindness. The theoretical defence is that the intervention is a gift: it gives the young person information about themselves that they could not otherwise obtain, and it does so in a form that the young person can verify. The clinical caution is that the intervention must be timed to the young person's contact with the field, and a practitioner who confronts a young person who is not in contact is offering an interpretation without the ground to receive it.

## 6. Gestalt Therapy with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The developmental fit

The developmental fit of Gestalt therapy with young people is good, and better than it is usually claimed to be. Three features support it.

**The emphasis on the body is developmentally appropriate.** The theory's attention to posture, to gesture, to the sound of the voice, to the placement of the body in the room, and to the felt sense is congruent with the developmental fact that a young person's emotional state is expressed in and organised through the body, and with the somatic and trauma-informed literatures developed in Part VI.

**The emphasis on the present moment matches the developmental condition of the child.** A young child's past is in the present, and a child's work is in the present, and the here-and-now principle is a close fit with a way of working in which the past is reached through what is happening now.

**The focus on the boundary and differentiation matches the developmental task.** The developmental literature on the second decade describes the adolescent's task as the achievement of a differentiated position within a continuing relationship with the family, and the Gestalt account of confluence and differentiation describes the same work in a way that is directly usable.

### 6.2 The developmental modifications

**With younger children**, the work is conducted through play and through the body, and the "unfinished business" is often located in a physical rather than a verbal form. The child who cannot complete a gesture, who cannot finish an action, who leaves every activity half-done, is showing an interrupted gestalt, and the intervention is to help the child complete the action, physically, in the session.

**With adolescents**, the modification concerns the pace and the holding. The adolescent's contact with the field is generally good, and the challenge is the regulation of the affect that the contact brings. The developmental judgement is that the adolescent can tolerate a great deal of feeling provided the practitioner can stay present and provided the session has a structure that signals its boundaries.

**With young people in crisis**, the modification is substantial. The Gestalt work is contraindicated in acute crisis, and the practitioner must establish safety, predictability, and some control before any confrontation. Chapter 38 develops this.

### 6.3 The specific risk with young people

The specific risk of the confrontational techniques with young people is that a young person who has been criticised, shamed, or hit will experience the confrontation as a repetition of the familiar, and will respond with the compliant or defensive behaviour they have developed. The practitioner must therefore be attentive to the young person's developmental history of being confronted, and must calibrate the intervention accordingly. A young person who has been shamed will need to know the difference between a challenge and a humiliation before the challenge can be useful, and that knowledge comes from the relationship, not from the technique.
