# Existential Therapy: Meaning, Isolation, and Freedom

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Trace the intellectual origins of existential therapy in European philosophy and literature, and explain why it entered counseling when it did.
- State the core existential propositions about the human condition and their significance for practice.
- Explain the four givens of human existence — mortality, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness — and the anxiety each produces.
- Describe the existential account of defence and avoidance, and explain its clinical implications.
- Outline Yalom's four elements of the therapeutic encounter and their developmental adaptation.
- Describe logotherapy's theory of the will to meaning and the techniques it generates.
- Apply existential thinking to work with children and adolescents, and identify the developmental limits of the approach.
- Evaluate the evidence for existential therapy and for the "meaning" constructs on which it rests.

## Chapter Overview

Existential therapy is the only theory in this book that begins with a philosophical account of the human condition rather than with a clinical problem. Its two predecessors, psychoanalysis and the crisis-oriented work of the mid-century European psychiatrists, both treated meaning as something a person might construct to defend against anxiety. Existential therapy inverts the sequence: anxiety is the condition, meaning is the response, and the therapeutic task is to help a person confront the anxiety of the human condition in a way that makes a genuine, chosen commitment possible.

The theory is of particular interest in a course on counseling children and adolescents, and for two reasons that pull in opposite directions. In its favour: adolescence is precisely the period in which the four existential givens become pressing, and the developmental literature on the adolescent identity task describes a young person engaged in exactly the existential questions the theory addresses. Against it: the developmental literature is equally clear that the capacity to hold an existential concern in a reflective, symbolising, non-paranoid way is a developmental achievement, and that an adolescent asked to confront the absurdity of existence at fourteen may be pushed into a psychological crisis that is developmental rather than pathological.

The chapter presents the theory in its intellectual context, develops the four givens and their associated anxieties, describes the defensive structures, presents Yalom's operational formulation of the encounter, covers logotherapy, and then spends considerable space on the developmental question — including the specific adaptations that make existential work with young people possible, and the specific ways in which it can go badly wrong.

## 1. Historical and Intellectual Context

### 1.1 The European roots

Existential therapy draws on three European traditions, and a student who knows only the clinical literature will underappreciate how much of its content is philosophical.

**The phenomenology of Edmund Husserl** supplied the method: the study of the structures of experience as they present themselves to consciousness, and the reduction of the natural attitude in which the world is taken for granted. Existential therapists inherit the practice of bracketing the assumption that the world is as it appears, and the practice of attending to the contents of experience. In the clinical setting, this becomes the practice of listening to the young person's experience as it is, rather than as it is presumed to be.

**The phenomenology of Martin Heidegger** supplied the core ontological claims: that the human being is a being-toward-death, whose distinctive mode of being is an involvement with a world in which the being at stake is the person's own; that the person's fundamental relation to the world is one of care rather than of detached observation; and that the "they" — the impersonal, public, conventional self — is the ordinary mode of being from which the individual self must be won. Heidegger's analysis of the they-self and of authenticity was taken directly into clinical theory by the existential psychiatrists, and it is the source of the clinical concept of the "defences" against anxiety.

**The phenomenology and existentialism of Martin Buber**, with its account of the I-Thou and I-It relations and its account of the self as constituted in relation, supplied the relational dimension that a purely ontological account of the individual lacks.

**The existentialism of Jean-Paul Sartre** supplied the analysis of freedom and responsibility, and with it the sharpest version of the existential conflict: the person is radically free and therefore radically responsible, and the principal means by which people avoid this responsibility is the adoption of a false project, a role, or a self-image that determines the person's course in advance. Sartre's account is the most coherent statement of the existential position and the one that most directly informs the clinical concept of bad faith.

**The work of Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, and the phenomenology of the body in Merleau-Ponty** supplied the account of anxiety as the appearance of the absurd, and of the body as the lived ground of existence.

### 1.2 The clinical introduction

The existential psychotherapies emerged in the Europe of the 1940s, in the context of the Second World War and its aftermath, where the encounter with death, displacement, and the collapse of received meanings was not a philosophical abstraction but a daily reality for both clinicians and patients. Three figures are central.

**Ludwig Binswanger**, whose work on the "being-in-the-world" and on the modes of existence in the *Being and Time* of Heidegger brought existential ontology into the psychiatric clinic, and whose case studies of melancholia, of the "spatiality of the insane," and of the significance of the body remain outstanding as clinical writing.

**Medard Boss**, whose work in daseinsanalysis applied the existential analysis to the practical problems of psychiatric care, and whose emphasis on the "unhiddenness" of being and on the ordinary everydayness of human existence was a corrective to the pathological focus of the psychiatric institution.

**Irvin Yalom**, who brought the European tradition into the American profession in the 1960s, in a form that was immediately usable, that was written in English with clinical precision, and that was demonstrated in a small number of very good books. Yalom's contribution was to make the theory teachable and to give it an operational form in the four elements of the therapeutic encounter.

**Viktor Frankl**, whose logotherapy developed from his work in the concentration camps and which, in its emphasis on the will to meaning, is the branch of existential therapy that has had the most influence outside the profession, including in the education and organisational literatures.

### 1.3 The timing

The reason existential therapy entered the profession when it did is instructive. The 1950s and 1960s saw a profession dominated by the behaviourist and psychodynamic wings, both of which were, from an existential point of view, precisely committed to avoiding the question. The behaviourist commitment to observable, measurable, and controllable behaviour located human difficulty in the realm of the objective, and thereby made the question of meaning invisible. The psychodynamic commitment located difficulty in the realm of the historical, and thereby made the question of the present, of the person's actual situation now, invisible. Existential therapy arrived as the voice of a question that both wings had suppressed, and its subsequent history in the profession has been a history of a theory that has been in continuous tension with the field's commitment to operationalisation.

That tension is the theme of this chapter, and the student should hold it throughout.

## 2. The Core Propositions

Existential therapy rests on four propositions, and a student should be able to state each of them accurately.

**Existence precedes essence.** The person is not a thing with a fixed nature who then acts; the person is a project who acts and thereby becomes something. In the clinical setting, this means that the person is not a set of fixed characteristics to be understood and then modified, but a being in process, whose identity is being constituted in what they do. This is a close cousin of the person-centred account of the self as a process, and the two traditions have been allied since the 1990s.

**The person is free and responsible.** The person is not determined by their history, their genes, their circumstances, or their unconscious; they are free to choose, and therefore responsible for their choices. The clinical significance is enormous and double-edged. It supports the practitioner's respect for the young person's agency and it forbids the attribution of pathology to a person as an explanation. And it is a proposition whose cultural specificity is considerable: the Western philosophical account of the radically free individual is not a universal account of human beings, and Chapter 48 returns to this.

**The person is alone in a fundamental sense.** Existential isolation is not social isolation, which is a circumstance that can be changed. It is the irreducible fact that no one else has access to the centre of one's own experience. It is the condition of the possibility of every relationship, since two people can only be together because they are separately there. Existential therapy's characteristic symptom is a relatedness to the world, in which the person is in profound and unsatisfying contact with the world and with other people.

**Existence is fundamentally meaningless, or at least radically ungrounded.** The world has no inherent purpose, and the person's existence is not guaranteed, is not permanent, and is not part of a larger plan. This does not mean that the world is absurd in a comic sense; it means that meaning is not given and must be made.

## 3. The Four Givens of Human Existence and Their Correspondent Anxieties

Yalom's formulation of the four givens is the standard clinical presentation, and the givens are not four separate topics but four existential facts whose implications are experienced as anxiety.

### 3.1 Mortality

**The existential fact.** The person is finite. Death is certain, the timing is not, and the possibility of dying at any moment is a fact of every moment of life.

**The anxiety.** The anxiety of death is the anxiety of non-being, and it appears in a number of forms: as dread, particularly in the young; as the sense of the meaninglessness of everything in the face of its ending; and, in what Yalom called the "nightmare of the possible," as the possibility that one might never have existed at all, which is a form of anxiety about the arbitrariness of existence rather than about its ending.

**The clinical forms.** Existential therapists observe that the anxiety of death appears in young people as preoccupation with death and dying, as a need to control and to leave a record, as a difficulty with the committing of one's life to anything, and as a preoccupation with the body's mortality. In the counselling room with adolescents, it may appear as an almost theatrical nihilism, as a provocative testing of the adult's reaction, as a preoccupation with the future that has the quality of a refusal to have one, or as a crisis in which the young person has to be talked out of a suicidal plan.

**The developmental qualification.** Suicide in adolescence is, on the developmental evidence, more often a communication and a demand for a change in the situation than an ending of life, and the developmental reading of suicidal behaviour — that the young person is attempting to solve an intolerable situation by the only means available to them — is a much more useful clinical frame than either the existential reading or the diagnostic reading. The existential contribution to it is a single and important insight: that the young person who is in suicidal crisis is frequently in the grip of a coherent system of meaning in which death is the only available exit, and that the work is to enter that system and find another exit, not to argue the young person out of it.

### 3.2 Freedom and responsibility

**The existential fact.** The person is not determined. Whatever the conditions of a life, the person retains the possibility of choosing differently, and is therefore responsible for the choice.

**The anxiety.** The anxiety of freedom is the burden of responsibility, and it appears in three principal forms.

*The anxiety of the possible*: the recognition that a decision has not been made and that not deciding is itself a decision.

*The anxiety of the fallibility of the world*: the recognition that the foundations on which one has relied may not hold, that the authorities on whom one has depended may not be reliable, and that the certainties of one's life may be revealed as contingent.

*The anxiety of the responsibility of the self*: the recognition that one cannot finally be anything other than what one chooses, and that there is no appeal against the sentence of one's own life.

**The clinical forms.** The anxiety of freedom is the most common of the four in clinical practice with young people, and it appears most characteristically as the panic of a life that has become too narrow — the young person who has been told what to do by parents, by a faith community, by a partner, by a school system, and who experiences the prospect of an open future as intolerable rather than as liberating. It also appears as the anxiety that accompanies a genuine developmental transition, such as leaving home, leaving a relationship, or leaving the last structured setting in which the young person's life has been organised.

**The developmental qualification.** The developmental literature on the adolescent identity task gives a specific and practical form to the existential account of freedom. The adolescent's developmental task is exactly the task of taking responsibility for a set of commitments, and the anxiety of freedom is the anxiety of the identity task. A young person who is panic-stricken about a decision about college, about a relationship, or about a course of study is not being irrational; they are confronting the developmental task in a form that their capacity to bear it does not yet support. The clinical task is developmental: to make the choice smaller, more concrete, more reversible, and more supported, and to accept that the developmental task will be completed across years rather than in a single session.

### 3.3 Isolation

**The existential fact.** No one else has access to one's own experience. There is an irreducible element of aloneness that no relationship removes, and conversely no relationship is possible without it.

**The anxiety.** The anxiety of isolation is the anxiety of the unbridgeable gap, and Yalom described it as the deepest of the four, the one that undercuts every other. It appears in three forms.

*Primary isolation*: the isolation of the infant from the world, before any object or person can be related to.

*Secondary isolation*: the isolation that arises in the course of a life, from the impossibility of fully sharing one's experience with anyone, from the failure of any single relationship to provide a complete answer to the need for relatedness.

*Existential isolation*: the isolation of the individual as such, which is not reducible to the other two and which is a condition of the possibility of the others.

**The clinical forms.** Yalom's observation that the existential therapist encounters the patient's isolation most sharply at the moment of termination — the point at which the possibility of real relatedness has been established and the impossibility of it is simultaneously established — is a clinical truth that every practitioner will recognise, and it is a strong argument for attending to termination early rather than late. The isolation anxiety also appears in young people as a pervasive sense of being fundamentally not-understood, which is frequently mistaken for a cognitive distortion and which is better understood as an accurate description of a condition: a young person with a neurodevelopmental difference, or one who is the only member of their family in a country, or one who is gay in a family that cannot accept it, may have a very accurate sense of being unreachable.

**The developmental qualification.** The developmental literature on the adolescent's renegotiation of attachment is relevant here. The adolescent's isolation is developmental, and the developmental work is the movement from the childhood state of complete inclusion in the family to the adult state of a selective, chosen, and therefore limited set of attachments. A young person who feels isolated is frequently in the middle of that transition, and the work is not to remove the isolation but to enable the young person's making of the choices that will constitute their eventual connection.

### 3.4 Meaninglessness

**The existential fact.** The universe presents no intrinsic purpose, and the person's existence is neither guaranteed nor part of a plan.

**The anxiety.** The anxiety of meaninglessness is the sense of the emptiness, the pointlessness, of a life in which nothing has been chosen because nothing needs to be. Yalom described this as the "hiding in the fog" experience, and the existential therapist's approach to it is neither nihilism nor reassurance but engagement.

**The clinical forms.** Meaninglessness is the most commonly presented existential difficulty in outpatient work with young people, and it appears as a pervasive emptiness, as a sense of going through motions, as the conviction that nothing matters and that this conviction is itself a sign that something is wrong with them, and as a general loss of the capacity to want anything.

**The developmental qualification.** Here the developmental literature is especially useful, because the adolescent's sense of meaninglessness is frequently a developmental stage and frequently a developmental opportunity. The developmental research on the possible self shows that the adolescent's motivation is organised around the future and around the gap between the present self and the possible self, and that a young person who has no future has no possible self, and a young person with no possible self has no motivation. The therapeutic work with a young person who is meaninglessly going through the motions is therefore frequently the work of finding a possible self — of enabling the young person to imagine a future in which something is at stake — and this is a developmental task with a developmental timetable.

## 4. The Existential Account of Defence

The existential account of defence is the most original clinical contribution of the tradition, and it is worth understanding precisely, because it differs from the psychodynamic account in a way that has direct practical consequences.

In the psychodynamic account, defence is a mechanism that protects the person from internal anxiety, and it operates within the personality. In the existential account, defence is a way of **avoiding the anxiety of the human condition** by adopting a **determining system of meaning** that reduces the person's freedom and thereby removes the burden of responsibility.

The structure is: the person encounters one of the four givens; the anxiety is experienced; and the person responds by organising a system of beliefs, values, and commitments that makes the anxiety unnecessary. The "determining system" is the name Yalom gave to the resulting structure, and the account of it is in some ways the most clinically usable part of the existential literature because it is a direct description of what a young person is doing in the counselling room.

A young person who has adopted a determining system of "nothing matters, I am beyond all that" has solved the problem of meaninglessness by deciding that meaning is not available, and has thereby become free of the responsibility of choosing. A young person who has decided that "I am the family's rock" has solved the problem of the anxiety of freedom by making the choice in advance and foreclosing it. A young person who has decided that "I must be completely self-contained" has solved the problem of isolation by pre-empting the need for anyone. A young person who has decided that "I am going to die anyway so it doesn't matter" has solved the problem of mortality by making the ending certain in advance and thereby removing the need to live.

The clinical implication is precise and practical: **the task is not to remove the determining system but to enter it, to understand what it is for, and to enable the young person to relinquish it.** And the therapeutic stance required is one of acceptance — the practitioner's understanding of the determining system as a solution rather than as a symptom, which is exactly the person-centred stance, and exactly the stance of the therapeutic relationship as described in Chapter 5.

## 5. The Therapeutic Stance and Yalom's Four Elements

Yalom's operational formulation of existential therapy is in four elements, and it is one of the most teachable formulations in the field.

**Presence.** The practitioner is fully available to the young person. Presence is the counterpart of Rogers' congruence, and it is defined in the same register: the practitioner is not offering a technique, and not withholding a reaction, but is genuinely there, with their own experience of the young person's situation available and honestly expressed.

**Empathy.** The practitioner understands the young person's experience from the inside, in a way that is experientially accurate rather than merely intellectually grasped. Yalom is careful to distinguish existential empathy from the empathic understanding of the person-centred tradition, on the grounds that the latter can be satisfied by a practitioner who understands the young person's experience as a set of problems to be solved, while the existential variety requires an understanding of the young person's lived situation, including the young person's relation to their own finitude, freedom, isolation, and possibility.

**Authenticity.** The practitioner is genuine, and the relationship is not a pretense. Authenticity is the strongest and most vulnerable of the four elements, and Yalom discusses at length the ways in which it can fail and the ways in which it can be used manipulatively by a young person — a point that a student should note, because authenticity without structure is a well-documented route to unhelpful therapy.

**Emotional involvement.** The practitioner is emotionally involved, and is willing to be affected by the young person. This is a relatively recent addition to the formulation and reflects Yalom's increasing emphasis on the "therapeutic factor of the encounter."

Yalom also specifies the **prime therapeutic factor**, which is the client's discovery that he or she has a choice, and that there are more choices than had been supposed. The whole of the work, in this account, is the expansion of the young person's sense of the possible.

### 5.1 The developmental adaptation of the four elements

The four elements require developmental modification, and the modifications are as follows.

With a younger child, the work is conducted through play and through the body, and the "givens" are approached indirectly. A seven-year-old's relation to mortality is not a philosophical position but an experience of a grandparent's death, and the therapeutic material is the grief and the questions.

With an adolescent, the four elements are in principle fully available, and the developmental problem is the balance between the adolescent's readiness for the work and the intensity of the anxiety it provokes. The clinical task is to work at the edge of the young person's capacity — the zone of proximal development of Section 3 of Chapter 9 — and to support them in tolerating the anxiety rather than in avoiding it.

## 6. Existential Therapy with Children and Adolescents

Three points of principle, and then a set of practical cautions.

### 6.1 The four givens are developmental facts, not philosophical propositions

A young person does not need to have read Heidegger in order to be confronted with mortality, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness. A twelve-year-old whose parent has a terminal illness is confronting mortality. A fourteen-year-old who has been told by their family exactly what they will study and do is confronting the question of freedom. A child who is the only one at school who speaks their family's language is confronting isolation. A seventeen-year-old with no future is confronting meaninglessness. The developmental work is to make these givens available as givens, rather than as symptoms.

### 6.2 The danger of premature confrontation

The most serious clinical error in existential work with young people is the premature confrontation of a givens. A fourteen-year-old taken by a practitioner to the question of mortality may experience the encounter as an abandonment, and the young person's response may be a crisis that is developmental in origin and iatrogenic in trigger. The safeguards are: the developmental judgement about readiness; the ordinary pacing of the work; the availability of the practitioner across the crisis; and, where the young person's suicidality is present, the full use of the risk assessment and management procedures that Chapter 54 sets out.

### 6.3 The danger of the philosophical posture

The second danger is that the practitioner adopts the philosophical posture as a performance. Existential language in a young person's hearing can easily read as pretension, and a young person who is told that their difficulty is an "existential dilemma" has been given a vocabulary that is more remote from their experience rather than less. The clinical test is simple and is the same test that Chapter 5 applied to the person-centred conditions: does the young person recognise the description as accurate? If the practitioner is working in language the young person does not use, the work is not in the young person's frame of reference.

### 6.4 The strength of the approach with young people

The strength of the existential approach with young people is that it takes seriously the young person's own questions. A fourteen-year-old who asks, in a session, whether it is pointless to try at anything, is asking an existential question and is usually being answered with reassurance or with a referral. An existential therapist sits with the question, and the sitting is the intervention. The developmental literature supports this: the adolescent's capacity for abstract, symbolic thought and for the consideration of the long-term future develops precisely in this period, and a young person who is met with respect for a genuine question is having an experience that developmental research shows to be rare in the lives of young people, and that promotes the development of exactly the capacities the question exercises.

## 7. Logotherapy and the Will to Meaning

**Viktor Frankl's logotherapy** is the branch of existential therapy with the most influence outside the profession, and it deserves separate treatment because its technique is more specified.

Frankl's theory holds that the primary human motivation is the **will to meaning** — the striving to find and to live a meaningful life — and that this is a more basic motivation than the drives for pleasure (Freud) or for power (Adler). Meaning, in Frankl's account, is discovered through the world and through oneself, and is not manufactured; it is a matter of the person's relation to the reality of the situation.

Frankl distinguished three paths to meaning: by **creating or working** something; by **experiencing** something, particularly love and the natural world; and by **choosing one's attitude** to a situation that cannot be changed. The third path is the one that is most relevant to work with young people in difficult circumstances, and it is the one most often misunderstood as a counsel to endurance.

The logotherapy techniques include **paradoxical intention**, in which the practitioner asks the young person to wish for the thing they fear, on the grounds that the wish transforms the relationship to the fear from a passive to an active one; **existential vacuum**, which names the specific phenomenon of a young person who has lost their sense of the reason for anything and who frequently presents it as a depression; and the **logotherapeutic technique of reflection** in which the practitioner draws attention to the young person's remaining capacities and possibilities.

**Paradoxical intention with young people requires considerable care.** With a young person who is anxious about a school presentation, asking them to hope for the worst is a technique that requires a relational context in which it is experienced as play rather than as cruelty, and with a young person who is depressed, asking them to wish for the thing they most dread can be experienced as a systematic instruction in self-harm. The technique is not a technique to be applied by rule.
