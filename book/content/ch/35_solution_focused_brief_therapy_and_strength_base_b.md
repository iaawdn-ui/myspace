
## 5. Solution-Focused Work with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The fit

The solution-focused approach is used more widely with young people than with any other population, and the reasons are worth understanding.

**It does not require insight.** A young person who cannot or will not articulate their difficulties can still answer the miracle question, the exception question, and the coping question, and the approach is therefore unusually accessible to a young person who is defensive, or inarticulate, or frightened, or who has been told by adults that their problem is bigger than they can manage.

**It is short.** A young person who has waited six weeks for an appointment and who suspects that the appointment will be another demand will engage with a short, concrete, future-oriented session in a way they will not engage with an open-ended exploration.

**It does not require the young person to be the problem.** The problem is separated from the person, and the young person is invited to be the expert on their own life, and this is a considerable relief to a young person who has been the identified patient in every previous attempt.

**It is compatible with the behavioural and the systemic work.** The exception question is, in the terms of Chapter 23, a question about the contingencies that were in operation; the small step is a graded task; the goal is a specification of a target behaviour with a time limit. The approaches fit together well.

**It produces something the young person can take away.** A written goal, a small step, a scale, and a telephone number are all things a young person can use between sessions, and a young person who is in trouble needs something to hold.

### 5.2 The specific modifications

**Use the child's language.** The approach's core discipline of using the client's own words is more important with young people than with adults, because the young person's words are also the material the young person will use outside the session. If a young person says the school is "a prison", the goal should be in the language of prisons, or at least should use the young person's words for the desired state.

**Ask the miracle question in the concrete.** "If the thing with school got sorted out, what would your Tuesday look like?" produces more usable detail than the abstract form, and it is easier for a young person to answer.

**Scale the small things.** The scale works particularly well with young people, and the "one point higher" question is the workhorse: "Where are you on a scale of zero to ten for how much you want to go to school? What would one point higher look like?"

**Find the exception in the ordinary.** The exceptions with young people are frequently in the ordinary week: the good lesson, the day the tutor was away, the time the argument did not happen, the evening that was fine. The therapist's job is to get the granularity right.

**Use the coping question to establish the baseline of competence.** "How have you managed to keep going?" is often the first question that produces a competent description of the young person, and it changes the frame of the session.

**Set the goal with the young person and make it a behaviour.** A goal of "feeling better" cannot be checked. A goal of "texting my mum on Monday and Wednesday" can.

**Use the family.** The approach's techniques work with the whole family and they work particularly well with parents, because the coping question to a parent — "how have you managed to keep this together?" — is often the first time the parent has been asked anything other than what is wrong.

### 5.3 Solution-focused work with a family in crisis

A family in crisis is a good fit for the approach and a poor fit if it is applied naively. The specific cautions are as follows.

**Do not go too fast.** The "one small step" rule is right for a family in a stable situation and wrong for a family in which a young person is at risk. A family in crisis needs enough time in the session to be held, and the short session is a decision to be made deliberately rather than by default.

**Do not scale the safety.** Scaling the young person's suicidality, or the family's functioning, in the first session of a crisis is a misuse of the technique. The scale is for a dimension that the client can hold, and a scale on a crisis dimension produces a number that is meaningless and is experienced as a reduction of the crisis to a number.

**Use the exception to find what was there before the crisis.** The "what was different" question is the most useful question in a crisis, because the answer identifies what was working and has stopped working, and the work is often the restoration of something that has been lost rather than the creation of something new.

**The goal in a crisis should be a safety goal, and it should be co-constructed with the referrer.** The goal of not going to school is not a useful goal in a crisis; the goal of going to the school nurse's office at eleven, with an adult waiting, is.

### 5.4 The problem of the positive

The approach's language is relentlessly positive, and this is its most significant clinical liability. The phrase **the tyranny of the positive** was coined to describe the experience of a client who is invited to focus on their strengths at the moment when they most need to describe their pain, and who experiences the invitation as a demand that they perform wellness.

Three safeguards are available.

**Ask about the pain explicitly and in the same tone.** "I want to hear about the hard stuff too, and about the times when it is worse" is a legitimate and necessary question, and it is the safeguard.

**Do not imply that a difficulty is the client's responsibility.** The approach's language of "the problem is separate from the person" must be used carefully, because a young person who has been told that the problem is theirs has had the framing imposed on them.

**Watch for the young person who is polite.** A young person who has learned to give the answer the adult wants will produce miracle answers and coping answers that are not true, and the practitioner's diagnostic task is to notice the discrepancy between the answer and the young person's presentation.

### 5.5 The problem of hope

The approach assumes that a future is available to be worked on, and the assumption fails in a number of circumstances that are common in the adolescent population.

**A young person who has no reason to want a future** — a young person who is suicidal, or who is in a family in which the future is genuinely uncertain — will produce answers to the miracle question that are either empty or despairing, and the practitioner must not treat the despairing answer as a failed miracle question.

**A young person for whom the goal has been confiscated.** A young person in a care placement whose every decision is made for them cannot set a goal, and the practitioner should work with what they can control, and should be honest with the system about the constraint.

**A young person whose future is genuinely not available** — where there is no realistic prospect of a stable home, or of safety, or of health — needs work that is not solution-focused, and the approach's optimism will be experienced as indifference if it is not matched by an honest assessment of the circumstances.

## 6. Case Illustration: The Young Woman Who Had Two Good Tuesdays

**Presentation.** Amira is seventeen and is referred by her school for "low mood and withdrawal". The referral describes a young woman who has stopped attending her college, who has stopped answering messages, who is "always in her room", and who has been "like this since October".

**The first session.** The practitioner does not begin with the problem. The practitioner begins with the coping question: "Before we talk about how things have been, I want to know how you've been managing. You've been through a lot this year — what's kept you going?"

Amira is quiet for a moment and then says, quite steadily: "Music. And my gran. And Tuesday." The practitioner asks what is special about Tuesday and Amira says that on Tuesdays she has a music lesson in town, and that she goes with a friend, and that she comes back and the evening is fine. The practitioner asks what happens on a Tuesday that does not happen on a Wednesday and Amira says: "Nothing happens. I just don't want to."

The practitioner asks a scaling question: "On a scale of zero to ten, where's your Thursday this week, and where's your Tuesday?" Amira says Thursday is a two and Tuesday is a seven. The practitioner asks what a three on Thursday would look like, and Amira says: "Getting out of bed before my dad shouts. And eating something that isn't in my room." The practitioner asks what a three would look like on the actual Thursday, and Amira says: "Get to college. Even for one lesson. And tell the tutor I'm coming."

**The exceptions.** The practitioner spends most of the session on the exceptions, and the exceptions are specific. On the Tuesday, Amira gets up at eight because she wants to catch the bus. On the Tuesday, she has something to wear that she has chosen. On the Tuesday, her phone is charged because she needs it for her friend. On the Tuesday, her dad is working late, and her gran is there. The practitioner asks what else is different, and Amira says, after a pause: "I don't have to talk to anyone. In the tutor's office, I'm in a corridor and I don't have to speak."

**The formulation.** The formulation is not a depression formulation. The formulation is: Amira's week is structured by two things — a music lesson and the presence of her grandmother — and everything else is structureless, and the structureless days are the difficult ones. The withdrawal from the college is not a mood; it is the absence of the structure and the presence of the requirement to speak to people she does not know. Her mother, who is in the referral as a problem, is not in the formulation at all, and the practitioner notes that she has not yet heard from the mother, and she arranges to.

**The intervention.** Four elements.

*Element one: the goal.* Amira's goal, which she states herself, is: "By Friday, I go to the tutor's office and say I'm coming back, and I don't have to stay." The goal is specific, it is achievable, it is bounded in time, and it is a behaviour. The practitioner asks what she will do in the office and Amira says: "Stand at the door and say it. Then leave."

*Element two: the small steps.* The steps are derived from the exception, and they are the Tuesday factors applied to the difficult days. The first step is that Amira will get up and dress before her dad shouts, and the second is that she will charge her phone, and the third is that she will have something to wear that she has chosen. The purpose of the steps is stated explicitly: "You get dressed and charge your phone on a Tuesday because those are the things that make a Tuesday a Tuesday, and I'm asking you to try them on a Thursday, and if it works even a bit, that is evidence that a Thursday can be more like a Tuesday."

*Element one, revised: the grandmother.* The practitioner asks whether the grandmother could be involved and Amira says no, and the reason is that the grandmother is the person who looks after her and the family would be worried. The practitioner accepts this and does not pursue it, and the acceptance is itself an intervention, because it is the first time in the session that an adult has not pushed past a "no".

*Element three: the mother.* The practitioner sees the mother separately in the following week, and the mother's account is that Amira is "in her room all day, she's rude, she doesn't want to talk to me, she snapped at me on Sunday and I cried in the bathroom." The practitioner asks the mother the coping question — "How have you been managing?" — and the mother says, after a pause: "I don't know. I've just been going to work." The practitioner asks the mother to identify the last time she and Amira had a conversation that was not about a problem, and the mother cannot think of one. The practitioner asks the mother what Amira was like before October, and the mother says: "She was funny. She was really funny." And then she says: "I've been bringing her food up to the room. I thought that was helping."

The practitioner names the mechanism carefully: "I don't think you're doing anything wrong. But the food is the only contact you're having with her, and it's happening at her door, which means she doesn't have to come to you. And it's probably the only time you two talk. And if that's the only contact, then everything you want to talk to her about is going to go through that door, and she's going to close it."

The specific change: the food goes to the kitchen, and the mother and the daughter eat together once a week, on a fixed day, and the mother is asked to make one thing that is not on the ordinary rotation, and the conversation is not about the problem.

*Element four: the follow-up.* The session is booked for the following week, and the practitioner asks Amira to rate the session on the scale, and Amira says: "Seven." The practitioner asks what a seven-and-a-half would look like, and Amira says: "Texting my cousin. She keeps texting and I keep not replying and it's starting to be weird." The practitioner takes this as the small step for the week, and it is the correct one, because it is the smallest social behaviour in the account.

**The outcome.** At six weeks, Amira is attending one lesson a week, has returned to the music lesson, and has replied to her cousin. Her mother reports that they eat together once a week and that "it's not brilliant but it's something". The college has not reported a full-time return, and the practitioner says clearly that this is not the outcome the referral wanted and is nevertheless the outcome that is durable.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the approach in full, and it also demonstrates its characteristic mechanism: the exception was located with precision, and the intervention was the transfer of the factors present in the exception to the days on which the problem occurred. The mother's daily delivery of food to the door was the sequence in the family system, and it was interrupted by the same logic, and the intervention with the mother was a single concrete change. And the therapist's discipline about the client's own words was what produced the formulation: the whole account was built on "Tuesday" and on what Tuesday contained, and no diagnostic formulation of a depressive disorder would have generated that intervention.

## 7. Evidence and Research Support

### 7.1 The status of the evidence

The solution-focused approach has a good evidence base, and it is stronger than its clinical reputation in some quarters would suggest.

**The brief therapy outcome literature is good.** The studies of brief therapy in general, in which the solution-focused approach is the most fully operationalised, show substantial effects on a range of outcomes and good retention.

**The research on the specific question types is the distinctive contribution.** The studies that recorded the therapists' questions and correlated them with outcomes found significant effects for the future-oriented and the exception questions, and the effects held after the therapeutic alliance was controlled for.

**The meta-analyses of the solution-focused approach report moderate-to-large effects** on depression, on the broad measures of psychological distress, and on the goal-attainment measures, and the effects are comparable with those of longer treatments in several populations.

**The evidence with adolescents is reasonable and growing**, and the approach is used extensively in school settings, and the school-based studies report good effects on the young people's measured outcomes and on the measures of school engagement.

**The evidence in specific high-risk populations is good.** The approach has been successfully applied in homelessness services, in the justice system, in chronic pain services, and in services for families with a young person at risk of removal from home, and the results in the homelessness literature in particular are among the most encouraging for a very brief intervention.

### 7.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The studies are small and heterogeneous**, and the model has been applied with considerable variation, and the fidelity measures are rarely reported.

**The measures are largely symptom and goal-attainment measures**, and the long-term outcomes are less well documented.

**The samples skew towards the mild and the voluntary**, and the young people in crisis are under-represented.

**The "positive outcome" findings are vulnerable to the response set of the participants.** A client who wishes to please a friendly practitioner who has asked them to describe a miracle will produce a miracle.

**The comparison with longer treatments is often unfair in either direction.** The studies frequently compare a brief solution-focused intervention with a longer treatment of a different theory, which confounds the brevity with the model.

## 8. Strengths and Limitations of the Solution-Focused Approach

### 8.1 Strengths

**It is accessible.** It requires no insight, no theory, and no trust in the therapist's expertise, and it works with young people who are resistant, inarticulate, or frightened.

**It is short and it retains people.** The approach has among the best retention rates in the psychotherapy literature, because people who are not helped do not come back.

**It is portable.** The five skills can be taught in an afternoon and used in any setting, and they are now used in services with no connection to the school.

**It is compatible with other modalities.** The exception question is a functional analysis in everyday language; the goal is a specification of a target behaviour; the small step is a graded task.

**It changes what the practitioner sees.** The question discipline changes the information the system produces, and a practitioner who has learned it becomes substantially better at seeing what is working.

**It is compatible with a working alliance.** The approach's posture of curiosity and respect is compatible with the person-centred conditions of Chapter 10, and the two are frequently combined.

### 8.2 Limitations

**It can trivialise.** The tyranny of the positive is a real and predictable harm, and a young person in pain can experience the approach as a demand that they perform recovery.

**It is not appropriate for every problem.** Where the problem is a danger, a symptom of a medical condition, or a consequence of a systemic failure, the focus on exceptions and goals is beside the point.

**It has no theory of aetiology, and this is a strength and a weakness.** It is a strength because it does not require a diagnostic formulation, and a weakness because the practitioner has no framework for understanding what they are seeing and no way of deciding what to do when the exceptions do not reveal a usable pattern.

**It can produce a compliant but unconfronted young person.** A young person who sets a modest goal, achieves it, and is congratulated has been given a success that is real and that may not change the underlying situation.

**The evidence does not address the severe cases**, and a service that adopts the approach wholesale will struggle with the young people it most needs to serve.

## 9. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 Culture and the language of the approach

The solution-focused approach's most powerful technique — using the client's own words — is also its most culturally sensitive, because the words are the culture.

**The miracle question assumes a future in which the problem is absent, and the future is culturally structured.** In a context in which the young person's future is materially uncertain, the question may be experienced as an accusation, and the answer may be despairing rather than evasive.

**"Strengths" are culturally specified.** The approach's assumption that the client's own language and the client's own people are the source of the strength is sound; the assumption that what the client values is what the client should work towards is not available, because the client's values are partly the values of a community that the practitioner may not understand, and in some contexts the young person's own stated goal will be a goal the practitioner regards as harmful.

**The exception search can be culturally loaded.** A young person in a family in which openness about the difficulty is itself dangerous may produce exceptions that are false, and the practitioner must be alert to the possibility that the exception is a performance.

**The language of the approach is individualistic**, and the "the problem is separate from the person" formulation is a claim about the person and the problem that does not transfer to every cultural frame, and in some settings the person and the problem are not separable in the way the formulation assumes.

### 9.2 Ethical considerations

**The obligation to acknowledge the pain.** A practitioner using this approach has an obligation to ask about the difficulty and to sit with the answer, and the approach's own literature now contains this caution.

**The obligation not to substitute positivity for safety.** A goal-setting process must not displace a safeguarding or a risk assessment.

**Informed consent to the approach itself.** A client should know what the approach does, and that it is brief, and that the practitioner is not going to explore the past in depth, and this should be said at the beginning rather than discovered at the end.

**The avoidance of coercion through goal-setting.** A goal that the client sets under the pressure of a therapist who is warm and encouraging and who has made the session pleasant is not a free choice, and the practitioner should notice the difference between a goal the client wanted and a goal the client produced to please.

**The equity issue.** The approach is culturally and economically attractive precisely because it is efficient, and a service under pressure may adopt it because it is cheap, and the risk is that it is then applied to the families who complain least. The approach works best with the families who can engage, and the families who cannot are the ones the service is under pressure to serve.

**The termination question.** A brief approach terminates quickly, and a young person who has experienced a short, warm, effective piece of work and then a termination may learn that relationships are brief, and the termination should be handled with the same care as any other.

## 10. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Solution-focused brief therapy** — A short-term, future-oriented, and non-exploratory therapy developed from the study of brief therapy in Milwaukee, in which the client's exceptions and resources are the primary clinical material.
- **The tyranny of the positive** — The experience of a client who is invited to focus on their strengths at the moment when they need to describe their pain, and who experiences the invitation as a demand to perform wellness.
- **Miracle question** — "Suppose that for some reason the problem got solved, what would be different?" The signature technique of the approach, in which the counterfactual produces a description of the consequences of the solution.
- **Exception question** — A question asking the client to describe a time when the problem did not occur, occurred less, or was handled better, and what was different about it.
- **Past question** — A question asking about a time when the problem was better or worse, used to locate the trend and to establish hope.
- **Coping question** — A question asking what the client has been doing to manage the problem, which identifies a skill that is already present and partly generalised.
- **The goal** — A specific, achievable, and time-bounded target set by the client, and the last of the core skills.
- **Scaling question** — A question asking the client to rate a dimension on a scale, followed by the "one point higher" and "one point lower" questions.
- **The one-point-higher question** — The question that identifies the immediate next step, grounded in the client's own frame of reference for the dimension.
- **The one-point-lower question** — The question that surfaces the client's fear and the risk, and the question to ask when a client becomes complacent.
- **Session scaling** — The practice of rating the usefulness of the session itself on a scale, and the most direct way for a beginner to learn the model.
- **Solution** — The behaviour or state of affairs that the client wants more of, defined by the client in the client's own words.
- **Exception** — A time when the problem did not occur, occurred less, or was managed differently, and the search for the factors present in it.
- **The distinction between the solution and the exception** — The solution is the target and the exception is the evidence, and a client asked for the solution before the exceptions have been explored will usually offer an aspiration.
- **Solution language** — The client's own words for the desired state, which the practitioner uses verbatim and which is the primary material of the work.
- **The change has already happened** — The assumption that the client's life contains times when the problem was better, smaller, absent, or different, and that these times are the raw material of the work.
- **The small can be a catalyst** — The principle that an achievable and verifiable small step produces a momentum disproportionate to its size.
- **Problem is separate from the person** — The reframe the approach offers to every client, and one that must be used with care.
- **The questions determine the data** — The principle that the questions a practitioner asks determine what the system produces, and that a system asked only about problems will produce only problems.
- **The behavioural common factors** — The therapeutic alliance, the social contract, the expectancy of improvement, the client tasks, and the therapeutic feedback, and the approach's compatibility with the common factors model.
- **Cooperation** — The orientation in which the practitioner brings the question and the client brings the answer, in place of an expertise in which the practitioner brings the answer.
- **The small step** — The specific, doable action that the client takes between sessions, derived from the exception.
- **Question discipline** — The consistent use of the model's core questions, and the skill that separates a solution-focused practitioner from a generalist who asks the odd future-oriented question.
- **The training in listening** — The formal training in identifying the client's own solution language as it appears in the conversation.
- **Retain the client** — The principle that a client who is not helped will not return, and the approach's unusually good retention figures.
:::

## 11. Chapter Summary

The solution-focused school began with the observation that therapists were working only on the problem and had never asked what was working, and it developed into one of the most widely used approaches in the world. The chapter traced the Milwaukee brief therapy project, in which the therapists' own recorded behaviour was analysed and the future-oriented and exception questions were found to predict outcome, and it placed the school within the wider strength-based movement and its shared technical proposition that the questions asked determine the information the system produces.

The core assumptions were stated with their clinical consequences, and the distinction between the solution and the exception was drawn: the solution is the target, the exception is the evidence, and a client asked for the solution before the exceptions have been explored will offer an aspiration.

The five core skills were described in detail, with the therapist's discipline for each: the miracle question, in which the counterfactual produces the description of the consequences of the solution and the practitioner must resist the urge to make the question realistic; the exception questions, which locate the solution and disrupt the problem's hold and must be recent and must be treated as informative rather than accidental; the past question, which locates the trend; the coping question, which identifies the skill; and the goal, with its three requirements of specificity, achievability, and boundedness in time, and the requirement that the client sets it. The scaling technique was described with the three questions that constitute it and with the one-point-higher and one-point-lower functions, and session scaling was identified as the most direct way for a beginner to learn the model.

The developmental and clinical fit with young people was developed through the five reasons the approach is widely used with this population, the six specific modifications for young people, the cautions for work with a family in crisis, and the two serious liabilities: the tyranny of the positive and the assumption of an available future. The three safeguards against the first and the circumstances in which the second fails were given.

The case of Amira demonstrated the approach's characteristic mechanism. The exception was located with precision — Tuesday, and everything Tuesday contained — and the intervention was the transfer of the factors present in the exception to the days on which the problem occurred, and the mother's daily delivery of food to the door was identified as the family sequence and interrupted by the same logic, and the whole formulation had been built out of the client's own words and would not have been produced by a diagnostic formulation of depression.

The evidence was summarised with its limits, and the strengths and limitations were set out, with the two most important limitations identified: that the approach can trivialise, and that it has no theory of aetiology, which is both its strength and its weakness.

The cultural analysis addressed the cultural structure of the future assumed by the miracle question, the cultural specificity of strengths, the possibility that the exception search can be culturally loaded, and the individualistic framing of the approach. The ethical section addressed the obligation to acknowledge the pain, the obligation not to substitute positivity for safety, informed consent to the approach, the avoidance of coercion through goal-setting, the equity issue that a service under pressure may adopt an efficient approach and apply it to the families who complain least, and the termination question.

## 13. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 35.1 — The Five Questions (60 minutes, individual)

Write out and answer, in writing, all five of the core questions for a problem of your own, and then write the answers as a client might give them. Then write the miracle question as a therapist tends to ask it by mistake, and compare. The exercise is the fastest way to learn the model's discipline, and the comparison of the two forms of the miracle question makes the difference between the real and the implied question visible.

### Activity 35.2 — The Exception Search (90 minutes, field-based)

Take a young person from placement and find three exceptions — times when the difficulty was smaller, or absent, or handled better. For each, write the "what was different" analysis: the specific factors, the people, the time of day, the state of the other part of the life. Then write the small step for each. Most students find that the exceptions are ordinary and that the factors are mundane — a charged phone, a chosen outfit, a friend present, a door open — and the discovery that the treatment is the transfer of the mundane is the point.

### Activity 35.3 — Scaling the Session (three sessions, in pairs)

In pairs, run a fifteen-minute conversation in which the practitioner asks only the five core questions, and at the end scales the session and asks the "one point higher" question. Then reverse roles, with the second student now acting as the practitioner, who should ask fewer questions and listen more. The second round is the instructive one, because it demonstrates the difference between a practitioner who is asking questions and a practitioner who is listening for the answer.

### Activity 35.4 — Auditing a Miracle Question (60 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, take a real referral and write the miracle question as it would be asked in that case, in the exact words the client would use, followed by the scaling question and the exception question. Then have each group member read the set aloud and identify the question that is most likely to produce a useful answer, and the one that is most likely to produce a polite one. Then write the version of the miracle question that would work with a young person who has been in trouble with adults before. The final step is the practical lesson of the chapter.
:::

## 14. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model.** Read de Shazer (1985) and then O'Connell (2003), which is the clearest clinical handbook and the most usable with young people.

**For the research.** Read the original Milwaukee project papers and the later research on the question types, and read Shonk and Greasley (2015) for the contemporary account. The question-type research is the most interesting evidence in the book, because it is the evidence for a specific technique rather than for a treatment package.

**For the practice.** Read O'Connor and McGee (2016), which is written for parents and educators and is exceptionally clear about the mechanics. Then practise the five questions, because the model is a skill and not a set of propositions.

**For the evidence.** Read the meta-analyses of solution-focused therapy and of brief therapy, and the school-based studies. Read the criticisms alongside them.

**For the critique.** Read the literature on the tyranny of the positive, and read the critical accounts of brief therapy in complex cases. The critique is important because the approach's virtues and its failures are the same feature seen from two sides.
