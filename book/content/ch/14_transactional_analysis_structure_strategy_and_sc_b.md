## 7. Script Systems and the Case Formulation

### 7.1 Mapping a case

The three-by-three script matrix is a practical instrument for structuring a case discussion, and the exercise of filling it in with a group of students is one of the most instructive in the course, because the disagreements are productive and are usually about the *payoff* rather than about the facts.

The three columns are: what the young person believes (about the self, about others, about the world), what the young person does (the behavioural strategies, the manipulations, the avoidances), and what the young person feels (the emotional core of the script). The three rows are: the conscious level, the preconscious level, and the unconscious level.

A worked example is worth giving, because the instrument is only useful if the student can see it applied. Take a fifteen-year-old boy, Jonty, who is described by his school as "lazy, oppositional, and always lying."

**Unconscious level, belief:** "I am not the sort of person who is any good at anything, and there is no point in finding out." **Unconscious level, behaviour:** disengages from every academic task, and has learned to be charming in the first ten minutes of a session and to say nothing after that. **Unconscious level, feeling:** shame, and a terror of being seen to be stupid.

**Preconscious level, belief:** "If I don't try, I can't fail, and then it isn't my fault." **Preconscious level, behaviour:** the disengagement, the avoidance of the tests, the preference for the work he can do well. **Preconscious level, feeling:** relief, which is the payoff.

**Conscious level, belief:** "School is pointless and my teachers have it in for me." **Conscious level, behaviour:** the refusal, the lateness, the rudeness. **Conscious level, feeling:** boredom, resentment, and a conviction that he is being treated unfairly.

**The payoff.** What does the script buy? It buys the avoidance of the humiliation that would accompany a genuine attempt. The script system is coherent: the unconscious belief generates the unconscious behaviour, which generates the relief, which reinforces the belief, and the conscious layer protects the whole system with a grievance. A practitioner who addresses the conscious layer — arguing about the teachers' fairness — is arguing with the protection, and the argument will not succeed, because the young person is not lying about the teachers; the teachers are, in fact, not always fair.

**The developmental reading.** Jonty is fifteen, and the developmental question is what this pattern was in the earlier years. The interview with his mother reveals that he was a keen reader until the age of nine, when he was moved up two years and spent a year being the youngest in a class of much older children. The script was written then.

**The redecision.** In a redecisional approach, the work would be: the old decision, "I decided that trying is more dangerous than not trying, because when I try and fail, everyone sees"; the origins, in the year he was moved up; the payoff, which is the protection from the humiliation and the relief of the avoidance; the script belief about the self, "I am the one who does not put himself in a position to be found out"; and the redecision, which the young person must make himself and which the practitioner may only make possible.

**The limitation of the redecision in this case.** A redecision made in the consulting room will not survive the first test, because the script belief is held in the body and in the habit, and because the payoffs are immediate and the gains delayed. The redecision is best understood as the beginning of the work rather than the whole of it, and the integrative school would add the work on the relationship and the work with the mother, who has her own script — a "my son has always been lazy" script which is a Parent position and which the practitioner will need to address in the family work, because Jonty's script and his mother's are, in the developmental account, two halves of one system.

## 8. The Integrative Development and the Contemporary Position

The contemporary state of transactional analysis is best described by the integrative school, and the four features of it are worth describing.

**A relational rather than a structural account of the personality.** The integrative school has largely abandoned the idea of a person with three discrete compartments and has adopted a relational account in which the person is constituted in relationship and in which the "ego states" are best understood as *positions* or *modes* that a person takes up in interaction, and in which the significant others are part of the system. This is a considerable revision of Berne's original model and it is a much better fit with the contemporary relational and systemic literatures.

**A developmental and attachment-informed account of script.** The integrative school has drawn on the attachment literature and on the developmental literature to understand how the script is formed, and this has substantially improved the theory's work with young people, because it locates the script in the early relationship and in the actual developmental history rather than in a hypothetical past.

**A here-and-now account of the therapeutic relationship.** Erskine's work on contact-in-relationship, in which the therapist attends to the felt sense of the relationship and to the young person's momentary experience of the therapist, has given the theory a here-and-now dimension that the classical school lacked, and that has made it usable in work with young people who are hypervigilant for adult inconsistency.

**A clearer ethics.** The integrative school's attention to the power differential and to the practitioner's own states has partly addressed the danger identified in Section 5.2.

## 9. Evidence and Research Support

**The status of the scientific controversy.** Two distinct issues should be separated, because they are frequently conflated and the conflation has damaged the theory's reputation unfairly.

The first is the general scientific question: whether the ego-state model corresponds to measurable psychological structures. The classical answer was that the states are phenomenologically distinguishable and can be reliably identified from speech content, and the counter-argument has been that the model lacks a validated psychometric instrument. The contemporary position, developed by the integrative school, is that the states should be understood as *positions* in a relational system rather than as structures of the mind, and that the hypotheses of interest are behavioural and relational rather than psychometric.

The second is a specific and publicly conducted dispute concerning the psychometric properties of a particular personality instrument and the process by which a particular professional body granted a theory recognition. The dispute was conducted in the journals of the professional association, and a student who wishes to understand it should read the primary sources on both sides. The general professional significance of the dispute is that it raised, and continues to raise, the question of what the profession's bodies ought to require before they confer the status of a recognised theory of psychotherapy.

**The outcome evidence.** A small number of controlled outcome studies, mostly from the 1980s and 1990s, have reported favourable outcomes for transactional analysis in general, and a smaller number of studies have reported outcomes comparable to other bona fide therapies. The redecisional school has produced a body of process research linking the completion of a redecision to favourable outcomes, and the integrative school has produced a more methodologically careful body of work on the relationship and on script-related change. A systematic review rated the overall evidence base as limited, with small samples, weak designs, and a heavy reliance on self-report and on measures closely tied to the theory's own constructs.

**What the evidence supports.** That the redecision is a coherent, well-specified, time-limited intervention that a practitioner can learn to deliver competently, and that it is among the more time-efficient interventions in this book. That the script account provides a coherent developmental formulation for young people whose difficulties are best understood as adaptive responses to earlier circumstances. That the theory's relational propositions are consistent with the broader alliance literature.

**What the evidence does not support.** Any claim that transactional analysis is superior to other bona fide therapies for any condition. Any claim that the redecision is the unique mechanism of change. Any claim that the popular version of the theory — in which the Adult is healthy and the Child is pathological — is a valid account of a young person.

**The developmental evidence.** The specific evidence for transactional analysis with children and adolescents is thin. The developmental applications are widely described in the clinical literature and poorly studied, and the cautions set out in Section 5.2 are not reflected in the outcome literature.

## 10. Strengths and Limitations of Transactional Analysis

**Strengths.** It offers a clear and legible theory that a young person can be helped to understand and to use, and the legibility is a genuine clinical asset, particularly with young people who have been told that they are "a mess" and who benefit from a framework in which their difficulties are structured and explicable. The script account is developmentally congruent, in that it locates the difficulty in the early relationship and treats it as an adaptation with a history. The redecision is a well-specified, efficient intervention with a clear mechanism and a clear protocol. The account of the injunction and the permission is a distinctive and clinically useful way of working with an adolescent's internalised authority. And the theory's insistence on the practitioner's own states and on the here-and-now relationship has been taken up productively by the integrative school.

**Limitations.** The popular version of the theory is actively harmful when applied to young people, because it reproduces the adult's view of the child. The evidence base is thin and heavily dependent on measures closely tied to the theory's own constructs. The theory's classification of the young person's "Child" state as the locus of the problem will, in a naive practitioner's hands, become the mechanism of a new pathologising. And the theory's account of the person is individualistic, with the developmental and relational revisions of the integrative school being genuinely new rather than a matter of emphasis.

## 11. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**The cultural location of the Parent–Adult–Child model.** The model is a product of a particular mid-century American understanding of the family: a nuclear household, with an internalised authority figure, a reasoning self, and a natural versus adapted division of the child's experience. In cultural contexts in which the family is structured differently, in which the authority is distributed, and in which the "reasoning self" is understood as relational rather than as a separate faculty, the model's developmental assumptions do not hold. A young person from a family in which the grandmother is the primary authority has a Parent whose content is not the parents', and a practitioner who works with the model as though it were the parents' will misunderstand the case.

**The danger of the Child/Adult hierarchy.** The ethical danger is the sharpest in this book. The framework, in its popular form, holds that the Child state is immature and the Adult state is mature, and that the therapeutic task is to strengthen the Adult and to quiet the Child. Applied to a young person, this is a prescription for the suppression of exactly the parts of the young person that are healthy and vital, and it is a prescription that will appeal to adults who find the young person's affectiveness difficult. The ethical requirement is that the practitioner hold the Natural Child — the joy, the spontaneity, the play, the creativity, the capacity for feeling — as the part of the young person that is doing the most good, and that the therapeutic task is the integration of the Adapted Child's pain with the Natural Child's vitality, not the elimination of either.

**The ethics of the redecision.** The redecision raises the question of whether the young person has genuinely chosen or has been led. The ethical requirements are: the decision must be the young person's; the practitioner must not have a preferred decision in mind; the young person must be able to decline to redecide; and the practitioner must be able to work with a young person who has, in fact, decided that they do not want to change. The last of these is the one most often failed, and the failure is usually a failure of the practitioner's own anxiety rather than of their technique.

**The ethical requirement of relational safety.** The integrative school's emphasis on the here-and-now relationship and on the practitioner's own states has an ethical dimension that is easily stated. With young people, a practitioner who is reactive, who is inconsistent, or who has an unresolved script of their own will damage the work regardless of the technical quality of the redecision. The requirement is that the practitioner attend to their own reactions as data about the young person's effect on them, and to bring that to supervision.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Ego state** — A phenomenologically distinct set of feelings, thoughts, and behaviours, available to awareness; the Parent, the Adult, and the Child.
- **Parent ego state** — The internalised representation of early authority figures, operating by injunction and by permission.
- **Adult ego state** — The component that observes, organises data, and estimates outcomes, operating on the principle of reality.
- **Child ego state** — The component that feels, remembers, wants, and acts from feeling; divided into the Natural Child and the Adapted Child.
- **Natural Child** — The spontaneous, joyful, and creative part of the young person; the part most often mistaken for the problem.
- **Adapted Child** — The part of the young person that has learned responses to the world that no longer fit; the location of the hurts, fears, and compliance.
- **Structural analysis** — The account of the composition of the personality and of the contamination between ego states.
- **Strategic analysis** — The account of the person's characteristic recurrent patterns of behaviour in relation to others.
- **Script analysis** — The account of the unconscious life plan and the early decision on which it is based.
- **Transaction** — The exchange of a stimulus and a response between two ego states, having a social, psychological, or operational vector.
- **Complementary transaction** — A mutually satisfying exchange between equivalent ego states.
- **Crossed transaction** — An exchange in which the response comes from a different ego state than the stimulus, producing confusion.
- **Ultraparallel transaction** — An exchange in which both parties address different subjects while appearing to communicate; a major mechanism of covert family communication.
- **Game** — A recurring patterned exchange with a predictable and self-defeating payoff; largely superseded by the script concept.
- **Script** — An unconscious life plan formed in early childhood, reinforced by parental injunctions and permissions, and expressed in predictable behaviours.
- **Script belief** — The unconscious conviction about the world and the self that underlies the script.
- **Script payoff** — The benefit, however small or costly, that the script is designed to produce; the key to the therapeutic work.
- **Script matrix** — A three-by-three grid mapping the levels of the script system against the areas of belief, behaviour, and feeling.
- **Racket system** — The covert, illegitimate means by which a person seeks to obtain what the script promises, as distinct from the legitimate means.
- **Injunction** — A parental prohibition or command that becomes part of the young person's internal standard.
- **Permission** — A parental allowance that becomes part of the young person's internal standard.
- **Redecision** — A mindful change made now, by an adult, in a decision, belief, or behaviour originally made in childhood.
- **Redecision process** — The six-step protocol: establish the script belief, trace its origins, identify the payoff, identify the script belief about the self, redecide, and test in action.
- **Contact-in-relationship** — Erskine's account of the therapist's attention to the here-and-now experience of the relationship with the young person.
- **Script system** — The network of beliefs, behaviours, and payoffs that sustains a script.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Transactional analysis developed from Berne's observation, made in group therapy with soldiers in the early 1950s, that his patients' accounts of their lives had a repetitive and dramatic structure that could be described in a vocabulary of social roles, exchanges, and life stories. It split into four schools — classical, redecisional, cathexis, and integrative — with different theories and different evidential standings.

The three-level model describes the Parent, the Adult, and the Child, with the Child's division into the Natural and the Adapted being the feature that most distinguishes the theory from its popular version. Structural, strategic, and script analyses describe the person's composition, their characteristic patterns, and their life plan. The script concept has been refined into the modern account of a script belief, a script payoff, and a script system, and the redecision is the theory's most distinctive intervention, with a six-step protocol that identifies the old decision, traces its origins, establishes its payoff, and invites a new one.

The developmental fit with young people is genuinely good in the script account, which locates the difficulty in the early relationship and treats it as an adaptation with a history. The specific danger is equally clear: the framework's hierarchy of Child and Adult reproduces the adult's view of the young person, and a practitioner who adopts the framework naively will validate the very attitude the young person came to escape. The requirement that the practitioner hold the Natural Child as the part doing the most good, and that the redecision be the young person's own, is the ethical core of the theory's contemporary development.

The evidence is limited and heavily dependent on measures closely tied to the theory's own constructs. The controversy over the theory's scientific recognition was conducted in the professional journals and should be read in the primary sources. The case of Jonty illustrates the script matrix in use and shows how a redecision, in a case with an early and painful origin, must be tested in action rather than made in the session.

## 14. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Describe Berne's founding observation and explain why the "blackout" was significant.
2. Distinguish the four schools of transactional analysis and state the clinical difference between the classical and the integrative.
3. State the three ego states and the six primary features of each.
4. Explain the difference between the Natural Child and the Adapted Child, and why the distinction matters in work with young people.
5. Distinguish structural, strategic, and script analysis, with an example of each.
6. What is a crossed transaction and what is an ultraparallel transaction, and why is the latter clinically important in family work?
7. State the six steps of the redecision process, and explain why the identification of the payoff is the most important of them.
8. Explain the developmental account in which the child's script is an adaptation to the parent's script, and state its clinical consequence.
9. State the specific danger of the transactional analysis framework in work with young people, and describe the clinician's own state in that session.
10. In the Jonty case, why would a practitioner who argued about the teachers' unfairness fail, and what would the argument need to address instead?
:::

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 15.1 — The Script Matrix Workshop (2 hours, groups of four)

Each group takes a case and fills in the three-by-three script matrix in the group, marking each cell with the group's degree of confidence. The groups then compare matrices, and the comparison is the exercise: the groups almost always agree on the factual material and disagree on the payoffs, and the disagreement is usually the most useful part of the session. The group should then produce a single sentence stating the young person's early decision.

### Activity 15.2 — The Redecision Protocol Rehearsal (90 minutes, triads)

One student presents a real or imagined stuck point, one conducts the six-step protocol with the triads' advice, and one observes for the point at which the "new decision" became the practitioner's decision rather than the client's. The observer's task is to mark the exact sentence, and the practitioner then responds to it in the next session. This is the most practically useful exercise in the chapter and it is also the most demanding, because the pressure to supply the young person with a good decision is strong.

### Activity 15.3 — The Popular Version Audit (60 minutes, written)

Write a 500-word account of transactional analysis as it is usually presented to the public — on a popular psychology podcast, in a self-help book, in a training advertisement. Then write a second 500-word account of it as the integrative school would present it. Then list the differences, and identify which of the differences are matters of clinical fact and which are matters of framing. The exercise develops the critical eye that the theory requires, and it is best done before the student begins using any of the concepts with a real young person.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory.** Read Berne (1964) for the structure and the script, and Berne (1972) for the redecision. Read them in that order, and read them with a pencil.

**For the contemporary clinical development.** Read Erskine (2014) and the integrative school's edited volume. This is where the theory is strongest and where the practical guidance for work with young people is clearest, and it is very little read.

**For the controversies.** Read the primary sources on both sides of the scientific recognition dispute, in the professional journals. The dispute is a useful case study in how a profession handles an internal disagreement, and the student should be able to describe it accurately and without taking a partisan position.

**For the development.** Read the attachment and developmental literature that the integrative school has drawn on, and read the developmental applications in the clinical literature with attention to the cautions in this chapter rather than to the enthusiasm.
