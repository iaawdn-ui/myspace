## 7. Cognitive Therapy with Children and Adolescents

Chapter 17 develops the developmental modifications in full. This section provides the outline that the full chapter follows, so that the reader can see the shape of the developmental argument before encountering it in detail.

**The four modifications.** Cognitive therapy with a young person requires modification in four areas: the level of processing at which the work is done, the language and the medium of the thought record, the degree of developmental approximation involved in the behavioural experiments, and the involvement of the parent or other adults.

**The developmental prohibition and its limit.** A young person below approximately twelve cannot reliably produce abstract propositional thoughts, cannot reliably introspect into automatic thoughts, and cannot reliably evaluate alternative beliefs against evidence. The modification is to work with concrete, situational, imaginal, and behavioural material, and the limit is that the work is then closer to behavioural therapy than to cognitive therapy in its adult form.

**The problem of the doubting mind.** A young person who has learned to doubt everything, and who has been told by adults that their thoughts are "not true," will not accept the collaborative empiricism on which the approach depends. The modification is to establish the collaborative stance through action rather than through argument — that is, through the joint conducting of behavioural experiments whose results the young person can observe.

**The parent.** The developmental literature supports parental involvement, and the cognitive therapist must be able to work with a parent who is a well-meaning but anxious participant in the young person's difficulties. Chapter 17 sets out the specific modifications.

## 8. Modern Developments

### 8.1 The transdiagnostic model

The transdiagnostic cognitive model, developed principally by Susan Muse and colleagues, is the most significant theoretical development in the cognitive tradition since Beck's original formulation, and it has a direct relevance to a youth service because it addresses the co-occurrence of problems rather than treating them as separate.

Its central claim is that a limited number of cognitive processes are shared across the emotional disorders: the overestimation of threat, the overestimation of uncontrollability, and the overestimation of the unfavourableness of the self. The accompanying treatment, the unified protocol, is a transdiagnostic cognitive therapy delivered in a flexible number of sessions.

The relevance to this course is that it matches the clinical reality of a youth service, where a young person frequently presents with several difficulties at once — anxiety, low mood, a conduct problem, and substance use — and where the traditional approach of treating each diagnosis with its own protocol is impractical. The transdiagnostic approach makes it possible to work on the shared processes and to address the co-occurrence directly.

### 8.2 The third wave and the relational turn

The third wave approaches discussed in Chapters 20 to 22 reformulated the cognitive model in two fundamental ways: they shifted the target from the content of thoughts to the function of thoughts, and they made the therapeutic relationship the primary intervention. The **relational cognitive therapy** tradition, associated with Jeremy Holmes and with the work that follows the maintenance model of the alliance, has made a complementary move: it locates the young person's core beliefs in the relationship with the practitioner, and it treats the alliance rupture as the primary occasion for core belief work.

This last development is particularly relevant with young people, because it does not require the young person to have the verbal capacity to produce propositional thoughts, and because it uses the relationship itself as the data.

### 8.3 The computerised and digital delivery

The digital delivery of cognitive therapy is now a substantial evidence base in its own right, and the findings are highly relevant to youth services: the digital treatments produce modest but real effects, they are acceptable to young people, and their acceptability is partly a function of the fact that they remove the need for the young person to disclose to an adult. The digital cognitive therapy for anxiety and for depression in young people is among the most evaluated of all psychological interventions, and the numbers of young people reached are an order of magnitude greater than those reached by any face-to-face service. Chapter 55 discusses the ethical and practice implications.

## 9. Evidence and Research Support

### 9.1 The strength of the evidence

The cognitive-behavioural therapies have the largest and most methodologically careful evidence base of any treatment in the profession, and a student should be able to specify it.

**For depression in young people.** The evidence for the manualised cognitive therapy for depression in adolescents and young adults is among the strongest in the child and adolescent literature, with multiple large randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses, and with a well-established developmental adaptation. The effects are moderate to large, and the treatment is the recommended first-line treatment for depression in young people in most international guidelines.

**For anxiety.** The evidence for the cognitive-behavioural treatments of anxiety in young people is likewise strong, and the adaptations for children — with the exposure components, the modelling, the parent involvement, and the concrete behavioural work — have been tested in large trials.

**For specific phobias and for panic.** The evidence is strong in adults and good in young people, and the exposure-based components have an unusually clear mechanism.

**For the transdiagnostic protocol.** The evidence is smaller but growing and includes randomised controlled trials in young people.

**For the alliance and the mechanism.** The process literature is substantial: the alliance predicts outcome in cognitive therapy, the alliance mediates the delivery of the technique, and the in-session rating of the young person's perceived helpfulness and the therapist's rating of the young person's receptiveness are among the more reliable predictors of session outcome.

### 9.2 The limits of the evidence

**The population problem.** The adolescent trials have included more diverse samples than the earlier adult literature, but the over-representation of white, affluent, female, and non-medically-ill participants persists, and the evidence for young people with neurodevelopmental conditions, with intellectual disability, with chronic physical health conditions, with severe and multiple mental health difficulties, and with justice involvement remains much thinner.

**The developer and allegiance problem.** A substantial proportion of the key trials in the adolescent depression literature were conducted by investigators with a strong allegiance to the treatment, and a re-analysis of the trials of a widely used manualised treatment for adolescent depression that excluded the developer-investigator trials found a substantially smaller effect. The finding has been widely discussed and is important for the student to know: the effect size in the independent trials is smaller than the effect size in the developer-led trials, though it remains clinically meaningful.

**The brevity of the follow-up.** A number of the key adolescent depression trials have relatively short follow-up periods, and the longer-term questions — about maintenance, about the developmental trajectory of the young person after the treatment ends, and about the effect on the developmental tasks the young person is working on — are largely unanswered.

**The generalisability question.** The evidence is strongest for the treatment of a defined disorder with a defined protocol, delivered by trained practitioners to a selected population, and it is considerably weaker for the "practical, integrated treatment" that most services actually deliver.

### 9.3 What the evidence does not support

That cognitive therapy is the treatment of choice for every young person with a cognitive presentation. That the cognitive model is a complete account of how young people become depressed or anxious. That the mechanisms proposed are the only mechanisms operating. Or that the equivalence of outcomes across therapies implies that the cognitive model is as good a framework as any other — the equivalence finding is a statement about the absence of a general hierarchy, not about the interchangeability of models in a given case.

## 10. Strengths and Limitations of the Cognitive Model

**Strengths.** It provides a clear and testable account of how difficulties develop and are maintained, and the account is one that can be checked rather than merely believed. It generates a coherent technique repertoire in which every intervention is connected to a mechanism, and this is the practical source of the treatment's efficiency and its manualisability. The collaborative empiricism is a genuinely respectful relational stance that suits a young person's developmental interest in being treated as a thinker rather than as a patient. The evidence base is the largest in the field, the treatment is deliverable in brief forms, and the digital versions reach young people who will not come to a clinic. The technique repertoire transfers into many other frameworks.

**Limitations.** The model is thin on the body, on emotion as an independent phenomenon, and on the developmental and relational embedding of the young person's thinking. Its account of the person is individualistic, and its account of learning is almost entirely propositional, which excludes the young people who do not think in propositions. The early versions of the model were developed and tested on adults and the developmental adaptations were added later, and the evidence for the adaptations is thinner than the evidence for the original. The model has a well-documented tendency to produce compliance rather than learning, particularly with young people who have learned that adults want a particular answer. And the cognitive model has been, historically, the least attentive of the major therapies to the social and structural determinants of a young person's difficulties.

## 11. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 11.1 The cultural location of the cognitive model

The cognitive model assumes that the young person is capable of a reflective, propositional, individual, and largely verbal process, and it assumes that the beliefs that maintain the difficulty are individual, internal, and appropriately open to examination. Several elements of this assumption are culturally marked.

**The individual and the autonomous thinker.** The model's figure is a person who generates beliefs, examines them, and revises them. In cultural contexts in which the self is relational, in which a young person's account of what is "true" is understood to be a matter of shared agreement rather than of private appraisal, and in which revision of one's beliefs is a public and socially consequential act, the collaborative empiricism may be experienced as intrusive rather than as respectful.

**The treated vs. the treating dynamic.** The collaborative empiricism presupposes a symmetry between the practitioner and the young person in the examination of evidence. Where the practitioner holds social, institutional, and possibly legal authority over the young person's life, this symmetry is nominal. The ethical requirement is to make the asymmetry explicit, and the practitioner's own account of a problem should be offered for examination alongside the young person's.

**The demand for verbal and introspective competence.** A young person who is not verbally fluent, who is neurodivergent, or who comes from a home in which the explicit articulation of belief is not modelled will struggle with the model's central procedure. The ethical requirement is that the practitioner's method adapt to the young person and not the other way about, which in practice means abandoning the thought record format for something more concrete while retaining the underlying discipline of collaborative examination.

### 11.2 The ethical requirement of not blaming

The most important ethical question raised by the cognitive model is the one it has been most often accused of: that the cognitive account of a young person's difficulty can become a claim that the young person is responsible for their own distress.

The distinction the student must hold is between an account of how a belief was formed and a judgement about the person who holds it. The cognitive model is an account of formation, and the clinical work is explicitly to trace the belief to its origins in a context in which the young person had little choice — a family in which the belief was taught, a school in which it was confirmed, a world in which it was accurate. The ethical requirement is that the tracing is done and is done genuinely, and that the practitioner does not allow a young person to end a course of treatment believing that they have simply failed to think correctly.

A second ethical requirement follows: the cognitive model is not an appropriate frame for difficulties that are produced by injustice, and a practitioner who applies it to a young person who has been racially abused at school, or who is in an unsafe home, or who is living in poverty, will produce a young person who blames themselves for a situation in which they had no part. The practitioner must hold the two accounts together, and the test of whether they have done so is whether the young person leaves the treatment with an accurate account of what happened to them.

### 11.3 The ethical requirements of the collaborative stance

**Informed consent to the examination of beliefs.** A young person should know what cognitive therapy involves, including that their beliefs will be examined and that the practitioner will offer alternative accounts. This is a real requirement and it is not usually met, because the method is presented as a conversation rather than as an intervention.

**The requirement of a right answer.** A young person should be able to decline to change a belief, and the practitioner must be able to work with a young person who holds a belief that is, on the evidence, accurate. A young person who believes that the school is unsafe because it is unsafe should not be helped to think otherwise.

**The requirement of competence.** The procedures of cognitive therapy are not neutral instruments. The guided discovery, the thought record, and the behavioural experiment can all be experienced as intrusive, and the practitioner must be competent in their delivery, must attend to the young person's response, and must be willing to abandon a procedure that is not working.

## 12. Case Illustration: The Thought Record and the Young Woman Who Could Not Say No

**The referral.** Aiko, seventeen, is referred by a college tutor for "panic and depression." Aiko has missed twenty-one of her forty-two scheduled classes. She has a long history of "anxiety" and has been prescribed medication twice, which she stopped on both occasions without telling her GP. She describes herself as "a worrier." She is bright, has a place at university conditional on grades she is not achieving, and her parents expect her to attend a medical school in her home country.

**The cognitive conceptualisation.** The conceptualisation is built over the first three sessions, and the practitioner works from the session model — asking Aiko to report what went through her mind in specific moments rather than asking her to describe her feelings in general.

The **hot spot** is the tutor's email. The **automatic thought** is: "Everyone will think I am stupid and a let-down." The **emotion** is a sharp, hot feeling in the chest and throat, and the **behaviour** is to not open the email, to avoid the tutor, and to miss the class.

The **intermediate beliefs** are: "If I make a mistake, people will judge me as a failure" and "If people judge me, I will not be able to survive it." The **core belief** is: "I am fundamentally inadequate." The **coping behaviours** are avoidance, over-preparation in a way that never quite arrives at adequacy, and, in the sessions themselves, a compliance with the practitioner that the practitioner notices and names.

**The compliance.** In the fourth session the practitioner says: "I want to check something with you. When I asked you yesterday what you thought about missing the class, you said 'I don't know, it just happened'. Three days ago you told me a very precise story about what you were thinking in the corridor. Which of those is the real one?" Aiko is quiet. Then: "The second one. I just didn't want to be a difficult client."

**The therapeutic work.** The work has three strands.

The first is the **behavioural experiment**, and it is designed collaboratively and rigorously. Aiko believes that if she asks a question in class, people will judge her as stupid. The experiment: she asks one question in a small seminar group, in a way agreed in advance, and the practitioner specifies the predictions and the observations in advance. Aiko's prediction: "They'll think I'm stupid and they'll remember it." The agreed observation: "Note what actually happens — who says what, and what they do next." The outcome: two people answer her question normally and one other student asks one back. The practitioner then insists on the detailed examination: "Your belief predicted that they would think you stupid and remember it. What actually happened?" Aiko says: "They didn't." The practitioner does not press the point further, which is a discipline the technique requires.

The second is the **thought record**, and it is adapted. Aiko is seventeen and can do a written thought record, so she does the standard version, and the practitioner reviews it weekly. Two features are added. The first is that the record is completed in the session, in part, so that the practitioner can observe the process; the second is the "what does the world need to look like for this belief to be true" column, which is the technique that connects the cognitive work to the systemic level.

The third strand is the **core belief work**, and it is here that the model's limits appear. Aiko's core belief of inadequacy is thoroughly supported by her developmental history: she was the family disappointment, the one who did not speak the family's language well, the one whose educational achievements were interpreted as compensating for something. A cognitive therapist who works only with the present will produce a young woman who knows better but does not feel better. The practitioner therefore works explicitly on the origin of the belief, in a manner closer to the psychodynamic than to the classic cognitive tradition, and in the sessions the practitioner begins to look at what happens between them, because the young woman's relationship to the practitioner is rapidly reproducing the relationship with the tutor.

**The developmental and cultural questions.** Three.

The developmental question: Aiko is at the stage at which the identity task is in progress, and the possibility of a life that is not the family's plan is a developmental task and not a symptom. The practitioner asks what Aiko wants, and discovers that Aiko does not know, and that this not-knowing is a developmental achievement rather than a deficit. The cognitive work on the belief "I am fundamentally inadequate" is therefore also the work of making room for the developmental task of choosing.

The cultural question: Aiko's family has a plan, the plan involves a return to the home country, and the plan is not a choice that Aiko has made. The practitioner asks the family question directly: "What does your daughter need to be?" and the answer, when the mother is asked, is that she needs to be a doctor who can support the family. The practitioner does not proceed further with the family work at that point, and records the decision and the reason, which is that the differentiation of Aiko from the family's project has to wait until Aiko has enough of her own sense of what she wants to be able to survive the conversation.

The ethical question: Aiko's medication has been stopped twice without the GP knowing. This is a safeguarding and a coordination issue that the practitioner raises directly with Aiko, and it is handled as a collaborative problem rather than as a disclosure.

**The outcome.** Over four months, the attendance rises to seventy per cent, the panic episodes reduce, and Aiko continues to be treated. The core belief is not resolved and is unlikely to be; Aiko describes it as "still being there, but quieter." The practitioner records this as a partial and honest outcome and continues the treatment at a reduced frequency. The last session in this phase is spent on Aiko's own formulation of her position, which she writes out and which the practitioner keeps with Aiko's permission. It is a good formulation. It is not a cognitive one, and the practitioner notes that in the record.

## 13. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Cognitive model** — Beck's account in which the way a person construes a situation determines the emotional and behavioural response to it.
- **Session model** — Beck's method of eliciting and evaluating thoughts in the middle of a session, the ancestor of the thought record.
- **Automatic thought** — A self-directed internal statement arising spontaneously in a situation, experienced as true, and producing an emotional response.
- **Hot spot** — The specific situation in which a particular automatic thought produces the strongest emotional response; the organising structure of the assessment.
- **Intermediate belief** — A conditional rule or assumption derived from a core belief, operating in specific situations.
- **Core belief** — A global, deeply held, and highly resistant belief about oneself, one's situation, or one's future.
- **The cognitive triad** — Beck's original formulation of unhelpful beliefs about the self, the world, and the future; the model of which the triad was the heart.
- **Cognitive conceptualisation** — The five-component account of a case: core belief, intermediate beliefs, automatic thoughts and hot spots, behavioural and physiological responses, and coping behaviours.
- **Collaborative empiricism** — Beck's term for the joint investigation of propositions, in which the practitioner and the client are both prepared to be persuaded by the evidence.
- **Guided discovery** — The method by which the collaborative stance is enacted through questions that help the young person find the answer rather than delivering it.
- **Thought record** — The structured record of a situation, the emotions, the automatic thoughts, the evidence for and against, the alternative thoughts, and the consequences.
- **Behavioural experiment** — A planned test of a prediction derived from a belief, conducted in the world, whose outcome is the data.
- **Decentring** — The technique of holding a thought as a thought by considering the full range of evidence and perspectives.
- **Continuum of change** — The technique of helping a young person see a belief as a continuum rather than as an absolute, used with dichotomous thinking.
- **Imagery rescripting** — The modification of a mental image in order to change the emotional response to it.
- **Transdiagnostic model** — The account in which a limited number of cognitive processes operate across the emotional disorders.
- **Unified protocol** — A transdiagnostic cognitive therapy designed to be delivered flexibly in a limited number of sessions.
- **The maintenance model** — The account in which the young person's core beliefs are maintained in and through the therapeutic relationship, and in which the alliance rupture is the primary occasion for core belief work.
- **Convergence of therapy and medicine** — The practice of treating the body's physiological state as a component of the cognitive work, developed in behavioural medicine and in health psychology.
- **Equivocality** — The quality of a young person's thinking that is a normal and developmentally appropriate feature of the adolescent's mind and is frequently pathologised by adults.
:::

## 14. Chapter Summary

Cognitive therapy was developed by Aaron Beck in the 1960s in the treatment of depression, and it is the first treatment in the profession's history to have been manualised, tested in a controlled trial, and exported. Its theoretical foundation is George Kelly's personal construct theory, and its central method is the session model.

The model describes cognition at three levels: the conscious, the automatic, and the underlying. The automatic thoughts are situationally bound and adaptive; the intermediate beliefs are the conditional rules that generate difficulty in specific situations; the core beliefs are the global and deeply held convictions that give the others their structure. Beck's original cognitive triad has been replaced by a hierarchical model, and the technical organisation of the work is around the hot spot.

The cognitive conceptualisation has five components — core belief, intermediate beliefs, automatic thoughts and hot spots, behavioural and physiological responses, and coping behaviours — and it is a hypothesis to be tested in session rather than a document to be filed. The technique repertoire follows from the model: the thought record, the behavioural experiment, the agenda-setting session structure, and the more advanced work with core beliefs through behavioural experiments and the examination of the young person's evidence about themselves.

The session is agenda-led and the relationship is collaborative, and the empirical literature on the alliance in cognitive therapy confirms what the theory predicts: the alliance mediates the delivery of the technique. The modern developments — the transdiagnostic model, the relational turn, and the digital delivery — have extended the model's reach considerably, and the last of these is reaching young people who would never attend a clinic.

The evidence base is the largest in the field, and the limits of that evidence are substantial: the population problem persists, the developer-allegiance problem has reduced the estimated effect size in independent trials, and the follow-up periods are often short. The model's limitations are equally real: it is thin on the body, on emotion, on development, and on the social determinants, and it has a documented tendency to produce compliance rather than learning. The ethical requirements that follow are the examination of the young person's belief with genuine openness, the requirement to trace the belief to its origins, and the requirement not to use the model to explain away injustice. The case of Aiko shows the model at its most effective and the point at which it requires supplementation.

## 16. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 16.1 — The Hot Spot Map (90 minutes, individual, then pairs)

Choose a recent difficult situation from your own life. Reconstruct the moment in the concrete, sensory terms of the session model — what you saw, what you heard, what you were thinking, in the form in which the thoughts occurred. Identify the automatic thought, the emotion, and its bodily location. Then write the intermediate belief and the core belief beneath it. Bring the map to the seminar. The exercise is uncomfortable and it is the foundation of every later clinical skill in this course.

### Activity 16.2 — The Experimental Design Clinic (2 hours, small groups)

Each group takes a belief that is amenable to a behavioural experiment and designs the experiment in detail: the prediction stated in a form that could be disconfirmed, the specific test, the agreed observations, and the analysis of what would count as disconfirmation. The group must also specify what the young person would do differently if the belief were true. The groups then swap designs and critique them, looking specifically for experiments that are incapable of disconfirming the belief. The critique is the exercise.

### Activity 16.3 — The Compliance Check (60 minutes, pairs, recorded)

One student role-plays a practitioner using guided discovery; the other plays a sixteen-year-old who is compliant and who wants to please. The practitioner is instructed to notice the compliance aloud. Then swap, and the "practitioner" in the second round is instructed to notice the difference in the young person's response. The purpose is to make the compliance of a young person visible, which is a skill that is difficult and that most adults find almost impossible to do without considerable practice.
:::

## 17. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model and the technique.** Read Beck (1976) for the model, Beck (1988) for the technique with depression, and Beck (2011) for the current clinical practice. Read Padesky (1994) alongside them for the collaborative empiricism, which is the conceptual heart of the approach and is usually under-taught.

**For the developmental work.** Read Chapter 17 of this book and then the developmental depression and anxiety literatures, including the treatment adaptation literature. Note what the adaptations preserve and what they sacrifice, and note the cost of the adaptations in terms of the evidence base.

**For the critique.** Read the health psychology literature on the cognitive model in physical health conditions, the transdiagnostic literature, and the critical literature on the cultural assumptions of the cognitive model. The model is more robust than its critics allow and more limited than its proponents claim, and the student should be able to hold both.

**For the mechanism.** Read the process research on the alliance in cognitive therapy and on the mediators of outcome. This is the evidence that connects the theory to the outcome and that reconciles the cognitive and common factors positions.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is aaron Beck's theory of depression and anxiety, the cognitive conceptualization triangle, and the disciplined practice of cognitive therapy. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is appraisal, belief, emotion, behaviour, skills practice, and relapse prevention. The mechanism to keep in view is how thoughts, bodily states, avoidance, reinforcement, and action maintain one another in repeating cycles. The characteristic professional risk is arguing with a young person, overlooking development and environment, or treating a thought as false merely because it is painful. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |

Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in structured sessions, homework reviews, school-based practice, and family-supported skill building, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Tunde is fifteen and is seen in a community adolescent clinic for school avoidance and rising panic. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Tunde's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Tunde explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on appraisal, belief, emotion, behaviour, skills practice, and relapse prevention.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Tunde chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Tunde has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Tunde can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Cognitive Therapy: The Cognitive Model to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
