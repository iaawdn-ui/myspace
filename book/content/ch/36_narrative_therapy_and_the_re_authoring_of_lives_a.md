# Narrative Therapy and the Re-Authoring of Lives

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of the narrative therapy movement and describe the intellectual context in which it emerged.
- State the core concepts of narrative therapy, including the idea of the person as the author of their own life, the externalisation of the problem, and the re-authoring.
- Describe the techniques of externalisation, the naming of the problem, the mapping of its influence and its influence on the young person, the unique outcomes, and the re-authoring.
- Explain the use of documents, letters, and the therapeutic group in narrative practice.
- Apply the narrative framework to a case involving an adolescent and a family.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of narrative work, including the questions of whose story is told, of power, and of the relationship between the personal narrative and the material circumstances.

## Chapter Overview

Narrative therapy is the most recent of the family therapies in this book and the one with the most substantial intellectual debt to the wider human and social sciences. It came out of the late 1980s in Australia and New Zealand, and it was shaped by three sources that practitioners in the helping professions rarely combine: literary theory, particularly the structuralist and post-structuralist analysis of narrative; feminist epistemology, particularly the critique of the way in which knowledge about women has been produced by the professions; and the intellectual traditions of post-colonialism and of the social sciences' interest in how identities are constituted by discourse.

Its central claim is that **a person's identity is constituted by the stories they live by and tell about themselves, and that the stories are not the person**. This sounds abstract and is in fact highly practical. A young person who has lived for two years with the story "I am the one who ruins everything" has organised their behaviour around that story, their relationships around it, and their expectations about the future around it. The story is not a description of the person. The story is a framework through which the person has made sense of a life that has been genuinely hard, and the framework is now the thing that is producing the outcomes.

The practice of narrative therapy is therefore the practice of helping a young person to identify the stories that are organising their life, to examine them, to find the exceptions to them, to develop what the work calls **unique outcomes** — the moments and the events that do not fit the dominant story — and to use those unique outcomes to begin to author a different account of their life.

The approach is the most explicitly political of the therapies in this book, in the sense that it is concerned with how power operates in the production of knowledge, and this is both its greatest contribution and the source of its most substantial difficulties, which are addressed in Sections 9 and 10.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The development

The movement grew out of the family therapy practices of Michael White and David Epston in Melbourne and Auckland, from the mid-1980s. Both were family therapists with a social work and a literary background, and both were frustrated by two features of the family therapy of the period: its assumption that the family was a self-contained system that could be understood by mapping it, and its silence about the larger social structures within which families lived and about the power relations within them.

The break with the family therapy tradition was sharp. The structural and strategic schools treated the family as a system whose patterns could be mapped, and the narrative therapists asked a prior question: **by what criteria is this family understood to be a family at all?** A family that includes a violent father and an abused child is not a family in the way that the systemic models assume, and the framework of the family is itself a product of a moral and political judgement.

### 1.2 The intellectual sources

**The literary and post-structuralist analysis of narrative.** The structuralist narratologies of Propp, Todorov, and Genette, and the post-structuralist work of Barthes, Derrida, and Ricoeur, provided the analytic vocabulary: the distinction between the story and the discourse; the concept of the **fabula** as the raw material of lived events and the **syuzhet** as the organising shape given to them; the concept of the **narrative frame**, which selects certain events and excludes others; and the concept of the **absent or excised text**, the parts of the story that the frame excludes.

**Feminist epistemology.** The critique of the professional claim to know the truth about a client, particularly a woman, and the analysis of the ways in which a professional discourse can constitute the identity of the person it describes, came from the feminist critique of the therapies and the feminist research tradition.

**Post-colonial and social constructionist thought.** The analysis of how identities are constituted within discourses of culture, race, gender, and class, and the concept of the "othering" of people by the dominant discourse, informed the practice's treatment of the problem as an agent.

**Social work practice research.** The practice-based research movement in social work, of which the Australian and New Zealand family therapy tradition was a part, contributed the emphasis on the small, the local, and the specific.

### 1.3 The reception

Narrative therapy has been adopted in a very large number of settings, and it is used in services that have no connection to family therapy, in schools, in community mental health, in social services, in services for refugees, and in services for domestic violence. Its influence extends well beyond the practice of the model: the concept of the externalisation is used in coaching, in education, in organisational change, and in the self-help literature, and the **therapeutic conversation** in its modern form is discussed in Chapter 58 of this book.

## 2. The Core Concepts

### 2.1 The person as the author of their life

The foundational claim is that **a person is the author of their life**, and the word "author" is doing specific work. An author does not write a book in a single stroke; an author writes, revises, and re-writes, and a book is the product of many drafts. The claim is therefore not that the young person has complete control over what happens to them, and the practice is careful to say so, but that the young person has a role in the shaping of the account of their own life, and that the account is a *draft* rather than a sentence.

Three corollaries follow.

**Identity is not fixed.** If the identity is constituted by the story, and the story is a draft, then the identity is revisable.

**The problem is not the person.** The problem is a thing that does things to the person, and it is the subject of a story, and it has interests of its own.

**Meaning is made, not found.** The meaning of an experience is not in the experience; it is in the account given of it, and the account can be different.

### 2.2 Externalisation

**Externalisation** is the practice of separating the person from the problem, so that the problem becomes an object that can be seen, described, named, and examined, and the person becomes the subject of their own life rather than the object of the problem's.

The classic example in the literature is the young person who says of their drinking, "The Drink took over my life." The externalisation takes the sentence and changes the grammar: "the drink" is given the attributes of an agent — it is powerful, it is persuasive, it takes over, it is cunning — and the young person is given the attributes of a subject who is acted upon and who can resist.

**The practice of externalisation** has a technical vocabulary, and the practitioner should be able to name it.

**Naming the problem or naming it differently.** The name is the young person's own, and the practitioner sometimes suggests a name and sometimes asks. "The Worry" and "The Fog" and "The Voice" are names that appear in the literature, and they are usually funny, and the humour is a feature: it is what makes the problem nameable without it being an insult.

**Writing the problem's influence.** "What does the worry do to you? What does it make you do?" and the answer is written down, often by the young person themselves, and often in the form of a list.

**Writing the problem's influence on the young person's influence on others.** "What does the worry make you do to your mum? What does it make you do to your friends?" The second question is the crucial one, because it locates the problem in the young person's life as an agent with power, and it makes the young person's own actions explicable.

**Identifying the unique outcomes.** "Are there any times when the worry doesn't have as much power? What did you do to make that happen?" and this is the heart of the practice.

**Authoring a new story.** "If the worry is not in charge, what would your life look like? How would your friends know? What would be different at school?" and the new account is written.

### 2.3 The problem's influence and the young person's influence

The **problem's influence** is the network of effects that the problem has on the young person's life: what it prevents, what it makes them do, what it costs them. The **young person's influence** is the network of effects that the young person has on their own life and on others: what they do that makes things better, what they are good at, what they influence in other people.

The practice of mapping both is the most structured technique in the model, and it is usually done on paper: a diagram with the problem at the centre, arrows going out to the effects it has, and arrows going in from the areas of the young person's life in which they have influence.

The clinical significance is that the second map is almost always thinner than the first, and this is the point. The young person has been living in a world in which the problem is the most powerful thing in it, and the discovery that they already have influence in some areas — however small — is the beginning of a different account.

### 2.4 Unique outcomes

A **unique outcome** is an event, a moment, a feeling, a thought, or a piece of learning in the young person's life that does not fit the dominant story, and that therefore opens a space for a different story.

The criteria are specific, and the practitioner should be able to state them.

**The unique outcome must be the young person's own.** It is not something the practitioner suggests and it is not something that happened to them.

**It must be a specific event, not a generalisation.** "I was able to tell my dad I was not going to the party" is a unique outcome. "I am a more assertive person" is not.

**It must be a departure from the dominant story.** If it fits the story, it is not a unique outcome, and a practitioner who cannot find one has usually asked the wrong question.

**It must be sufficiently detailed to be re-told.** The detail is what makes the re-authoring possible, because the re-authoring works by re-describing the same event with a different meaning.

**It must be linked to a skill or a preference or a value that the young person already has.** The unique outcome is not a fantasy; it is evidence of a capacity that already exists.

### 2.5 The dominant story and its problems

The **dominant story** — in the older literature, the "problem-saturated story" — is the account of the self that the young person lives by, and the practice of naming it is a distinctive and important step.

A dominant story is characterised by: a **subject position**, which is the self that the story requires the young person to occupy, usually a self that is the cause of the problem; a **plot**, which is the sequence that the story tells; and a **moral**, which is the conclusion the story draws about the young person and about the world.

The dominant story of a young person referred for behavioural problems in school frequently runs something like: "I am the one who causes trouble, and trouble follows me everywhere, and there is nothing to be done about it." The story is coherent, it is widely reinforced by the responses of others, and it is self-fulfilling: a young person who is the one who causes trouble behaves in the way that confirms the story, and the confirmation is read as evidence for the story.

**Naming the story** is done carefully and with permission. The practitioner asks: "How would you describe someone in your situation? What would you say is the story about you?" and the young person's own words are used, and the question "What is the effect of this story on your life?" opens the examination.

### 2.6 The re-authoring

The **re-authoring** is the process of rewriting the account of the young person's life so that it includes the unique outcomes, and it is the outcome of the whole practice.

The re-authoring is not the same as the positive reframe of the solution-focused school, and the difference is worth stating. A positive reframe replaces a negative account with a positive one. A re-authoring expands the account, and the expansion makes room for the unique outcomes, and it does not require the negative account to have been false. The young person who has been bullied did not imagine it, and the re-authoring does not require them to say otherwise; it requires them to be able to say that the bullying did not determine the rest of their life, and to point to the specific evidence.

The technique of the re-authoring is to return to a specific unique outcome and to re-describe it, in detail, with the young person dictating, in their own words, and then to ask the questions that extend the account: "How does this change your understanding of what happened? If this is who you are in some situations, what does that mean for the other situations? Who else would have to change their story to make room for this?"

The last question is the one that makes the work systemic, and it is the question that distinguishes narrative therapy from a purely individual practice.

## 3. The Techniques in Detail

### 3.1 Externalising conversations

**The grammar of externalisation.** The work is done in the language. "The problem makes you do" becomes "You do" and "the problem" becomes the subject. "The Worry" is given a name, a personality, a set of characteristics, and an agenda. A young person who says "the worry takes over my body" is invited to describe what the worry does, and the describing is the intervention, because the young person is now observing the worry rather than being inside it.

**The questions that externalise.** "What does the problem do to you?" "What does the problem want?" "How does the problem talk to you?" "What would the problem say if it could speak for itself?" "What does the problem do to make sure you do what it wants?" These questions are the technique, and they are asked slowly, and they are asked in a tone of genuine curiosity.

**The rules of externalising questions.** They are asked of the problem and not of the person. They are asked more than once, because the first answer is usually a summary. They never suggest the answer. And they are not asked in a way that triviallyises: the question "what does the problem want?" presupposes that the problem wants something, and for a young person who has been told that their problem is their own fault, the presupposition is the intervention.

### 3.2 The documents

The **documents** are the most distinctive feature of the narrative practice, and they are of three kinds.

**The letter from the problem.** A letter written by the problem to the young person, in the problem's voice, describing what it has done to them and what it wants. It is usually written by the young person, sometimes dictated to the practitioner, and it is often the first document because it is the most engaging and the least threatening.

**The letter to the problem.** A letter written by the young person to the problem, often as a reply, in which the young person tells the problem what it may and may not do from now on. This is the beginning of the boundary-setting, and the letter is often more useful than a conversation because it is in the young person's own words and it can be re-read.

**The letter to the young person.** A letter from the practitioner to the young person, written at the end of a piece of work and given to them, and the letter is a record of what the practitioner has noticed and what the practitioner values, and it is often the artefact that the young person keeps.

**The "take-away" letter.** The most common of the documents: a letter written by the young person to themselves, to be read at a difficult moment, containing the unique outcomes and the re-authored account. Its efficacy depends entirely on the young person's own words.

**The value of the documents.** They externalise the conversation from the session; they slow it down, because writing is slower than speaking; they give the young person something to hold; and they make the re-authoring concrete, because the re-authored account has to be written in sentences.

### 3.3 The therapeutic group

The **therapeutic group** — also called the storytelling group — is the group in which the re-authoring is done collectively, and it is a distinctive and powerful element of the practice.

The structure is: each member tells their story to the group in a structured way; the group listens for the unique outcomes; and the group members then ask questions, and the questions are restricted to two forms — "what was different?" and "how come?" — with the rule that no advice, no interpretation, and no "have you tried" is permitted.

The rules are the therapeutic mechanism, and they are the point of the whole technique. The questions "what was different?" and "how come?" are questions that the young person answers, and the answers are the unique outcomes, and the group is a machine for producing them.

**The evidence for the group format is good**, and the group is used with adolescents, with families, and with adults, and it is the format in which the approach is most clearly distinct from the individual work of the other therapies in this book.

### 3.4 The role of documents in the school setting

In a school, the documents have a specific and useful function: they can be shared with the teachers. A letter from the problem, written by the young person, that describes what the problem does in the classroom and what it wants, can be given to a form tutor, and it converts the teacher's response from a reaction to the young person into a response to the problem. This is a small intervention with a large effect, and it is one of the most practically useful applications of the model in a school setting.
