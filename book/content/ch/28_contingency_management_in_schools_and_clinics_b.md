
## 7. Implementation: The Part That Determines Everything

### 7.1 The fidelity problem

The single most reliable finding in the school-based applied literature is that the outcome is determined by the fidelity of the implementation, and the school-based studies that measure fidelity and find it low report null results that reflect the implementation rather than the intervention. The practitioners who design programmes are systematically the ones who implement them least, because the work of teaching is what occupies the time.

Four measures make the difference, and they should be built into any plan from the beginning.

**A written protocol**, in the room, in short form, with the target behaviours, the responses, and the exception procedures.

**A staff training session**, including a rehearsal, and a refresher at the start of each term.

**A fidelity check**, in which the practitioner or a colleague records whether the response was delivered as specified. The fidelity check is uncomfortable and it is the most useful thing in the plan, because it converts "this isn't working" into "this is being delivered 40 per cent of the time".

**A short review cycle.** Fortnightly is usually right. A monthly review is too slow to catch a drift; a daily review is not sustainable for a practitioner with a caseload.

### 7.2 The problem of the adult who is not on the plan

Any school-based plan has to specify what happens with the adults who are not on it. The options are: train every adult who will have contact with the young person, which is the best option and is often impossible; or specify a small number of responses that any adult can apply, which is the second best; or accept the inconsistency and design the plan to survive it.

The third option is not a failure. A plan that survives inconsistent implementation is a robust plan, and robust plans are built on the antecedents and the consequences that are most widely available. The "first day" design of Chapter 25 is robust in this sense, because the young person's own action is the treatment.

### 7.3 The problem of turnover

Staff turnover destroys school-based interventions with a regularity that practitioners underestimate. A plan that depends on a named adult's consistent implementation is a plan with a shelf life of one or two terms.

Three responses help. Build the plan around the classroom environment rather than around the teacher's skill, so that it survives the teacher's departure. Train the whole class in the routine, so that the class maintains it. And put the plan in a form that a relief teacher can follow in thirty seconds.

### 7.4 The problem of the young person who is not in the room

Contingency management assumes the young person is present when the contingency operates. In a school, the young person is frequently not present: they are at the office, in the inclusion room, in the corridor, at the dentist, in a fixed-term exclusion. A plan that delivers a reward at the end of the lesson to a young person who was in the corridor for the last twenty minutes will teach the young person that the corridor is the place to be, because the reward is available at the corridor's end too.

The specification should say explicitly what happens to the rewards accumulated during an absence, and in most systems the correct answer is that they are held and available on return, with a stated rule about the maximum accumulation.

### 7.5 The problem of the young person who declines

A young person who refuses to participate in a contingency programme presents a question that the practitioner should be ready for, and the answer depends on the young person.

Where the young person is refusing because the plan is not what they want, the plan should be renegotiated, because a plan the young person does not accept will not be implemented. Where the young person is refusing because they do not believe the system will be honoured, the first task is to honour it exactly as written for long enough to build the credibility, and the practitioner should say openly that this is what is happening. Where the young person is refusing because the behaviour is being maintained by something the plan does not address — and this is the most common reason — the plan is wrong and the functional assessment must be revisited.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The status of the evidence

The evidence for school-based behavioural intervention is substantial and mature, and it is strongest in three areas.

**Classroom-level interventions.** The class-wide function-related intervention team and its relatives have good support for reducing the level of disruption in a classroom, and the effect sizes reported in the meta-analyses are large, particularly on the proximal outcomes of on-task behaviour and teacher-directed talk. The limitations are the modest effect on the individual student's academic outcomes and the dependence on the teacher implementation.

**Token economies.** The evidence for classroom token economies is good for the reduction of disruptive behaviour, and the older institutional literature is extensive. The modern evidence is thinner than the practice.

**Function-based support plans.** Plans built on a functional behavioural assessment, and delivered consistently, produce better outcomes than plans built on the behavioural reduction model, and the finding is consistent with the applied evidence in Chapter 24.

**Contingency management for substance use.** The school-based and clinic-based applications of contingency management in the treatment of substance use have some of the largest measured effects in the psychological literature, and Chapter 47 develops this.

### 8.2 What the evidence says about implementation

Three findings are more useful to a practitioner than the outcome studies.

**Fidelity determines the outcome.** Where fidelity is measured, it is usually low, and the studies with high fidelity show larger effects.

**Training alone is insufficient.** Staff training improves knowledge and does not reliably improve implementation, and the addition of a written protocol, a rehearsal, and a monitoring process is what produces change in behaviour.

**Classroom-level systems have larger effects than individual-level systems** for classroom-wide outcomes, and the reason is that the classroom-level system changes the peer ecology, which the individual system cannot.

### 8.3 Limitations of the evidence

**The outcomes are proximal.** The studies measure the behaviour in the classroom, and the distal outcomes — the attainment, the family relationships, the mental health, the educational trajectory — are rarely measured, and the assumption that the proximal gains produce the distal gains is an assumption rather than a finding.

**The samples are selected.** The studies are frequently conducted with the classes and the students who are easiest to include, and the young people with the most severe needs and the least predictable attendance are systematically under-represented.

**The implementation conditions are exceptional.** The published studies are frequently conducted by the developers of the intervention, in schools with the resources to support the research, with the research team providing the training that a school would have to provide for itself.

**The evidence is Anglo-American and context-specific.** A token economy depends on the availability of certain backup reinforcers, on the currency of the tokens, and on the institutional culture of the school, and the transferability across systems is limited.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Contingency Management in Institutions

### 9.1 Strengths

**It is the clearest and most teachable form of the behavioural principles**, and it is the point at which the abstract concepts of reinforcement become visible and manipulable to a team.

**It produces rapid, measurable change**, which is essential in a setting where the young people who are referred have already experienced many slow interventions.

**It is compatible with the rest of the school's disciplinary framework**, because it specifies the consequences rather than replacing them.

**It involves the adults**, and the process of writing the plan with the teacher is itself a useful clinical intervention, because it forces the adult to state explicitly what they are currently doing and why.

**It can be adapted to any setting**, from a classroom to a clinic to a residential unit.

### 9.2 Limitations

**It is only as good as the implementation**, and school-based implementation is a chronically unreliable enterprise.

**It can be experienced as punishment.** A young person who is subject to a visible token economy in a school where the rest of the students are not may experience it as a punishment, and in some settings the removal of privileges in the name of a token system is indistinguishable from the removal of privileges in the name of a sanction.

**It is easily turned into a control system rather than a teaching system.** A system that removes more than it rewards, that is applied to a class rather than to a person, and that is not reviewed, has become a control system, and the literature on institutional practices makes this abundantly clear.

**It says nothing about why the behaviour occurs.** A young person whose disruption is maintained by a peer dynamic, by a learning difficulty, or by a family situation will not be helped by a well-run token economy, and the practitioner should be alert to the displacement of the problem.

**It is rarely faded.** A great many token economies in schools are in their third year, and the young people in them are correspondingly unable to work without them.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 The market objection in the school

The ethical objection discussed in Chapter 26 is sharper in a school, because a school is an institution with an authority relation to the young people in it, and because the peers can see.

Four points follow, and they are the practical requirements of any school-based system.

**Never trade a right.** No system may condition access to education, to meals, to the toilet, to safety, to advocacy, or to a statutory process on behaviour. This is a legal requirement as well as an ethical one, and a practitioner who proposes a system that breaches it is proposing an unlawful arrangement.

**Never trade attention or warmth.** The adults' attention and warmth are not the currency. The specific practical prohibition is that the adults do not use praise and approval as the tokens, because that makes the adults' regard conditional on the young person's performance, and because it is the practice that most reliably produces a compliant and dependent young person.

**Never make the system public if it would humiliate.** For a young person who is already a target, the visible token chart is a hazard, and the design should protect them. The individual system should be delivered without a public display, and the class system should be designed so that the tokens are not individually visible.

**Do not let the system replace the relationship.** A class-wide system that reduces the disruption and fills the lesson with token administration can produce a classroom in which the teacher and the class no longer talk to each other, and this is a real and documented outcome, and the practitioner should watch for it.

### 10.2 Cultural considerations

**The reinforcers available in the school reflect the school's resources and values.** A school in a community with a strong culture of sporting achievement can offer access to the team; a school in a community where that is not valued cannot. The token economy must be built from what is genuinely available and genuinely valued in that community.

**The response to disruption is culturally patterned.** In some school cultures, a public correction is the ordinary way of maintaining order, and a practitioner who introduces a subtler response must be prepared to explain it and to support the teacher in it, because the teacher will be managing the reactions of colleagues.

**The status of the individual in the peer group determines the viability of the system.** A system that signals compliance as a virtue is received very differently by a young person whose peer group values defiance, and a practitioner should establish what the peer group rewards before designing a system that asks the young person to move against it.

**The discipline and the support frameworks are culturally and legally specific**, and a practitioner working outside the jurisdiction in which they trained must learn the local framework before writing a plan.

### 10.3 Ethical requirements

**Consultation and assent.** The young person and, where the system applies to a class, the class must be consulted. A system imposed on a group will be resisted, and the resistance is rational.

**Proportionality and the least restrictive option.** The consequences must be proportionate to the behaviour, and the less intrusive alternatives must be considered and recorded.

**Protection from the aggregation of power.** A behaviour support plan that concentrates the authority of several adults over one young person without review is a risk, and the plan should specify the review point at which the accumulation is examined.

**Documentation and the right to see it.** The plan, the data, and the reviews should be documented, and the young person and the family should be able to see them.

**Review with the family and with the young person, on a stated cycle**, with the question "is this plan still necessary?" included in the review.

**The end of the plan.** Every plan should have a stated end, and a plan with no end is a plan that has become the young person's accommodation.

**The recognition of the possibility of harm.** A practitioner who is introducing a system into a school should be able to state what harm the system might do, and how they would know, and what they would do. In most cases the answer involves the young person's peer relationships, and the answer is not usually "nothing".

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Contingency management in a setting** — The deliberate restructuring of the reinforcement structure of a classroom, a clinic, a ward, or a family, with specified behaviours, specified consequences, trained staff, and a review cycle.
- **Token economy** — A system in which specified behaviours are reinforced with tokens exchangeable for specified backup reinforcers.
- **Token** — The small, durable, immediately delivered item used as the conditioned reinforcer in a token economy.
- **Backup reinforcer** — The item or privilege for which the tokens are exchanged.
- **Exchange procedure** — The specified manner, time, and place at which tokens are converted into backup reinforcers.
- **Response cost** — The specified, small, and immediate loss of tokens following a targeted behaviour.
- **Fade plan** — The written schedule on which the token economy will be withdrawn, with the criteria for withdrawal.
- **Class-wide system** — A token economy applied to a whole class, with the consequences delivered to the class as a unit.
- **Individual system** — A token economy applied to a named young person for a specific target behaviour.
- **Contamination of the target behaviour** — The process by which the behaviour becomes contingent on the token rather than on the contingencies of the young person's life.
- **Behaviour support plan** — The written document specifying a young person's target behaviours, strategies, contingencies, adult roles, data collection, and review arrangements.
- **Graduated response procedure** — The specified, rehearsed, and consistent sequence of adult responses to increasing levels of disruption.
- **Escalation procedure** — The specified adult response to a young person's escalation, including the condition for the return to the setting.
- **Return condition** — The specified requirement that must be met before a young person removed from a setting returns, which prevents the escalation from being reinforced by the relief.
- **Fidelity of implementation** — The extent to which the specified adult responses were delivered as designed, measured independently of the outcome.
- **Written protocol** — The short, in-room document specifying the target behaviours, the responses, and the exception procedures.
- **Staff rehearsal** — The practice, in a training session, of delivering the specified responses to a simulated disruption.
- **Refresher training** — The term-time repetition of the staff training, provided because the skills decay and the staff change.
- **Fidelity check** — The independent observation of whether the intervention was delivered as specified, which converts an unexplained null result into a diagnosable implementation problem.
- **Contingency inventory** — The assessment of what currently follows a behaviour in a setting, for the young person, the peers, and the adults.
- **Class contract** — A term-long written agreement between a class and the school, drafted with the students, in return for specified privileges.
- **Ritualised response** — A predictable adult response to a class-wide behaviour that functions as a signal of significance rather than as a consequence.
- **Function-related intervention team (FRIT)** — A classroom-level system in which a team of staff share the function of a behaviour and respond consistently, increasing the number of adults available to reinforce.
- **Manifestation determination** — The procedural requirement, under disability legislation, that a behaviour resulting in a change of placement be assessed against the possibility that it is a manifestation of a disability.
- **Functional behavioural assessment (FBA)** — The assessment required in many jurisdictions before a change of placement for a student with a disability, and the appropriate basis for a behaviour support plan.
- **Placement change** — A change in the educational setting of a student that triggers the procedural protections of the disability legislation in most jurisdictions.
- **Behavioural contract for a class** — An agreement between a class and the school in which the class commits to specified behaviours in return for specified privileges.
- **Special educational needs and disability code** — The jurisdictional framework that determines the legal status of a behaviour support plan and the procedures that must be followed.
- **Instrumental participation** — A young person's continued involvement in an intervention because it produces a benefit, without the corresponding development; the risk of an unfaded system.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Contingency management in schools and clinics is the most direct application of the operant principles of Chapter 26 to real settings, and its central difficulty is not technical. A school is an organisation of many adults, each of whom responds to a young person's behaviour, and a contingency system is only as strong as its weakest response. The failure mode is predictable: a programme is designed with the class teacher, the teaching assistant is not told, the supply teacher is not told, and on the first day the young person learns that the system holds on Tuesday and not on Wednesday.

The chapter's first idea is that every setting is already a contingency system, and that the question is never whether to introduce consequences but whether the existing ones serve the young person. The contingency inventory is the assessment tool, and its three common findings are that the problem behaviour is reinforced, that the desired behaviour is unreinforced, and that the adults are inconsistent.

The conditions for appropriateness were stated and the conditions for inappropriateness were stated with them, and the special caution about young people with severe cognitive impairment — for whom the tokens must be understood and the fade correspondingly slower — was included. The token economy was described in its six design decisions and its four implementation requirements, and the class-wide and individual systems were contrasted, with the hybrid recommended. The problems specific to token economies were set out: the contamination of the target behaviour, the peer problem, the staff problem, the incompatibility of token delivery with exclusionary responses, and the risk of promising a reward the school cannot deliver.

Behaviour support plans were described in their elements, and the escalation procedure was given the attention it deserves, because an adult's improvised response to a young person's escalation is the treatment, and because the most common errors — removal without a return condition, variable duration, and return on calming — all inadvertently reinforce the escalation. The chapter noted the legal framework that gives the plan a standing beyond the clinical one, and the practitioner is directed to establish the local regulatory position before writing a plan.

The case of Mr Adeyemi's class illustrated the whole chapter. The referral described three students; the analysis of the data described a single Tuesday afternoon lesson and a thirty-minute unsupervised activity; the functional analysis described a ritualised adult response that functioned as a signal of significance, and the plan that changed the adult's behaviour changed the class. The class contract was, in the end, the smallest part of the intervention.

The implementation section addressed the fidelity problem, the adult who is not on the plan, staff turnover, the young person who is not in the room, and the young person who declines, and the section on evidence noted that the school-based literature is mature and proximal, that fidelity determines the outcome, that training alone is insufficient, and that the classroom-level systems have larger effects than the individual ones because they change the peer ecology.

The ethical analysis in a school setting is sharper than elsewhere because a school is an institution and the peers can see, and the four prohibitions were stated without qualification: never trade a right, never trade attention or warmth, never make the system public if it would humiliate, and do not let the system replace the relationship. The cultural analysis addressed the community-specificity of the available reinforcers, the cultural patterning of public correction, the peer group's valuing of defiance, and the jurisdictional specificity of the discipline framework. The ethical requirements closed the chapter with the question that every review should contain: is this plan still necessary?

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 28.1 — The Contingency Inventory (90 minutes, field-based)

In a placement, choose one behaviour that concerns the adults in the setting but that the referral describes in general terms. Complete the contingency inventory for it: what happens immediately before, what happens immediately after, for the young person, the peers, and the adults, and what the young person's alternative produces. Write the inventory in one page. Then write the single sentence stating what the behaviour appears to accomplish. Most students find that the behaviour is being reinforced by something nobody intended, and the finding is almost always more actionable than the behaviour.

### Activity 28.2 — Designing a Token Economy (90 minutes, individual)

Design a classroom token economy for a real secondary class. Specify the three target behaviours, the token, the backup reinforcers, the schedule, the exchange procedure, the response cost, and the fade plan. Then write the protocol as it would appear on the desk, in no more than twenty lines. The twenty-line constraint is the discipline, and most first drafts are twice as long as they should be and cannot be used at the front of a room.

### Activity 28.3 — The Fidelity Simulation (60 minutes, small group)

In groups of four, one student plays the teaching assistant, one the subject teacher, one the young person, and one the practitioner observing. The practitioner describes a plan and the two staff members respond to a simulated disruption in the way they think appropriate, without further instruction. The practitioner then checks the response against the written protocol. The exercise is uncomfortable and it is the most accurate simulation of the implementation problem that the book contains, and it consistently shows that the discrepancies are not in the staff's motivation but in their knowledge of what the plan requires.

### Activity 28.4 — Redressing a Plan (60 minutes, individual)

Take a behaviour support plan from a placement, or construct one, and audit it against five questions: does it condition any right on behaviour; does it use adult attention or approval as a reward; is it visible to peers in a way that would humiliate a particular young person; does it specify what happens with the adults who are not on the plan; and does it have a stated end? For each failure, write the revision. Most plans fail the first three, and the revisions are the practical work of the chapter.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the design.** Read Azrin and Lanza (1976) for the token economy, and read the classroom-level systems literature for the function-related intervention team and its relatives. The classroom-level material is the most direct guide to the class-wide approach and it has the strongest effect sizes.

**For the implementation.** Read the fidelity literature, and read one study that measured implementation and reported a null result. The contrast with a study that measured implementation and reported a large effect is the most useful pair of readings in the chapter.

**For the law.** Read the local behaviour and disability framework before writing any plan, and read the manifestation determination guidance. This is not optional reading and it takes an afternoon.

**For the evidence.** Read the school-based reviews and the classroom-level meta-analyses, and read the substance use contingency management literature, which is the most methodologically mature part of the applied evidence base.

**For the ethics.** Read the critical literature on reward-based practice in schools and on the historical use of token economies in institutions, and read the disability rights literature on the protections of students with behaviour difficulties. The combination of the technical and the critical literature is the preparation for writing a plan that a school will use and a family will accept.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is token systems, behaviour contracts, and the negotiated restructuring of reinforcement in real settings. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is functional analysis, learning history, contingencies, mastery, and measurable behaviour change. The mechanism to keep in view is how antecedents, consequences, skills, avoidance, and environmental design select and maintain behaviour. The characteristic professional risk is using control without assent, reinforcing compliance rather than wellbeing, or ignoring the meaning and function of behaviour. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |

Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in classrooms, homes, clinics, and other real environments where behaviour occurs, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Tunde is fifteen and is seen in a community adolescent clinic for grief following a major family change. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Tunde's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Tunde explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on functional analysis, learning history, contingencies, mastery, and measurable behaviour change.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Tunde chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Tunde has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Tunde can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Contingency Management in Schools and Clinics to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
