## 7. Schema Therapy with Children and Adolescents

### 7.1 The developmental fit

The schema theory is developmentally congruent in a way that is unusual among the characterological therapies, and there are three specific reasons.

**The schemas are by definition developmental.** A young person with a schema of Defectiveness/Shame has a schema that was formed when the young person's worth was contingent on performance, and the therapist's task is to identify when and how that happened. The theory's insistence on the childhood antecedents is a developmental insistence, and it means that the therapist is doing developmental work by doing schema work.

**The mode model captures the adolescent's reality.** The adolescent's experience of being in one state and then another — the enthusiastic, engaged, hopeful adolescent on Monday and the ashamed, self-critical, self-hating adolescent on Thursday — is precisely what the mode model describes, and naming it is often the first thing that makes the pattern visible to the young person and to their family.

**The developmental task is explicitly in the model.** The unmet needs that the schemas reflect — for a secure base, for autonomy, for freedom to express emotion, for realistic limits, for acceptance of the self — are the developmental needs, and the theory's reparenting specification is a bounded provision of what was not provided.

### 7.2 The developmental modifications

**The developmental task of the therapeutic relationship.** The adult specification of limited reparenting has to be modified in two ways with a young person. First, the boundary is the boundary: a young person who has been harmed by an adult who was supposed to care for them will test the therapist's reliability, and the therapist's consistency is the treatment. Second, the developmental literature on the adolescent's attachment renegotiation is relevant: the young person is in the process of moving from dependence to autonomy, and a therapist who provides too much warmth and too little challenge will be experienced as another adult to be managed.

**The use of the child version.** The imagery rescripting's use of the "child version" of the young person is developmentally appropriate and powerful, and it requires care. The work is not to regress the young person; it is to give them access to a part of their own experience that has been inaccessible, and the distinction must be maintained throughout.

**The mode of the presenting problem.** A young person who presents in an angry child mode is often, in the first instance, in a system in which the anger is the only available expression. The schema therapist's task with a young person is to get to the vulnerable mode, which is where the unmet need is, and this requires that the young person be able to trust that the vulnerability will be survivable.

**The parent's role.** The parent is usually a significant part of the schema's maintenance, and the theory's stance is that the parent's own schema and modes are part of the picture. The work with the parent is a sub-field in its own right, and the ethical care described in Section 10 applies.

**The specificity of the developmental adaptations.** The research on the childhood and adolescent applications of schema therapy is considerably thinner than the adult research, and the adaptations have been made in practice more often than they have been evaluated. A student should treat the developmental adaptations as clinical judgement informed by the theory, not as an evidence-based protocol.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

**The evidence base is substantial and growing, and it is stronger for the adult treatments than for the developmental applications.**

**Personality disorders.** The schema-focused treatments for borderline personality disorder and for avoidant personality disorder have been evaluated in a series of randomised controlled trials, with substantial effects on both the symptoms and the schema-related constructs, and the evidence is among the more convincing in the personality disorder literature. The larger and better-powered trials have been conducted in adults.

**The construct validity of the schemas.** The question at the centre of the Beck–Young dispute has been answered substantially in favour of Young. The self-report and clinician-rated measures of the schema constructs have demonstrated good reliability and convergent, discriminant, and construct validity across a substantial international literature. The schemas have been shown to be predictive of treatment outcome, to be associated with a range of psychopathology, to be stable over time, and to be culturally modulated.

**The outcomes.** Meta-analyses of schema therapy across conditions report moderate to large effects, and the therapy has been evaluated in depression, anxiety, personality disorder, eating disorder, chronic pain, and other conditions. The comparison with standard cognitive therapy in several trials has shown differences in some measures and not in others, and the honest summary is that schema therapy adds value in the characterological presentations and is broadly comparable in others.

**The developmental applications.** The evidence for schema therapy with children and adolescents specifically is thin. A small number of studies, mostly uncontrolled or with small controlled samples, have been conducted with adolescents with eating disorders, with self-harm, and with personality disorder features, and the results are promising enough to justify further work. The developmental adaptations, including the specific work with the child version and the modifications to the chair work, are described in the clinical literature and have not been systematically evaluated.

**The limits.** The allegiance problem persists, and a substantial proportion of the studies have been conducted by investigators with an allegiance to the theory. The constructs are numerous, and the theoretical breadth of the model creates a risk of unfalsifiability that Beck's original objection identified, and which the validation research has substantially but not completely addressed.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Schema Therapy

**Strengths.** It addresses the characterological level, which the standard cognitive therapies do not reach, and it does so with a model that is unusually precise about the developmental origins of a young person's difficulties. The mode model gives a young person an immediately usable description of their own experience, and the maps are often the turning point in the work. The techniques — imagery rescripting, empathic confrontation, chair work, dialogue work — are well specified and are among the most usable in the field. The theory is compatible with the developmental literature, with the attachment research, and with the emotion-focused and somatic traditions, and the integration is productive rather than merely fashionable. And the emphasis on the unmet need behind the difficulty is a humane and clinically useful orientation that is often absent from the other characterological approaches.

**Limitations.** The developmental evidence is thin, and the adult evidence is not transferable without modification. The theory's constructs are numerous and the risk of unfalsifiability is real, and a student who uses the theory loosely will produce an unfalsifiable and unhelpfully general account of a young person. The reparenting specification is ethically delicate and requires careful handling, and a practitioner who is unclear about the limits will do harm. And the model's breadth, which is its intellectual strength, is also its practical weakness, because a young person with a simple and clear difficulty can be over-formulated.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 Culture and the schemas

**The universality of the schemas.** The schemas are presented as universal, and the research has found substantial cross-cultural consistency in the factor structure of the schema measures, which is a genuine finding. The consistency is nevertheless at the level of the schema categories, and it does not mean that the *content* of a young person's schemas is the same across cultures. A schema of Unrelenting Standards in a culture with a strong achievement ethic may be considerably less maladaptive than in a culture where the same standard is unusual, and the developmental antecedents of a schema of Emotional Deprivation will look different in a family that depends on extended kin for childcare than in a nuclear family.

**The cultural specification of the reparenting.** The theory's specification of the therapist's role assumes a particular form of emotional availability and a particular form of limit-setting. In a cultural context in which the expression of need is understood to be a sign of immaturity, or in which the authority of the professional is not questioned, or in which the family is the primary unit of emotional life, the reparenting may be experienced as intrusion rather than as provision, and the developmental work with the parents is correspondingly more important.

**The risk of pathologising the family.** A young person who has been brought up in a family with high standards and strong expectations may be diagnosed with the Unrelenting Standards and the Subjugation schemas, and the diagnosis may be accurate in a technical sense while being profoundly misapplied, because the standards may be the young person's primary source of belonging. The ethical requirement is that the therapist ask what the schema is *for* before treating it, which is the same requirement that governs the work with the person-centred and the systemic traditions.

### 10.2 The ethical requirement of the bounded reparenting

The reparenting is the element of the theory that most requires ethical specification, and the specification is the following.

**The therapist provides warmth, empathy, validation, and unconditional positive regard.** This is a therapeutic stance, and it is a legitimate one.

**The therapist sets clear, consistent, kindly limits.** The limits are the boundary of the relationship, and they include the practical ones — the time, the frequency, the setting, the availability — and the limits on the young person's use of the relationship.

**The therapist does not become a parent substitute.** The relationship is a therapeutic relationship with a specific function, and the young person's developmental task is to become an autonomous adult, not to acquire a second parent. A practitioner who is unclear about this will find a young person who becomes dependent, and who has been harmed by a therapy that was supposed to help.

**The developmental task of the relationship.** The theory's account of the developmental needs behind the schemas implies that the therapeutic relationship should itself be developmentally appropriate, and that means that as the young person develops, the therapist's role should change, and that the eventual ending of the relationship is a developmental event and not merely an administrative one. Chapter 54 develops this.

**The value of the approach.** The ethical value of the theory is also considerable, and it is worth stating. It is one of the few approaches in the field that is explicitly concerned with the young person's developmental history, that takes the early relationship seriously, and that gives a young person a way of understanding the difficulties that they have carried since childhood — which is, for many young people, the most relieving thing that has ever happened to them.

## 11. Case Illustration: The Girl Who Was Two Different People

**The referral.** Ines, fifteen, is referred by a child and adolescent mental health service for "self-harm, school refusal, and possible borderline." The referral notes that Ines has cut for eighteen months, that she has been in three schools in two years, and that she "swings between being delightful and being abusive." Her parents are separated; her mother has a new partner; Ines sees her father every other weekend.

**The assessment.** The schema assessment establishes the following.

**The primary schema: Abandonment/Instability.** The developmental antecedents are the father's departures, the moves, and the fact that each departure has been followed by a return and by another departure, with the return never quite reliable. The unmet need is for a secure base and for stability.

**The second schema: Defectiveness/Shame.** The antecedents include the mother's emotional reliance on Ines, which began when Ines was nine and her father left, and a family in which Ines's emotional responsiveness was needed by adults. The unmet need is for unconditional acceptance and for permission to have needs of her own.

**The third schema: Emotional Deprivation.** The antecedents are the emotional structure of the household. The unmet need is for emotional warmth and for a sense of being wanted for oneself.

**The coping style: overcompensation, with a strong avoidance component.** Ines is hyper-competent and hyper-control with some adults — the "delightful" mode — and avoidant of others, particularly of the school. Her self-harm occurs in the context of the abandonment schema being activated.

**The mode map.** The "delightful" Ines is a demanding parent mode — the internalised "you must be perfect and you must not need anyone" voice, which is also her mother's voice. The "abusive" Ines is an angry child mode. The vulnerable, sad, desperate Ines is the vulnerable child mode and is the one the young person almost never shows. The healthy adult mode is small and undeveloped, and the practitioner describes it as "a nine-year-old who is very sensible."

**The therapeutic work.** The work proceeds in a specific order, and the order is the clinical skill in this theory.

**First, the map.** The map is drawn with Ines, over two sessions, and the drawing is the intervention. Ines says, looking at it: "That's what I do. I go nice or I go mad and there's nothing in between." The practitioner says: "What's in between?" And Ines finds, with some difficulty, the vulnerable child mode on the map, and says: "That one doesn't get to come out."

**Second, the empathic confrontation.** The confrontation is between Ines's own two positions, and it is done gently. "You've said you want to be completely independent of everybody, and you've also said that if you actually did that, you'd disappear. Both of those are true at the same time, and I want to show you something — which part of you is the one that says 'I must be perfect'?" The answer is revealing: "That's my mum's voice. It's her in my head." And then: "And who is it that is trying to be so good that she never has to need anybody?" And Ines, after a long silence: "Me. At nine."

**Third, the imagery rescripting.** Ines is asked to imagine the scene at nine: her mother crying in the kitchen, Ines coming in to see what was wrong. The scene is familiar and the affect is immediate. The rescripting asks: "What did you need from her that night? What would you have liked her to have said?" Ines: "I wanted her to say 'go to bed, I'll be all right.'" The practitioner asks her to imagine her nine-year-old self hearing that. It takes several sessions, and the practitioner is careful about the pace and about the fact that the nine-year-old needs the experience of a caregiver who is available, and that the imagery is providing it.

**Fourth, the work with the mother.** The mother's own schema and modes are the core of the maintenance, and the mother's "you must be strong" is the demanding parent mode that Ines has internalised. The work with the mother is the hardest part of the case, and the practitioner is explicit that the goal is not to make the mother a different person but to give her something other than her daughter to rely on. The practitioner also raises the question of the father's contact, and Ines's own ambivalence about it, which is complicated by the abandonment schema itself.

**The outcome.** Over nine months, the self-harm reduces to two episodes and then stops; Ines returns to school and stays; and the "delightful" mode becomes less rigid, so that she is occasionally awkward and occasionally disappointing and does not experience this as a catastrophe. The vulnerable child mode is more accessible. The practitioner records that the abandonment schema is not resolved and is not expected to be, and that the work is now about the young person's capacity to tolerate it.

**The teaching point.** The central intervention was not a technique but a map, and the map was made with the young person rather than about her. The order — the map, the confrontation, the rescripting, the parental work — was determined by the theory's account of what maintains the schema, and every element of the order was a decision to work with the protection rather than against it. The case also illustrates the theory's characteristic ethical delicacy, because the mother was doing the very thing that had been done to Ines, and the practitioner had to work with the mother without endorsing what she was doing.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Schema therapy** — A therapy developed by Jeffrey Young that addresses early maladaptive schemas and the modes through which they are enacted, and that is especially suited to characterological and developmental difficulties.
- **Early maladaptive schema** — A pattern of experience and affect, concerning the self and others, formed in childhood and enduring across the lifespan, that is a basis for a wide range of later difficulties.
- **Domains** — The five groups into which the eighteen schemas are organised: disconnection and rejection; impaired autonomy and performance; impaired limits; other-directedness; overvigilance and inhibition.
- **Coping styles** — The three characteristic responses to a schema: surrender, avoidance, and overcompensation.
- **Surrender** — The coping style in which the young person allows the schema to be enacted and thereby confirms it.
- **Avoidance** — The coping style in which the young person avoids the situations in which the schema would be activated.
- **Overcompensation** — The coping style in which the young person does the opposite of the schema in an exaggerated and rigid way.
- **Schema coping cycle** — The sequence in which the schema is activated, the coping style produces short-term benefit, and the long-term consequences confirm the schema.
- **Mode** — A distinct psychological state with its own feelings, thoughts, and behaviours, organised around a particular need or aim.
- **Vulnerable child mode** — The mode in which the young person feels small, sad, scared, ashamed, or helpless, and in which their unmet needs are felt.
- **Angry child mode** — The mode in which the young person feels anger, resentment, or defiance in response to a perceived violation of their needs; a common mode of presentation.
- **Punitive parent mode** — The internalised critical, blaming, and demanding authority; the source of the young person's harsh self-criticism.
- **Demanding parent mode** — The internalised standards that drive performance and prevent the experience of vulnerability.
- **Healthy adult mode** — The mode from which the therapy is conducted; the target of the developmental work.
- **Reparenting** — The bounded provision, within the therapeutic relationship, of the empathy, validation, and unconditional regard that the young person did not receive in childhood.
- **Limited reparenting** — The specification of the therapeutic relationship as the bounded provision of what was missing, within clear and consistent limits.
- **The perfect parent model** — The specification of the therapist's stance as warm and validating but also consistently limiting.
- **Empathic confrontation** — The technique of bringing the young person's own conflicting positions into direct contact in a supportive and non-blaming way.
- **Imagery rescripting** — The technique of imagining the original scene and then imagining a version in which the unmet need is met.
- **Chair work** — The technique of a dialogue between the young person and a part of themselves seated in an empty chair.
- **Dialogue work** — The more structured and cognitively oriented version of chair work, in which the conflicting voices are written out and restated.
- **Schema map** — The formulation specifying the schemas, their coping styles, their antecedents, their unmet needs, their triggers, and their consequences.
- **Mode map** — The formulation specifying the modes, their triggers, their triggers, their internal dialogue, and the conditions under which each is entered.
- **Characterological problems** — The pervasive, developmental, and rigid difficulties that the standard cognitive techniques do not reach, and that motivated the development of schema therapy.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Schema therapy was developed by Jeffrey Young out of the finding, documented in Beck's own research programme, that a substantial proportion of patients with characterological difficulties do not respond to standard cognitive therapy. The theory proposes that these difficulties are maintained by early maladaptive schemas — patterns of experience and affect, concerning the self and others, formed in childhood and enduring across the lifespan — that the young person responds to these schemas with one of three coping styles, and that their experience is organised in modes, of which the Healthy Adult is the one from which the therapy is conducted.

The schemas are organised into five domains, and the eighteen schemas are reproduced in Appendix C with their developmental antecedents and their unmet needs. The coping styles — surrender, avoidance, and overcompensation — maintain the schemas through the schema coping cycle, and the clinical implication is that the work is done with the coping style rather than against the schema. The mode model provides a description of the young person's shifting states that is immediately usable with a young person and with their family.

The techniques — limited reparenting, empathic confrontation, imagery rescripting, chair work, and dialogue work — are well specified, and the reparenting specification includes the limits that protect the young person and the therapist. The theory is unusually developmentally congruent, because the schemas are developmental by definition and the mode model captures the adolescent's experience of shifting between states.

The evidence is substantial for the adult treatments and thin for the developmental applications, and the construct validation research has substantially answered Beck's original objection. The ethical requirements concern the bounded reparenting, the danger of pathologising a family's values, and the requirement that the therapist ask what a schema is *for* before treating it. The case of Ines shows the sequence in action: the map made with the young person, the empathic confrontation, the imagery rescripting, and the difficult work with the mother whose own demand had been transmitted intact.

## 14. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. State Beck's research finding that motivated the development of schema therapy.
2. Define an early maladaptive schema and explain the significance of each of the five components of the definition.
3. Describe the five domains and give two examples of schemas from each.
4. Explain the three coping styles and the schema coping cycle, and state the clinical implication of the cycle.
5. Distinguish the vulnerable child mode, the angry child mode, the punitive parent mode, the demanding parent mode, and the healthy adult mode.
6. What is limited reparenting, and what are its four components and its limits?
7. Describe imagery rescripting and state its four cautions.
8. Explain why the schema theory is developmentally congruent, and identify the three specific reasons.
9. What does the schema and mode map do that a cognitive conceptualisation does not?
10. State the ethical requirement about the purpose of a schema before treating it, and explain the risk it addresses.
:::

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 19.1 — The Mode Map (2 hours, individual, then pairs)

Choose a young person you know — a client, a placement student, a relative — and map their modes. Identify the vulnerable mode, the angry mode, the demanding or punitive parent mode, the coping modes, and the healthy adult mode. Then write the developmental antecedents of the primary schema and the unmet need. Then, in a second pass, write what the young person would need in order for the vulnerable mode to be more accessible. Bring the map to the seminar. The exercise is difficult and the second pass is the part that teaches.

### Activity 19.2 — The Imagery Rescripting Rehearsal (90 minutes, triads)

One student offers a childhood scene from their own life; one conducts a rescripting; one observes for the moment at which the young person's affect changes and for the point at which the practitioner moves too fast. The observer's task is also to note the difference between a rescripting and a re-enactment, because the two are easily confused and the second is a mistake.

### Activity 19.3 — The Purpose of a Schema (60 minutes, written, then pairs)

Take a schema that you or someone you know holds — Unrelenting Standards, Subjugation, Emotional Deprivation — and write, in 300 words, what the schema is *for* in that person's life. Then write what the person would have to give up in order to change it, and what they would have to have instead. The exercise is the ethical core of the theory and it is a direct preparation for the cultural work in Chapter 48.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model.** Read Young (1990) for the founding of the schema concept, and Young, Klosko, and Weisbarth (2003) for the coping styles and the modes, which is the text a practitioner will actually use.

**For the evidence.** Read the schema-focused personality disorder trials and the meta-analyses, and then read the validation studies of the schema constructs. The history of the dispute with Beck is best understood from the primary literature, and it is one of the more instructive episodes in the profession's history.

**For the developmental applications.** Read the clinical literature on the childhood and adolescent applications with attention to the adaptations and their developmental rationale, and note how much of it is case-based.

**For the related traditions.** Read the object relations literature, the developmental literature on attachment and internal working models, and the emotion-focused literature. The schema theory sits at the intersection of all three, and a student who knows the three will find the theory considerably more coherent.
