# Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy and Mindfulness-Based Approaches

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Describe the origin of mindfulness-based stress reduction in the medical clinic and the origin of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy in the prevention of depressive relapse.
- Explain the account of the mechanisms of mindfulness, including the decentering and the reperceiving processes.
- Describe the eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction course and the eight-week mindfulness-based cognitive therapy course.
- Describe the practices that are common to the mindfulness-based approaches and the practices that are specific to each.
- Apply mindfulness-based work with children and adolescents, and specify the developmental modifications.
- Evaluate the evidence for the mindfulness-based approaches with young people.
- Critically evaluate the mindfulness-based approaches, including their cultural assumptions and their relationship to contemplative traditions.

## Chapter Overview

Mindfulness is the oldest technique in this book and the newest in the profession. It has been practised for some thousands of years in a variety of contemplative traditions, and it entered clinical psychology in the late 1970s in an unexpected place: the stress reduction clinic at the Massachusetts General Hospital, where Jon Kabat-Zinn was asked to develop a programme for patients with chronic pain and stress-related conditions that the medical system could not otherwise help.

The clinical proposition is simple and was for a long time unfashionable: that a person who is systematically trained to attend to the present moment, with a particular quality of attention, will be less at the mercy of the reactivity that maintains distress. The mechanism the tradition proposes is the development of **decentering** — the capacity to observe one's own thoughts and feelings as events occurring in the mind, rather than as facts about the world that require a response.

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy was developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale in the 1990s, and it is a specific integration of mindfulness practice with the cognitive model, designed for one purpose: the prevention of depressive relapse. The trial that established it was a well-conducted randomised controlled trial, and it is one of the very few studies in the field that produced a large effect on a clinically important outcome.

This chapter presents the theory, the mechanisms, the courses, the developmental adaptations, the evidence, and — because the question is unavoidable in a graduate course — the cultural questions that the mindfulness literature has engaged with least adequately.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The medical clinic

Kabat-Zinn's patients had chronic pain, anxiety, and stress-related conditions, and the medical options had been exhausted. He had trained as a molecular biologist and had undertaken extensive study of Zen practice, and his clinical proposition was that the patients' difficulties were maintained by their relationship to their own symptoms — by the struggle, the fear, the catastrophising, and the avoidance that the symptoms generated — and that training in a different relationship to the symptoms would be more useful than any further attempt at symptom removal.

The programme he developed, the mindfulness-based stress reduction course, is described in detail in Section 3. The key feature was that it did not require the patient to be in a state of distress, or to have a particular diagnosis, or to believe in anything. It required eight weeks, two and a half hours a week, and the practice of a defined set of exercises.

### 1.2 The relapse prevention trial

The collaboration between Segal, Williams, and Teasdale at the Oxford Centre for Cognitive Therapy had two features. The first was a programme of mindfulness practice that had been adapted for depressed patients, who have particular difficulty with the practice and who tend to find the present moment painful. The second was the theoretical and clinical argument that the mechanism of depressive relapse is a characteristic pattern of thinking — the reactivation of depressive schemas, and the associated rumination and the reactivation of a general negative thinking style — and that the interval between episodes is the time in which the treatment should be delivered, because it is the time at which the patient's mind is most vulnerable to the reactivation of the old patterns and most able to learn the new ones.

The trial, published in 1990 and replicated several times, compared mindfulness-based cognitive therapy with the continuation of the maintenance antidepressant medication on which the patients were stable, and found that the patients who received mindfulness-based cognitive therapy relapsed at a substantially lower rate over the follow-up period. The subsequent research literature has substantially replicated the finding, with the meta-analyses reporting a substantial effect on relapse rates, and the guidelines in several countries have recommended mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for relapse prevention.

### 1.3 The spread

The 2000s saw the diffusion of the mindfulness-based approaches through the profession, and the diffusion has been remarkable. The methods were applied to stress, to anxiety, to depression, to chronic pain, to cancer, to parenting, to education, to the workplace, to sports, and to the criminal justice system, and the number of published studies grew very rapidly.

The speed of the diffusion has outrun the research, and the field now contains a very large number of small studies of mindfulness-based interventions with heterogeneous protocols, heterogeneous measures, and a substantial risk of publication bias. A student who is asked to evaluate the mindfulness evidence base should be considerably more cautious than the popular accounts suggest.

### 1.4 The contemplative connection

The clinical approaches are not the first application of mindfulness in the West, and a student should know the history. William James's *Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) and his chapter on the "stream of thought" and on the "lens" of attention, William Brewer's work, and the study of the contemplative practices of Buddhism and of the Christian contemplative tradition all precede the clinical work, and the contemporary field is now in a complex relationship with these traditions, which is addressed in Section 10.

## 2. The Mechanisms of Mindfulness

### 2.1 The attentional framework

The standard definition of mindfulness is the one developed by the contemporary researchers, and it has two components: attention and orientation.

**Attention** is the deliberate focus on the present moment, with a quality of attention that is non-judgemental. The non-judgemental quality is the operative part, and it is the part that distinguishes mindfulness from ordinary concentration.

**Orientation** is the recognition that the experience of the present moment is itself the object of attention, rather than the object of attention being the content of the present moment. In the ordinary state, the mind is attending to the present moment while the "self" is located in the past and the future — in the recollection of what has happened, the anticipation of what will happen, and the conceptual commentary on both. Mindfulness involves a shift in the orientation of the self toward the present moment.

The theory's claim is that this shift is the mechanism of change, and that it is a change in what the theory calls the **reperceiving** capacity, and that the reperceiving capacity is a trainable skill.

### 2.2 Decentering

**Decentering** is the capacity to see one's thoughts and feelings as events occurring in the mind, rather than as facts about the world, or as accurate summaries of oneself. It is a concept borrowed from the cognitive therapy tradition, where it appears as a technique for holding a thought as a thought, and the mindfulness tradition adopts it as a mechanism.

The two components of decentering are: the ability to perceive a thought or feeling as a mental event rather than as a fact; and the ability to reduce the believability or the impact of that thought or feeling, in the sense of the capacity to step back from it.

The contrast with fusion is the key to the whole theory. In fusion, the thought *is* the reality: "I am anxious" and "anxiety is a fact about my situation" are both experienced as true, and the behaviour is governed accordingly. In decentering, the thought is experienced as a thought: "I am having the thought that I am anxious, and I notice that my chest is tight, and I notice that the thought is about a meeting that is on Thursday."

### 2.3 The eight mechanisms

The contemporary research literature has identified a set of mechanisms that are consistently associated with mindfulness practice, and they are worth knowing because they are what the evidence actually supports, as distinct from what the popular accounts claim.

**Attentional control** — the improved ability to shift and sustain attention, which is the foundation of everything else.

**Attentional awareness** — the ability to notice the contents of experience, which is the mechanism of the early stages of the practice.

**Decentering** — as described.

**Reperceiving** — the capacity to see a situation from a range of perspectives, including the perspective of the wider system and of one's own history.

**Reduction of reactivity** — the reduced tendency to react to stimuli in a conditioned way, which is the behavioural mechanism.

**Response flexibility** — the ability to respond in a way that is appropriate to the situation rather than habitual.

**Mindfulness with acceptance** — the capacity to be present with an unpleasant experience without either suppressing it or reacting to it.

**Values-based action** — the capacity to act in accordance with values, which is the outcome of the practice rather than a mechanism of it.

The components of the mechanisms that matter for work with young people are **decentering** and **reperceiving**, because these are the ones that require a developed metacognitive capacity, and the developmental literature in Chapter 9 is directly relevant to the question of when a young person can learn them.

## 3. The Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction Course

### 3.1 The eight weeks

The course structure is standard and a student should know it, because the structure is part of the intervention.

**Week one: the body scan.** A 45-minute practice of attending to the body from the feet to the head, and the didactic content is an explanation of mindfulness and of the stress response, and the establishment of the practice of the formal and the informal practice. The body scan is the practice most often used with young people, and it is also the practice that the somatic and trauma-informed fields have adopted.

**Week two: the body scan and the "tuning in" practice.** The didactic content is the exploration of the relationship between thoughts, feelings, and behaviour.

**Week three: mindful movement.** A practice of gentle, deliberate movement — walking, stretching, or yoga — with the attention on the sensations. The didactic content is the development of the observational stance and the demonstration of the difference between reacting and responding.

**Week four: sitting meditation.** The formal sitting practice is introduced, with the attention on the breath and on the arising and passing of thoughts. The didactic content is the development of the distinction between the sensations and the thoughts about the sensations.

**Week five: the body, the breath, and the environment.** The didactic content is the exploration of the interrelationship of the body, the breathing, the thoughts, and the feelings, and the beginning of the exploration of the obstacles to practice.

**Week six: the "choiceless" awareness.** The focus is on the observation of the internal and external world without the addition of meaning. The didactic content is the development of the capacity to tolerate discomfort and the examination of the patterns of reacting.

**Week seven: the development of a practice that suits the individual.** The didactic content is the identification and examination of the patterns that are most and least helpful, and the refinement of the individual practice.

**Week eight: the consolidation of the practice and the plan for the future.** The didactic content is the consolidation of what has been learned and the development of a plan for the continuation of the practice.

### 3.2 The practices

The formal practices are the body scan, the sitting meditation, and the mindful movement. The informal practices are the practice of bringing the attentional quality of mindfulness into the ordinary activities of the day — the "bring it back" practice, the practice of noticing the arising of thoughts and returning the attention to the body.

### 3.3 The didactics

The didactic content of the course is not decorative. It includes the stress response, the relaxation response, the reframing of the patterns of thought, the "decentering" and the "reperceiving" as a subject, and the examination of the patterns of relating to oneself and to others.

## 4. Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy

### 4.1 The structure

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy is an eight-week group programme, and its structure follows the stress reduction course with a number of modifications, most of which are diagnostic in character.

**Session one** adds an explicit formulation: the young person and the group identify their own pattern of depressive relapse — the situation in which the relapse is likely to occur, the thoughts that accompany it, the feelings, the behaviours, and the consequences. The formulation becomes the working document of the eight weeks.

**Sessions two to five** follow the stress reduction structure, with the addition of the cognitive elements in the didactic content.

**Session six** focuses explicitly on the depressive thoughts and on the recognition of the signs of relapse, and it is the session that most directly reflects the cognitive therapy inheritance.

**Sessions seven and eight** consolidate the practice and, crucially, develop a detailed **relapse prevention plan**: the personal warning signs, the internal coping strategies, the external coping strategies, the situations in which the mood is vulnerable, and the plan for the seeking of help. The relapse prevention plan is one of the most practically useful clinical documents in the entire field, and Appendix F provides the template.

### 4.2 The adaptation for depression

The adaptations of the standard mindfulness practice for depressed patients are specific and important.

The **body scan** in a depressed patient is often difficult, because the body sensations are unpleasant and the depressed patient has a strongly negative appraisal of bodily experience. The adaptation is to shorten it, to work with a narrower focus, and to be prepared for a great deal of resistance.

The **sitting practice** in a depressed patient is often experienced as intolerable, because the mind is highly active and the content is heavily negative. The adaptation is to work with a very brief practice, to anchor attention in a bodily location, and to accept the disturbance.

The **mindful movement** in a depressed patient is often the most accessible of the practices, and it is frequently the entry point.

The **relapse prevention plan** is the diagnostic heart of the therapy, and it is the element that the research has most clearly identified as important.

## 5. Mindfulness-Based Approaches with Children and Adolescents

### 5.1 The developmental fit

The developmental fit of the mindfulness-based approaches with young people is good, and the reasons are specific.

**The attentional focus is developmentally congruent.** The practices require the young person to attend to the present moment, which is precisely the developmental task that the adolescent is working on, and the evidence from the contemplative psychology literature is that the development of the attentional and metacognitive capacities that the practice requires continues well into the third decade.

**The practice is developmentally consistent with the somatic work.** The body scan, which is the practice most used with young people, connects directly to the somatic and the trauma-informed literatures, and the developmental research on the adolescent brain and on the body's representation of emotion is the same research that the somatic fields use.

**It does not require the young person to talk about their difficulties.** A practice that consists of sitting quietly and attending to the breath is accessible to a young person who cannot or will not describe what is wrong, and this is a considerable advantage with the young people who most need help.

**The relationship to the developmental task of self-regulation.** The developmental literature describes the adolescent's self-regulation as immature in the presence of arousal, and the practice is, in effect, a structured training in the regulation of attention and of the response to arousal.

### 5.2 The developmental modifications

**The duration of the practice.** The standard practices are 30 to 45 minutes, and this is far too long for a young person. The developmental adaptation is brief practices of 5 to 10 minutes, and the "stop, take a breath, notice what is happening" informal practice, which can be practised in a school corridor in ninety seconds.

**The language.** The didactic content must be translated into the young person's own terms, and the abstraction of the standard material — the stress response, the relaxation response, the reperceiving — is generally too much for a young person. The practice can be taught without the theory, and for a young person the practice is usually sufficient.

**The developmental modification of the body scan.** The body scan with a young person can be done standing, in a chair, at a desk, or in a very short form. It can be done with the eyes open, which many young people prefer. And the practice of attending to the body is developmentally appropriate and it is also the practice that the trauma field uses, which means that a young person with a trauma history may have a specific and complicated relationship with it.

**The group.** The eight-week group format is developmentally appropriate for adolescents, and the developmental literature on the adolescent peer group is directly relevant: a group of peers who are practising the same thing is a very different proposition from a group of adults.

**The parents.** The parental involvement in the mindfulness-based approaches is more limited than in the behavioural and the dialectical approaches, and this is a genuine weakness. The parents of a young person with emotional difficulties frequently need to understand what is being done, and the literature on the family dimension of the mindfulness-based approaches is thin.

### 5.3 The specific cautions

**The trauma caution.** This is the most important caution in the chapter, and the field has been slow to state it clearly. A practice that asks a young person to attend to the present moment, to the body, and to the sensations is, for a young person with a trauma history, a practice that may be profoundly reactivating, and the somatic and trauma fields have been clear that such practices must be titrated, prepared, and paced, and that a young person with a dissociative presentation may find the interior attention genuinely frightening.

**The risk of the young person treating the practice as a task to be performed.** The practice will be performed competently and will produce a report of benefit, and the young person will have learned the observable form of mindfulness without the underlying shift. The practitioner's task is to attend to the young person's relationship to the practice, and to notice the difference between a young person who is attending and a young person who is performing.

**The risk of the practice replacing the work.** A young person who has learned to manage their distress with a brief attention practice may decline to talk about the difficult things. The practice is a tool, and the developmental and clinical requirement is that it be used in the service of the developmental task rather than in place of it.

**The risk of a secular practice with a religious frame.** For a young person with a religious or spiritual practice of their own, the mindfulness-based approach may be welcome, or may be experienced as an intrusion, or may be welcome but in a modified form that they would prefer. The practitioner should ask.

## 6. The Third-Generation Mindfulness and the Contemporary Position

The field has continued to develop, and several of the developments are relevant to this course.

**Mindfulness-integrated behavioural therapy** and other hybrid approaches combine the mindfulness principles with the behavioural and cognitive techniques, and the rationale is that the two are addressing the same problem from different directions.

**Mindfulness-based relapse prevention** adapts the relapse prevention plan for a range of conditions other than depression.

**The "brief" mindfulness interventions** have been developed for schools and for primary care, and the evidence for brief interventions is generally weaker than for the full courses.

**The contemplative psychology literature** has developed a substantial research base on the developmental trajectory of the attentional and metacognitive capacities, on the relationship between contemplative practice and the brain, and on the cross-cultural comparison of contemplative traditions, and it is a better foundation for the clinical work than the clinical literature itself.
