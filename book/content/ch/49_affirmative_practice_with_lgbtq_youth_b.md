
## 6. Confidentiality, Names, and Disclosure

Confidentiality can be a safety intervention. Before counselling begins, explain limits in plain language and identify exactly what may be shared with parents, school staff, health providers, and safeguarding services. Distinguish safety information from identity information. A parent may need to know that a young person is self-harming without being told every detail of sexuality or gender exploration, subject to law and risk.

Disclosure planning asks:

1. What might be gained and risked by telling each person?
2. What is the likely response, based on evidence rather than hope alone?
3. What timing, setting, and medium are safest?
4. Who can be present or nearby?
5. What housing, financial, educational, and emotional supports exist if the response is poor?
6. What boundaries will the young person set about questions and further disclosure?

The counsellor does not pressure coming out as a marker of therapeutic progress. Concealment can be costly, but it can also be protective. The ethical goal is controlled, informed choice where possible.

Records require special care. Use chosen name and pronouns in the therapeutic record where systems allow, note context-specific names, and avoid unnecessary recording of sensitive details. Appointment reminders and school registers can out a student. Practitioners should advocate for record systems that protect privacy and dignity.

## 7. Family Work

Family responses range from celebration to grief, confusion, anger, fear, or rejection. Initial shock is not always final rejection. Parents may need space to ask questions without making the young person responsible for educating or comforting them.

Family sessions should not become debates about the legitimacy of the young person's identity. Ground rules can include respectful names and pronouns, no insults, no threats, no forced disclosure to extended family, and pauses when conversation escalates. Separate parent sessions may help adults process fears, obtain accurate information, and identify concrete accepting behaviours.

Concrete acceptance includes listening without immediate correction, defending the young person against bullying, welcoming friends, supporting safe expression at home, using the chosen name where agreed, and helping access supportive communities. Where family behaviour creates danger, safeguarding and alternative support take precedence over preserving a joint format.

Cultural and religious contexts require respectful complexity. This textbook does not use faith-based structures, and counsellors should not impose theological arguments. They can, however, help families distinguish their values and fears from immediate safety behaviours: even where beliefs differ, violence, expulsion, humiliation, and withdrawal of care cause harm. Referral to culturally congruent family resources may help when chosen by the family.

## 8. School Practice

School counsellors can assess bullying patterns, staff responses, facilities, dress codes, sports access, curriculum inclusion, and confidentiality in registers and communications. Interventions may include safety routes, trusted staff, timetable adjustments, anti-bullying action, peer bystander work, and advocacy.

Do not make the targeted student responsible for educating the whole school or repeatedly recounting harm. Incident systems should record patterns, locations, and adult responses. Restorative approaches are inappropriate where they force contact with an unsafe perpetrator or minimise identity-based harassment.

Clubs and peer groups can be protective where they are permitted and safely facilitated. If a club would expose students to danger, alternatives may include off-site youth services, moderated online communities with safeguarding, library resources, and individual mentoring.

## 9. Health and Specialist Referral

Counsellors should know local pathways for sexual health, HIV prevention and care, substance use, eating disorders, trauma, housing, and gender-related health care. Gender care for minors is regulated differently across jurisdictions and may involve specialist multidisciplinary assessment. The counsellor's role is supportive, non-directive regarding identity outcome, accurate about local process, and attentive to distress, family communication, school safety, and co-occurring needs while referrals proceed.

Avoid acting as gatekeeper beyond professional competence. Do not require a young person to prove identity through stereotypes. Do not delay mental health support while specialist assessment is pending. Coordinate with consent and document who holds responsibility for each part of care.

## 10. Case Illustration: Two Names, One Safety Plan

**Presentation.** Alex, fifteen, uses the name Alex and they/them pronouns with friends. At home and school, records use a different name. Alex has begun skipping lunch to avoid a changing-room corridor where students shout slurs. Their mother has requested counselling because Alex is “secretive and angry”.

**Assessment.** Alex is not depressed about being non-binary; they are exhausted by vigilance and by correcting adults. Home response is uncertain. An older sibling is supportive. Alex fears a school letter will reveal the chosen name.

**Intervention.** The counsellor first addresses administrative safety: appointment messages go to Alex's phone, not a shared family account; the counsellor checks what name appears on school sign-in sheets. Together they identify a trusted teacher and an alternative lunch space that does not isolate Alex from friends.

Disclosure is planned in stages. Alex tells their mother with the sibling nearby at home, after preparing answers and an exit plan to the sibling's room. The counsellor holds a separate parent session focused on immediate behaviours: listen, use Alex at home if Alex confirms, do not inform extended family, and challenge corridor bullying narratives that blame Alex's expression.

School action targets location and supervision rather than requiring Alex to report every incident publicly. Over time, Alex eats with friends again. Family conversations remain imperfect, but the mother practises the chosen name and stops threatening to remove internet access as a response to identity. The outcome is not framed as Alex becoming more resilient to harassment; adults reduce exposure and increase support.

## 11. Evidence and Limitations

Research consistently associates family acceptance, school belonging, supportive policies, and access to affirming communities with better mental health among LGBTQ+ youth. Interventions that reduce bullying and rejection operate at plausible causal pathways, though many studies are observational and contexts vary.

Evidence for specific counselling models designed solely for LGBTQ+ adolescents is still developing. Affirmative adaptations of cognitive-behavioural and family interventions show promise. Practitioners should monitor outcomes rather than assume affirmation as an attitude guarantees effective technique.

Legal and policy environments vary and can change. Students must learn local law on consent, confidentiality, safeguarding, education, health referrals, and discrimination, while recognising that professional ethics may require advocacy where law or institutional practice creates avoidable harm.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Affirmative practice** — Care that validates sexual and gender diversity and addresses stigma-related stress without pathologising identity.
- **Chosen name** — The name a young person uses for themselves, which may vary by safety context.
- **Outing** — Disclosing someone's sexual orientation or gender identity without consent.
- **Family acceptance** — Concrete behaviours that communicate support, protection, and belonging.
- **Conversion efforts** — Practices intended to change sexual orientation or gender identity; unsupported and associated with harm.
- **Chosen family** — Supportive relationships formed beyond biological or legal family.
- **Minority stress** — Stress produced by stigma, discrimination, vigilance, concealment, and internalised negativity.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Affirmative practice is neither neutral about harm nor prescriptive about identity. It protects confidentiality, follows the young person's language, reduces environmental danger, builds family acceptance where possible, and treats resilience as something communities can support rather than demand. The counsellor's credibility rests on small administrative details as much as large statements: the right name, a safe message, a private record, a teacher who intervenes, and a door that does not have to be explained every day.
