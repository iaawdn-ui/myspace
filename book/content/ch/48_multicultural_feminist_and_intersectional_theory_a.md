# Multicultural, Feminist, and Intersectional Theory

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why culture is not an optional variable added after theory but a context that shapes distress, identity, help seeking, and therapeutic power.
- Describe multicultural counselling competencies and cultural humility.
- Explain feminist theory's attention to power, gender, socialisation, and empowerment.
- Define intersectionality and apply it without reducing a young person to a list of categories.
- Use cultural formulation and broaching in assessment.
- Recognise cultural bias in diagnosis, testing, school discipline, and theory selection.
- Practise anti-oppressive, culturally sustaining counselling with children, adolescents, families, and institutions.

## Chapter Overview

Every counselling theory carries assumptions about what a person is, what a family should look like, what emotions mean, what counts as maturity, and who has authority to define a problem. Multicultural, feminist, and intersectional theories make those assumptions visible. They ask not only “What is happening inside this young person?” but also “What has happened around them, what meanings are available to them, whose knowledge is being trusted, and what power does the counsellor hold?”

## 1. Culture as Context, Not Decoration

Culture includes ethnicity, race, language, migration, nationality, religion or non-religious worldview where relevant to the client, class, disability culture, gender norms, sexuality, family forms, region, and institutional cultures such as school or care systems. This textbook avoids faith-based structures, but culturally responsive practice still requires recognising when a client identifies religious or spiritual commitments as personally important, without the counsellor importing religious content or practice.

Cultural competence is not mastery of a catalogue of group traits. That approach produces stereotypes. Contemporary practice emphasises **cultural humility**: lifelong self-critique, recognition of power imbalance, openness to learning from the client, and institutional accountability.

The counsellor asks: What should I understand about your family, community, language, and experiences for this work to be useful? What have services misunderstood before? Who should be involved? What would respect look like here?

## 2. Multicultural Counselling Theory

Multicultural counselling developed as scholars and practitioners challenged the assumption that theories derived from Western, educated, individualist populations were universal. Sue and colleagues articulated competencies in awareness, knowledge, and skills; later frameworks added action and advocacy.

Key concerns include:

- **Worldview:** individual versus collective decision-making, direct versus indirect communication, time orientation, emotional expression, and concepts of illness and healing.
- **Identity development:** how young people understand ethnicity, race, culture, migration, language, and belonging across contexts.
- **Acculturation and enculturation:** adaptation to a dominant culture and connection to heritage culture, neither inherently healthy or unhealthy.
- **Discrimination and racial trauma:** repeated invalidation, surveillance, exclusion, and threat can shape stress physiology, identity, school trust, and help seeking.
- **Language access:** assessment through an interpreter requires trained interpretation, confidentiality, and awareness that family members—especially children—should not interpret sensitive material.

## 3. Feminist Theory

Feminist counselling emerged from recognition that psychological distress cannot be separated from gendered power, violence, labour, caregiving, body norms, and social expectations. Its principles include egalitarian relationship, valuing lived experience, demystifying therapy, empowerment, social change, and attention to intersectional identities.

With adolescents, feminist practice asks how gender norms regulate behaviour. Girls may be rewarded for compliance and punished for anger; boys may be shamed for vulnerability and rewarded for dominance; non-binary and transgender young people may face invalidation or danger. But feminist analysis does not assume all girls are passive or all boys are emotionally illiterate. It examines specific social messages and their costs.

Feminist practice also notices adult gender patterns in the room: mothers blamed for family distress, fathers treated as peripheral, female staff expected to absorb emotional labour, or male counsellors presumed unsafe or authoritative. The goal is not to reverse blame but to redistribute responsibility and voice.

## 4. Intersectionality

Intersectionality, developed in Black feminist legal scholarship by Kimberlé Crenshaw, shows how systems such as racism, sexism, class inequality, ableism, homophobia, transphobia, and age hierarchy interact. A disabled Black girl in care, a working-class migrant boy, and an affluent white LGBTQ+ student may all experience “school anxiety”, but the mechanisms, risks, resources, and institutional responses differ.

![Power differentials in counselling: how professional, adult, institutional, linguistic, and economic power affect voice and choice.](fig75_power_differential.png)

Intersectional practice avoids additive checklists. It asks which identities are salient to the young person, which systems are active in this situation, and where the counsellor's own social position affects perception. It also recognises privilege within marginalised groups and marginalisation within privileged groups.

## 5. Minority Stress and Structural Formulation

Minority stress theory distinguishes distal stressors—discrimination, rejection, victimisation—from proximal processes such as internalised stigma, concealment, and expectation of rejection. Structural formulation extends case conceptualisation to housing, income, transport, school policy, policing, immigration status, health access, language, and disability access.

This matters because a counsellor can otherwise teach coping for conditions that should be changed. Breathing exercises do not remedy racist discipline. Self-esteem work does not replace accessible toilets, interpreters, safe transport, or anti-bullying enforcement. Culturally responsive counselling therefore includes advocacy and consultation.
