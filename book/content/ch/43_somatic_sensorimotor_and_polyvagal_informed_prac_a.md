# Somatic, Sensorimotor, and Polyvagal-Informed Practice

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain why trauma, anxiety, and chronic stress may be expressed through the body.
- Describe autonomic states using polyvagal-informed language while recognising scientific debate about aspects of polyvagal theory.
- Explain the window of tolerance and identify hyperarousal, hypoarousal, and regulated engagement.
- Describe sensorimotor and somatic assessment: posture, breath, movement, sensation, impulse, and procedural patterns.
- Use grounding, orienting, breathing, movement, and boundary exercises safely.
- Distinguish somatic work from massage, physical therapy, medical treatment, and touch-based interventions requiring separate consent and competence.
- Apply somatic principles to adolescents with trauma, panic, dissociation, chronic pain presentations, and shutdown.
- Evaluate evidence, cultural considerations, safeguarding, and ethical limits.

## Chapter Overview

Some young people can describe events but not feelings. Some can recite coping strategies while their shoulders rise, breathing quickens, hands shake, or gaze becomes fixed. Others become inexplicably sleepy, blank, compliant, or absent when a subject approaches danger. Somatic and sensorimotor approaches begin with these observations: psychological life is embodied, and regulation is not achieved by instruction alone.

The word *somatic* refers to the lived body. *Sensorimotor* emphasises sensation and movement: what the body senses, prepares to do, inhibits, or repeats. Polyvagal-informed practice draws on Stephen Porges' account of autonomic regulation and social engagement, while responsible teaching must acknowledge that some specific anatomical and evolutionary claims of polyvagal theory are contested. Clinicians can use the practical language of safety, mobilisation, shutdown, and co-regulation without presenting every theoretical claim as settled neuroscience.

## 1. Why the Body Matters

Threat prepares action. Muscles tense, breathing changes, digestion slows, attention narrows, and the body prepares to fight, flee, freeze, submit, or seek help. When threat passes, a regulated system can complete the arousal cycle and return to rest, appetite, play, learning, and social connection.

When threat is chronic, unpredictable, or inescapable, adaptive responses may become habitual. A student may scan every classroom for exits, startle at a door, hold their breath when criticised, collapse into exhaustion after school, or experience stomach pain before separation. These are not character defects. They may be procedural learning: the body has learned what to do before conscious appraisal catches up.

Talk remains important, but talk delivered to a body in alarm may be heard as noise or threat. The practitioner's voice, pace, facial expression, distance, posture, and predictability are therefore clinical variables, not decorative bedside manner.

## 2. Autonomic States in Clinical Language

A simplified three-state map is useful for teaching.

**Social engagement and relative safety.** Breathing is flexible; voice has prosody; facial muscles move; eye contact can be chosen; digestion and rest are possible; curiosity returns. The young person can listen, disagree, joke, and learn.

**Mobilisation.** Sympathetic arousal supports fight or flight. The young person may be restless, irritable, argumentative, panicky, vigilant, unable to sit still, or desperate to leave. The clinical need is not immediate insight but reduction of danger cues and safe discharge or settling of energy.

**Shutdown or immobilisation.** The young person may become quiet, numb, slowed, blank, dissociated, overly compliant, or unable to speak. Adults often misread this as calm or defiance. The clinical need is gentle orientation, choice, movement, sensory input, and connection without demand.

These states are not moral ranks and not diagnoses. A person moves among them. Shutdown may be life-preserving during unavoidable threat. Mobilisation may be appropriate in sport or emergency. Problems arise when transitions are rigid, prolonged, or triggered by cues that no longer signal actual danger.

## 3. The Window of Tolerance

Daniel Siegel popularised the window of tolerance as the arousal band within which a person can process emotion and information without becoming overwhelmed or shutting down. Trauma, sleep deprivation, pain, hunger, illness, sensory overload, and ongoing danger narrow the window. Safety, rhythm, predictability, relationships, movement, rest, and skills widen it.

Assessment therefore asks: What narrows this young person's window? What are the first signs of leaving it? What helps return? Who helps? Which environments make regulation easier? What does the young person already do—music, walking, drawing, sport, humour, prayer-free private reflection, being with animals, coding, cooking—that genuinely settles the body rather than merely distracting adults?

## 4. Sensorimotor Assessment

Assessment is observational and collaborative. The practitioner notices, without over-interpreting:

- posture: collapsed, braced, twisted away, frozen, restless;
- breath: shallow, held, rapid, sighing, mainly upper chest;
- movement: fidgeting, rocking, leg shaking, immobility, repeated gestures;
- voice: monotone, rushed, whispering, unusually loud;
- gaze and facial expression;
- reported sensation: pressure, heat, nausea, tingling, pain, emptiness;
- action impulses: urge to run, hide, hit, curl up, shout, cry, laugh, or disappear.

Questions are concrete: “As you mention the classroom, what changes in your body?” “Where does the tightness begin?” “Does your body want to move closer, back away, or become very still?” The young person may answer with metaphor—“a knot”, “static”, “a wall”—and the metaphor should be respected rather than translated too quickly.

Medical assessment matters. Pain, thyroid disease, anaemia, asthma, cardiac symptoms, medication effects, substance use, seizures, sleep apnoea, and many other conditions can mimic or amplify anxiety and dissociation. Somatic practice never replaces medical evaluation.

## 5. Principles of Intervention

**Safety and choice.** The young person controls pace, may keep eyes open, may decline an exercise, and knows they can stop. Choice is regulating.

**Titration.** Work occurs in small doses. Attention moves between a difficult sensation and a resource—a stable chair, a pleasant image, a steady breath—rather than flooding the system.

**Pendulation.** The practitioner helps the young person move attention between activation and settling, building confidence that states can change.

**Completion and discharge.** After mobilisation, the body may need walking, shaking out limbs, stretching, drinking water, or rest. The goal is not dramatic catharsis.

**Co-regulation before self-regulation.** A calm adult, predictable routine, and relational repair precede expectations that the young person regulate alone.

**Meaning follows regulation.** Narrative linking—“this stomach tightening happens when you expect criticism”—is more useful after the sensation can be noticed without overwhelm.
