## 7. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Would Not Do the Homework

**The referral.** Caoimhe, fourteen, is referred by a school for "low mood and refusal." She is not attending school consistently, she has stopped attending her swimming club, where she was previously competitive, and she has been withdrawn. Her mother reports that she "used to be a handful" and has recently become "completely switched off." There is a bereavement in the family six months ago: Caoimhe's grandmother, with whom she had a close relationship, died. Her mother has been off work since the bereavement and is "not handling it."

**The first session.** The practitioner begins, in the ACT tradition, by establishing what Caoimhe has tried. The question is: "What have you tried to make the feeling go away?" And Caoimhe, who is articulate and willing, produces a list with an almost impressive fluency:

- Not thinking about it: "I try to distract myself, watch videos, play games."
- Talking to friends: "I told them I was fine so they wouldn't worry."
- Getting angry: "Sometimes I get really angry and shout at my mum, and then I feel better for about ten minutes, and then I feel worse."
- Cancelling: "I said I was ill, and then I was ill, and then I stopped going and I couldn't go back."
- Trying to be good: "I try to be the same as I was. I do the swimming times and I'm slower and I can't do them, and I don't want anyone to know."

**The matrix.** The practitioner draws the matrix with her — the left column "What I tried" and the right column "What it cost me." She asks Caoimhe to fill in the second column for each of the strategies. And Caoimhe fills it in, and the filling in is the intervention: "Distracting — I don't feel better, I just don't feel it, and then I feel nothing at all, and the nothing is worse." "Telling friends I'm fine — they think I'm fine. I don't call them now." "Getting angry — I'm the person who shouts. My mum doesn't ask me how I am any more." "Cancelling — I don't go back. I don't think I could." "Trying to be good — I'm tired all the time and I hate the pool."

**The creative hopelessness.** The practitioner says: "So none of those has worked. Any of them, for any length of time, actually worked?" And Caoimhe says, after a pause: "No." And then, in a voice that surprises even herself: "Nothing works." And the practitioner says: "That's not you being broken, Caoimhe. That's you having tried really hard at something that doesn't work. Those are different. Hard-working people get stuck when the thing they're working on doesn't work. The problem isn't that you're not trying hard enough. The problem is that trying harder at this is what's keeping you stuck."

**The defusion.** The next exercise is the one that does the work. The practitioner says: "Can I tell you the story about the two people on the bus? There's a man on a bus, and the driver keeps turning around and saying 'you're doing it wrong, you're doing it wrong, you're doing it wrong.' And there's a passenger, and the passenger is screaming 'LEAVE ME ALONE, LEAVE ME ALONE, LEAVE ME ALONE.' And the bus goes nowhere. And the driver is the one who's giving directions, and the passenger is the one who's screaming, and the passenger can't drive. Who do you think is driving your bus?"

Caoimhe says: "Both." And then, after a moment: "No — the screaming. The screaming's driving."

The practitioner does not push the metaphor further. She asks: "What would the passenger look like if it was driving?" And Caoimhe says, slowly: "It'd be the voice that says 'you're not like you were, you're a disappointment, and everyone can tell.'"

**The work with the grandmother.** The practitioner establishes that the voice is the voice of Caoimhe's grandmother, whom she loved, and who is now — in the voice — the one who is disappointed in her. And the practitioner makes the developmental observation explicitly: the grandmother's death has removed the person who would have been able to say "you are not like you used to be, and that is fine." The acceptance work is then reframed: it is not a matter of accepting a truth about her incompetence, because it is not a truth; it is a matter of hearing the grandmother's voice without obeying it.

**The committed action.** The work then turns to the values, and the practitioner uses the concrete version: "You said you were competitive in swimming and that you stopped. If nothing changed in your head — if you woke up tomorrow and your brain was exactly the same — what would you still be doing?" Caoimhe says: "I'd go to the club. I'd go and I'd be slow, and I'd be last, and I'd hate it." "Would you?" "No, I wouldn't." "So what would be true if you went?" "That I'd be there." "That's the question. Not whether you'd be fast. Whether you'd be there."

The committed action is small and specific: to go to the club, to attend two sessions, to swim the distances but not to time them, and to tell her coach in advance. The practitioner and Caoimhe also plan what she will say to her grandmother's voice when it tells her she is a disappointment.

**The work with the mother.** The mother's own withdrawal is the maintaining factor, and the practitioner works with the mother separately, with her consent and with Caoimhe's knowledge. The mother is also in the creative hopelessness: she has been "trying to be strong" and "keeping things normal," and the effect has been to withdraw from Caoimhe entirely. The work with the mother is the same work — the matrix, the defusion, and a small committed action, which is that she will ask Caoimhe one question about the pool and will listen to the answer without trying to fix it.

**The outcome.** Over twelve weeks, Caoimhe returns to the pool and does not swim competitively; her attendance improves but is not consistent; the "voice" is still present but Caoimhe can now name it as the grandmother's, and the naming has changed its relation to her. The mother asks a question and is surprised to find that Caoimhe tells her about the grandmother. Caoimhe says, in the final session of the phase: "I'm not where I was. But I'm not in the same place either. I don't know who I am now. But I know I want to go back."

**The teaching point.** The intervention was not the "return to swimming" goal, which a behavioural activation practitioner might also have set, and it was not the acceptance of the loss, which a grief-focused practitioner might also have offered. It was the demonstration, produced by Caoimhe herself in the matrix, that none of her strategies had ever worked, and the consequent permission to try a different relationship with the content of her mind. The developmental framing of the values question — "if nothing changed in your head" — is what made the work possible for a fourteen-year-old, and it is the ACT analogue of the developmental modification described in Section 5.2.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

**The evidence base for ACT is among the largest in the profession, and it is the largest of any of the third-wave approaches.**

**The number and quality of the trials.** ACT has been evaluated in a large number of randomised controlled trials across a wide range of conditions and populations, including chronic pain, anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, psychosis, eating disorders, substance use, and a range of health conditions. Several large multisite trials have been conducted, including a major trial in the United Kingdom and the Netherlands in the treatment of psychosis, and the Australian national programme for youth depression, which used ACT-informed treatment.

**The comparison with CBT.** In head-to-head trials, ACT and CBT produce comparable outcomes on the symptoms, and ACT tends to produce somewhat better outcomes on measures of well-being, of psychological flexibility, of work and relationship functioning, and on the measures of values-based action. The differences on the symptom measures are generally small and are not always statistically significant, and the honest summary is that ACT and CBT are broadly comparable treatments with different emphases and with a modestly different profile of benefit.

**The adolescent literature.** The evidence for ACT with adolescents is smaller than the adult evidence but is growing and includes a substantial number of controlled trials in school settings, in the treatment of anxiety, of depression, of disordered eating, and of substance use. The trials are generally well conducted, and the effect sizes are moderate. The developmental adaptations are described in the clinical literature and the protocol-based evidence is less developed than in the adult case.

**The component evidence.** The studies of the acceptance and commitment components have produced a small body of evidence that the "creative hopelessness perspective" and the "defusion" components contribute to outcome in a way that is separable from the general effect of the therapy. The evidence is not strong, and it is not conclusive, and a student should describe it accurately.

**The limits.** As with the other third-wave approaches, the population problem is real: the samples are disproportionately adult, female, white, and without significant comorbidity, and the research in schools and in the juvenile justice settings is a smaller body with the same methodological qualities. A substantial proportion of the studies have been conducted by investigators with an allegiance to the theory, though the sheer volume of the literature and the number of independent investigators has made this less damaging than it has been elsewhere. The long-term follow-up data are limited.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

**Strengths.** It provides the clearest and most usable account in the field of why a young person who tries to control their internal experiences makes things worse, and the account is one that a young person can be helped to see for themselves, which makes it extraordinarily well suited to work with the young people who most resist a traditional symptom-focused treatment. The five senses of the therapist's behaviour give a beginning therapist a usable answer to the question of what to actually do, and the theory is unusually coherent as a piece of philosophy, psychology, and practice. The committed action and values work integrate the acceptance and the behavioural traditions rather than opposing them. And the evidence base is large and of good quality.

**Limitations.** The theory's philosophical sophistication is a barrier: the vocabulary of functional contextualism and relational frame theory is a genuine obstacle to training and to multidisciplinary working, and the field has frequently responded by teaching the techniques without the theory, which produces a practice that is often unrecognisable from the theory. The commitment to values work can become coercive if the practitioner has values of their own to impose, and this is a real risk with young people, who are developmentally in the process of determining their own values. The theory's relative neglect of the body, of the developmental context, and of the social and structural determinants is a genuine weakness. And the popular version of the theory, in which acceptance is confused with resignation, is a serious misrepresentation that the profession has not adequately addressed.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 Culture and acceptance

**The cultural specification of the struggle.** The theory's account of the struggle switch describes a specific pattern: a person who attempts to control their internal experiences and who is thereby harmed by the attempt. Whether this pattern is universal is an open question. Some features of the account are very widely attested: the tendency to suppress unwanted thoughts, the rebound effect, the narrowness of the behavioural repertoire that suppression produces. Others are more culturally marked.

**The value of control as a cultural good.** In a cultural context in which emotional restraint, in-group conformity, and the performance of composure are strongly valued — and in which the expression of distress is understood to have consequences for the young person and for their family — the struggle with one's own experience may be considerably more adaptive than the theory's account allows. A young person who suppresses their distress in front of a family that would be harmed by the display is not making a cognitive error; they are exercising a difficult and costly skill.

**Values and the developmental task.** The values work is the most culturally sensitive component, because the values in question are assumed to be the young person's own. In a family that organises the young person's life around filial duty, the "values" that the young person reports are not a psychological possession but a relational position, and the values work can become an instrument of the individualisation of a young person whose developmental task is not to separate from the family but to find a way of being a full member of it while retaining their own voice.

**Defusion and the reality of harm.** The defusion work assumes that thoughts are events rather than facts, and the assumption requires qualification where the young person's thinking is a report of something that is actually happening. A young person who thinks "I am going to be hurt" and who is in fact in a dangerous situation must not be helped to defuse that thought into a mere event. The ethical requirement is that the practitioner know the difference, and the clinical safeguard is the comprehensive assessment that every good assessment includes.

### 10.2 The ethical requirements of the values work

**The young person must not be directed towards the practitioner's values.** The temptation is real and the practice is well known. A practitioner who is moved by a young person's suffering may guide them towards a value — self-reliance, expression, self-determination — that is the practitioner's rather than the young person's, and the young person may adopt it, and it may not be theirs.

**The young person must be able to decline.** The committed action must be genuinely chosen, and the young person must be able to say no. And the therapist must be able to work with a young person whose values genuinely conflict with what the young person wants, which is a more frequent situation in adolescence than in adulthood, because the adolescent's values are often genuinely in conflict with each other and with their wishes.

**The "I don't have to" must be a real option.** The core of the therapy's offer is that the young person does not have to fight their thoughts. If the young person is in an environment in which they do in fact have to fight — in which the suppression of distress is required, in which the expression of a need has consequences, in which the safety of the young person depends on their performance — then the therapy's central offer is not available to them, and the practitioner must say so.

**Autonomy and the developmental task.** The theory's commitment to autonomy is in tension with the developmental reality of a young person who is not yet capable of autonomous decision-making in all domains, and the tension is the same one that runs through the whole of this book. The practical resolution is the developmental framing described in Section 5.2: the values work is about the young person's own concrete commitments, not about abstract principles.

### 10.3 The ethical value of the theory

The ethical value of ACT with young people is considerable and is worth recording. It is a therapy that says to a young person: you are not broken, you have been trying something that does not work, and there is a way of relating to your own mind that is different. For a young person who has been told that their anxiety is a illness to be cured, or that their mind is the problem, that is a considerable gift. And the theory's insistence on the practitioner's presence, attunement, and potency is a corrective to the therapeutic minimalism of much of the contemporary humanistic practice.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)** — A therapy developed by Steven Hayes in which the aim is psychological flexibility rather than symptom reduction, and in which the struggle to control unwanted internal experiences is identified as a primary maintaining mechanism.
- **Functional contextualism** — The philosophical position in which behaviour is understood in terms of its function in a context, and the practical question is what works.
- **Relational frame theory** — The formal account in which the human capacity to relate to language and to the relations among words is the platform on which both solutions and problems are built.
- **Cognitive fusion** — The condition in which a person is entangled with the content of their thoughts, so that the thought and the person are treated as identical.
- **The struggle switch** — The metaphor in which the effort to suppress unwanted thoughts and feelings is itself the cause of exhaustion, constriction, and reduced engagement.
- **Experiential avoidance** — The attempt to escape from or suppress unwanted internal experiences, and the mechanism that maintains many difficulties.
- **Psychological flexibility** — The capacity to remain in contact with the present moment and with the self-as-context while unwanted internal experiences are present, and to act in accordance with values.
- **The six processes** — Acceptance, defusion, contact with the present moment, the self as context, values, and committed action.
- **The ACT hexaflex** — The hexagonal arrangement of the six processes, with the three "open" processes loosening cognitive fusion and the three "closed" processes moving toward a valued life.
- **Stuck positions** — The pathological patterns in which the person is stuck in the closed processes without loosening, or in the open processes without movement.
- **Creative hopelessness** — The perspective in which the young person's difficulties consist of a coherent and unsuccessful strategy of controlling internal experience, which is demonstrated rather than asserted.
- **The matrix** — The structured comparison of the attempts to control and the costs of those attempts, from which the creative hopelessness follows.
- **Defusion** — The process of seeing thoughts as thoughts; distinct from suppression and from distraction.
- **The "passengers on the bus" metaphor** — The ACT metaphor for the distinction between the person and the content of the mind, and probably the most used of the metaphors.
- **The "quicksand" metaphor** — The metaphor for the difference between struggling, stillness, and slow movement in the presence of an unwanted experience.
- **The "milk, milk, milk" exercise** — The demonstration that the meaning of a word is a product of a context.
- **The five senses of the therapist's behaviour** — Evocation, freedom, presence, attunement, and potency; the specification of the practitioner's conduct.
- **Committed action** — Sustained, concrete action in the direction of a chosen value, aimed at changing the world rather than the person.
- **Values-based living** — The orientation in which the person's choices are guided by explicitly determined values rather than by the avoidance of discomfort.
- **The "if nothing changed in your head" question** — The developmental adaptation of the values work, in which the young person is asked what they would still do if their internal experience were unchanged.
- **Flexible exposure** — The trauma-informed descendant of the acceptance work, in which the young person approaches the avoided material while being helped to relate to it differently.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Acceptance and commitment therapy emerged from the acceptance-versus-change debate within behaviour therapy, and its central claim is that the attempt to control unwanted thoughts and feelings is itself the mechanism that maintains many difficulties. The theory rests on functional contextualism, which asks what behaviour is accomplishing rather than what is happening inside the person, and on relational frame theory, which locates the human capacity for problem-construction on the same platform as the capacity for solution-construction.

The clinical core is the struggle switch, the account of a person trying to hold a ball under water and being exhausted by the effort, and the therapeutic pivot is the recognition that the goal is not to change the content of the mind but to change the young person's relationship to it. The six processes of psychological flexibility are arranged in the ACT hexaflex, and the pathological patterns are the stuck positions: the rigid, over-controlled young person stuck on the closed side, and the passive, dissociated young person stuck on the open side.

The creative hopelessness perspective replaces the medical and the moral framing of a young person's difficulty with a strategic one, and the matrix is the technique by which the young person produces the evidence themselves. The practice includes the defusion exercises, the experiential avoidance metaphors, and the values and committed action work, and it is specified for the practitioner by the five senses of the therapist's behaviour.

The developmental fit with young people is good, and the modifications are concrete: pitch the metaphor at the edge of capacity, frame the values work developmentally, keep the tasks small, and use the acceptance work with care in the presence of trauma. The evidence base is among the largest in the profession and is broadly comparable to cognitive therapy on symptoms and somewhat better on well-being and functioning. The ethical requirements concern the young person's right to decline, the practitioner's own values, the assumption that the struggle is always maladaptive, and the requirement to know the difference between a thought and a report of something that is actually happening.

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 20.1 — The Matrix (2 hours, individual, then pairs)

Complete the matrix on your own situation: the "what I tried" column, and then the "what it cost me" column, and then the "what actually worked" column. Read the three columns in sequence and notice the creative hopelessness when it arrives. The exercise is the theory's central technique and it works on the practitioner as well as on the client.

### Activity 20.2 — The Metaphor Repertoire (90 minutes, groups of four)

Each group takes one ACT metaphor and produces three versions: for a nine-year-old, for a fourteen-year-old, and for a seventeen-year-old who will find the child version embarrassing. Then they specify the developmental reason for each adaptation. The groups then present the three versions to a peer in the role of a young person, and the peer's feedback on which version they would tolerate is as useful as the group's reasoning.

### Activity 20.3 — The "If Nothing Changed" Question (60 minutes, pairs)

Ask a partner the question: "If nothing changed in your head — if you woke up tomorrow and your brain was exactly the same — what would you still be doing?" Then ask: "Who does that matter to?" and: "What would be the smallest thing you could do this week?" The third question is the ACT one; the second is the developmental one. Then swap. The exercise is a compact version of the therapy and it demonstrates why the values work has to be concrete.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory and the practice.** Read Hayes, Luoma, and Wilson (2009) for the whole, and read the ACT Matrix by Luoma and Hayes for the practical use of the core methods. Read the functional contextual account of the therapist's behaviour with attention, because the five senses are the most immediately useful part of the theory for a new practitioner.

**For the evidence.** Read the comprehensive ACT research volume and the head-to-head comparisons with cognitive therapy. Note the pattern of the findings — comparable on symptoms, different on functioning — and consider what it implies for the choice between them.

**For the developmental application.** Read the adolescent ACT literature, and then read the school-based trials, which are the most relevant to a practitioner in a school setting and the most liberally available in the professional literature.

**For the critique.** Read the philosophical critiques of functional contextualism and the cultural critiques of the values work, and read the trauma literature on the misuse of acceptance work with young people who have been abused. The theory's own literature on the phased application of ACT in trauma treatment is the appropriate corrective.
