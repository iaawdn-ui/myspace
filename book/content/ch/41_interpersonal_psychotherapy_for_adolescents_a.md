# Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the origin of interpersonal psychotherapy and describe the clinical problems that produced it.
- State the four problem areas of the model and describe each in detail.
- Describe the three phases of treatment and the specific tasks of each.
- Describe the techniques of the model, including the exploration, the clarification, the dialogue with the significant other, and the enactment.
- Apply the model to the treatment of adolescent depression and to a case involving an adolescent interpersonal difficulty.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of interpersonal psychotherapy with young people, including the questions of the model's origins and of the limits of a dyadic model with a young person who is at risk.

## Chapter Overview

Interpersonal psychotherapy was developed in the 1970s by Gerald Klerman and colleagues, and it was designed for a population that the psychotherapies of the time served badly: people with depression whose difficulties were not primarily cognitive, people for whom the depression had become embedded in a set of strained relationships, and people for whom a long, exploratory, and interpretative treatment was not feasible.

The model's proposition is that **depression is maintained by problems in the relationships**, and that the treatment is the resolution of those problems. The depression is not treated directly and it is not interpreted; the relationships are treated, and the depression improves as the relationships improve.

This makes it a distinctive proposition in this book, and it is worth noticing what it shares with the systemic approaches of Part V and what it shares with the behavioural approaches of Part IV. It shares with the systemic approaches the view that the problem is in the interaction rather than in the individual. It shares with the behavioural approaches the view that the targets should be specific and observable. And it shares with the cognitive approaches the view that the young person's own account of their situation is the clinical material.

The adaptation of the model for adolescents, developed in the 1990s and tested in a large multisite trial, is the most thoroughly evaluated adolescent psychotherapy in the literature for adolescent depression, and it is a treatment that a school counsellor can deliver with a modest amount of training and a good manual.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The origin

The model was developed at the Massachusetts General Hospital and its origin was partly pragmatic. The 1970s psychopharmacological trials had established that antidepressants worked, and the clinical problem was that the improvement was partial, that the relapse rate was high, and that the patients were not engaged in any long-term psychological work.

The intervention that was designed to address these problems was not a new theory of depression but a **time-limited, focused, and practical treatment** that addressed the interpersonal situations in which the patient's depression was embedded. The manual was short by the standards of the time, the treatment was twelve to sixteen weeks, and the goals were specific, current, and changeable.

### 1.2 The development of the model

The theory is described in three propositions, and a student should be able to state all three.

**Depression is a disorder of the interpersonal sphere.** The mood disorder is a response to the way in which the person's relationships are arranged, and the interpersonal difficulties are the target rather than the context.

**The role of the therapist is explicit.** The therapist is not a passive listener, not an interpreter, and not a collaborator in the exploration of the unconscious. The therapist takes an active, teaching, problem-solving role, and the relationship is warm and personal but structured, and the structure is what makes the treatment safe with a young person.

**The problems are the target and the mood improves as a consequence.** The therapist does not attempt to make the patient feel better. The therapist attempts to resolve the interpersonal problems, and the improvement in the mood is the outcome of that work, and it is a reliable outcome, and the model predicts it.

### 1.3 The evidence and the extensions

The model's evidence base is among the best in the field, and the extensions are numerous: interpersonal psychotherapy for depression, for bulimia, for social anxiety, for the PTSD, for the perinatal depression, for the grief, and for the adolescent depression.

The adolescent adaptation, which is the subject of this chapter, was developed in the 1990s and was tested in a large multisite randomised controlled trial, the Treatment for Adolescents with Depression Study, in which interpersonal psychotherapy for adolescents was one of the two treatments tested against a placebo control. The interpersonal psychotherapy arm produced significant improvements in the depression, and the improvements were comparable to those of the other active treatment, and the results are among the strongest in the adolescent depression literature.

## 2. The Four Problem Areas

### 2.1 The four areas

The model specifies four problem areas, and a student should be able to name them and describe the specific interventions associated with each.

**Grief and loss.** The death of a person to whom the young person was close, and the loss is broader than death: the loss of a relationship, the loss of a role, the loss of a place, the loss of a future. The interventions are the **complicated grief** procedure, in which the therapist works with the young person to differentiate the loss from the other things that have changed, and to restore the other things.

**Disputes and conflicts.** The relationships in which the young person is in conflict with others, most often with a parent, a sibling, or a peer. The interventions are the **dispute resolution** procedure, in which the young person is helped to identify the specific dispute, to identify the specific point of disagreement, and to work out what specifically is being asked for and what specifically is being given.

**Deficits in role transition.** The changes of role that are developmental: leaving school, moving up, a friend moving away, a parent separating, a move to a new country, the end of a relationship, the first job. The interventions are the **role transition** procedure, in which the young person is helped to identify the roles that have been lost and the roles that are being taken on, and to explore the new role and to find the new supports.

**Deficits in social skills and interpersonal deficits.** The young person's difficulty in the relationships themselves: the difficulty in making and keeping friends, the difficulty in asserting, the difficulty in engaging with a wider network, the withdrawal. The interventions are the **social skills** and **relationship enhancement** procedures, in which the young person is helped to develop the specific skills and to practise them in role.

### 2.2 The assessment

The assessment in interpersonal psychotherapy is itself a therapeutic instrument, and the structure of the assessment is what the treatment is built on.

**The diagnostic assessment.** The diagnosis, the history, the current medication, the medical history, the current safety assessment, and the developmental and social history.

**The interpersonal inventory.** This is the distinctive instrument of the model and it is completed in a specific way: the young person is asked to name the people in their life, one at a time, and for each person to rate a set of dimensions. The dimensions are the areas in which the difficulties might lie, and the young person rates each of them on a scale, and the ratings identify the problem areas.

The dimensions are: the grieving and bereavement dimension, which asks about the loss; the disputes dimension, which asks about the conflicts; the role transition dimension, which asks about the changes; the social skills dimension, which asks about the social functioning; and the interpersonal deficits dimension, which asks about the relationships that are going badly.

**The most important person.** The inventory identifies the most important person in the young person's life, and this person becomes the focus of the treatment. In the adolescent adaptation, the most important person is usually a parent, and the treatment is therefore usually a treatment of the parent–adolescent relationship, and this is the single most important structural feature of the adolescent model.

**The specific interpersonal problem.** The interpersonal inventory ends with the identification of the specific interpersonal situation that is the current focus of the treatment, expressed as a concrete, in-the-present, and behaviourally specified request. "I want my mother to let me stay out until eleven on Fridays" is a specific interpersonal problem. "My relationship with my mum is bad" is not.

### 2.3 The role and the domains

The **role** of the young person in their own difficulty is the most clinically valuable single concept in the model, and it is worth a student's attention.

The role is asked in a specific form: "When you are with your mother and she is doing the thing that you hate, what do you do? What do you feel? What do you want?" And the three answers — the behaviour, the feeling, and the want — are then related to each other. The therapeutic work is in the relation between the three.

The clinical finding is that young people in distress frequently report a discrepancy: a role that does not match their own view of themselves, or an unsuccessful strategy, or a mismatch between what they want and what they do. The **discrepancy** is the target.

The **domains** are the specific situations in which the role and the discrepancy are observed, and the domains are agreed with the young person in the first session. In the adolescent adaptation, the domains are typically the school, the peer group, and the family, and the work moves through them in a planned sequence, usually beginning with the peer group and moving to the family.

## 3. The Three Phases

### 3.1 The structure

**Phase one: the initial phase.** Weeks one to four. The tasks are: to establish the working relationship and the contract; to provide the **education about depression**, which is a specific and empirically supported intervention in its own right; to complete the interpersonal inventory; to set the agenda for the treatment; to identify the specific interpersonal problem; to identify the domains; and to begin the work on the role and the discrepancy.

The **education about depression** deserves particular attention. It is a structured component in which the young person is told, in plain language, what depression is, what it does, what it does not mean, and what the treatment is going to do, and it has a measurable effect in its own right, and it works by normalising the experience and by removing the attribution of the depression to a personal or moral failing. The content includes: the symptoms; the prevalence; the biological and the psychological and the social contributions; the fact that it is not a character defect; the fact that it is not caused by a single event; and the fact that it is treatable. A student should be able to state this list.

**Phase two: the middle phase.** Weeks five to eleven. The tasks are the focused work on the specific interpersonal problem and on the domains, and the use of the specific techniques.

**Phase three: the termination phase.** Weeks twelve to sixteen. The tasks are: to consolidate the changes; to review the progress; to identify the remaining problems and to work on them; to review the coping skills; to make the plans for the termination; and to arrange the follow-up. The termination phase is not a summary; it is a phase with its own agenda and its own tasks.

## 4. The Techniques

### 4.1 Exploration

**Exploration** is the first of the four core techniques, and it consists of three elements.

**The exploration of the interpersonal problem.** The therapist helps the young person to specify the problem in behavioural and situational terms, and the specification proceeds by asking: who is involved; what is being said; what is being done; how often; with what consequence; and what the young person wants to be different.

**The exploration of the feelings.** The young person is helped to identify the feelings, and the specific word for the feeling is elicited, and the examination of the discrepancy between the feeling and the behaviour is begun.

**The exploration of the wishes and the wants.** The young person is asked what they want from the situation, and the question is asked separately from the feelings, and the two are then compared, and the comparison is frequently the moment of insight in the session.

### 4.2 Clarification

**Clarification** is the second technique, and it is the technical heart of the model. The therapist asks the young person to describe the interpersonal situation in specific terms, and the young person is prompted to give **details**: who was there, what was said, in what order, what the other person actually said, what the young person actually said, and what happened next.

The purpose of the clarification is to move the problem from a global and abstract description to a specific and observable event, and the effect of this is often immediate and often surprising to the young person, because a global description of a hopeless situation is transformed into a sequence of specific, and sometimes tractable, events.

The therapist's stance during the clarification is active, curious, and directive: the therapist asks, "Can you tell me exactly what your mother said?" and "And what did you say back?" and "And then what did she do?"

### 4.3 The dialogue with the significant other

The third technique is the **interpersonal dialogue**, and it is a specific behavioural procedure rather than a general encouragement to talk.

**The procedure.** The young person and the therapist construct, in the session, a script of what the young person would like to say to the significant other. The script is written down, word by word, and it is rehearsed in the session, and it is then used in the real situation between the sessions.

**The construction of the script.** The script is built from the specific problem, and the questions are: what do you want to say; what do you want them to do differently; what are you afraid will happen; and what would you say if you were not afraid.

**The rehearsal.** The rehearsal is in the session, and it is the most useful part of the technique for a young person, because it is the first time the words have been said out loud, and the young person discovers what the words sound like, and the therapist helps to revise them until they are usable.

**The practice and the follow-up.** The young person tries the script in the real situation, and the next session begins with what happened.

The clinical significance is that this technique puts the young person in the position of making a specific, specified request to a person who matters, in a form that has been rehearsed, and the effect on the relationship is often substantial, and the effect on the young person's self-efficacy is often the more important effect.

### 4.4 The enactment

The fourth technique is the **enactment**, in which the young person and the therapist stage a role-play of the problematic situation.

**The procedure.** The young person plays the significant other and the therapist plays the young person, and the scene is then reversed. The purpose is to give the young person the experience of being on the other side, and the experience is often revelatory: a young person who believes that their mother does not listen discovers, in the enactment, that they are not listening either, and the discovery is available in a way that a direct argument would not have made it.

**The cautions.** The enactment is a technique from the psychodynamic tradition and it carries the risks of that tradition: it can be experienced as an intrusion, it can overwhelm a young person with a fragile sense of self, and it should not be used with a young person who is in the early phase of the work or who is very unstable. The therapist's competence in the technique is the precondition for its use, and the model's training in the technique is rigorous.

### 4.5 The other techniques

**The encouragement and the support.** The therapist gives direct, specific, and earned praise, and the practice of noticing and naming the young person's successes is a specific technique with specific rules.

**The symptom monitoring.** The young person monitors their own mood daily, on a simple scale, and brings the record to the session. The technique has an effect in its own right and it is a useful device for the beginning of a session, because it opens the conversation with something the young person has produced.

**The homework.** The interpersonal psychotherapy session is prepared with the agenda, and the homework is the practice of the specific technique between the sessions.
