
## 7. Attachment-Based Work with Children and Adolescents

### 7.1 The developmental fit

Attachment theory is unusually well matched to the adolescent period, and the reasons are worth stating precisely.

**Adolescence is the period of the working model's revision.** The adolescent's developmental task includes the revision of the expectations about others that were formed in the first three years, and the revision is possible only in the context of a relationship that is safe enough to revise in.

**Adolescence is the period of the secondary attachment systems.** The peer relationships, the teacher relationships, and the relationships with non-parental adults become the significant developmental relationships, and the adolescent's working model is revised in the context of these.

**The attachment system is at its most activatable in adolescence.** The hormonal and social changes of the period increase the salience of attachment cues, and the adolescent who is in an insecure state is at the point at which the system is most easily reorganised and also most easily overwhelmed.

**The developmental task of separating from the parents is an attachment task.** The detachment from parental control described in Chapter 33 is the developmental renegotiation of the attachment relationship, and it is easier where the working model is secure, because a secure working model permits the adolescent to be autonomous without losing the relationship.

### 7.2 The presentations

Four presentations are common in adolescent attachment work, and the practitioner should be able to recognise each.

**The preoccupied adolescent.** The young person is intensely involved with the parents and with the practitioner, is anxious at separation, is angry on reunion, and is unable to tolerate the distance that the work requires. The underlying model is that the other is available but unreliable and that needing produces anxiety, and the intervention is the consistency of the response, and the introduction of a source of care that does not withdraw.

**The dismissing adolescent.** The young person is self-sufficient, deflects, does not seek help, describes the parents as fine and their own difficulties as nobody's business, and is subtly contemptuous of emotional display. The underlying model is that needing is dangerous and that the self is worthless as a source of comfort, and the intervention is the repeated, patient demonstration that the need will be met, which takes a long time and requires the practitioner to be willing to be unhelpful in order to be reliable.

**The disorganised adolescent.** The young person's behaviour in the session is contradictory: she approaches and then withdraws, or is simultaneously warm and hostile, or is dissociate and present at the same time. The underlying pattern is the absence of an organised strategy, and the clinical significance is that the young person's behaviour cannot be interpreted as communication, and the practitioner must not try.

**The young person with no stable attachment figure.** The young person has had a series of caregivers and has no figure to whom the attachment system is directed, and the system is consequently unattached and is available for attachment to a new figure. This is the young person for whom the therapeutic relationship may be the first secure base, and it is also the young person for whom the abandonment of the therapeutic relationship will be most damaging, and the termination planning of Chapter 58 is correspondingly important.

### 7.3 Working with the caregiver

The work with the caregiver has five components, and they are the substance of the attachment-based practice.

**The observation of the interaction.** The practitioner watches the young person and the caregiver together, and the practitioner's task is to see the bids and the responses. This is the technique of the ABC video-feedback and it is the technique of the observation in the DDP model, and it is learnable.

**The naming of the bids.** The practitioner names, in the session, what the young person did and what it meant: "she moved her chair closer to you three times in the last ten minutes, and each time she did it she also looked at the door." The naming is done with the caregiver, in a way that is observational rather than evaluative, and the shift from "she is attention-seeking" to "she is seeking you and checking whether you are available" is the single most useful clinical move in the work.

**The consistency of the response.** The caregiver is helped to respond promptly and congruently, and the specific guidance is given about the two common failures: the response that is delayed and the response that is inconsistent.

**The repair.** The caregiver is taught to repair ruptures with the young person, and the repair is demonstrated and rehearsed, because most caregivers have never repaired a rupture with their own parent and do not know that it is possible.

**The caregiver's own history.** Where the caregiver has an unresolved history, the work on it is a component, and it may need to be referred to a clinician who can work with an adult's attachment history, because it is a different set of skills from those of the adolescent work.

### 7.4 The young person's developmental work

**The recognition of the need.** Much of the work with the insecure adolescent consists of helping the young person to notice their own bids, which is a considerable achievement for a young person who has learned that bids are met with rejection or with withdrawal.

**The tolerance of the need.** The young person must also learn to tolerate the need — to sit with the discomfort of needing, without immediately doing something to end it. This is the developmental work of the secure base.

**The testing.** The young person will test the practitioner, and the tests will look like provocation or sabotage. The tests are of the same kind as the tests of the infant, and the practitioner's task is to respond in a way that is congruent and non-defensive, and to notice afterwards what the test was for.

**The multiple relationships.** The developmental task of moving from the primary relationship to a wider world of relationships is supported concretely: the young person is helped to find and to sustain other relationships, in the peer world, with other adults, and the practitioner supports rather than blocks this.

### 7.5 The relationship with the practitioner

The ethical and clinical requirements of the attachment relationship with the practitioner are strict, and a student should be able to state them.

**The practitioner is available and consistent.** This is the basic requirement, and it is the hardest to provide in a service with short contacts and high turnover, and the services with the highest turnover are the ones with the most attachment-disrupted young people.

**The practitioner does not withhold.** The practitioner does not use the relationship to secure compliance, and does not withdraw to produce a change, and does not withhold to teach a lesson.

**The practitioner does not require reassurance.** The young person is not required to reassure the practitioner, and the practitioner does not ask for it, and the asking is a common error in inexperienced practitioners.

**The practitioner repairs.** The practitioner names the rupture and does not require the young person to repair it.

**The practitioner is honest about the limits.** The practitioner tells the young person about the end of the therapy, about the limits of their availability, and about what will happen if the young person is in crisis. The honesty is itself an attachment experience, and it is different from the experience of a practitioner who is reliable in the sessions and absent in the crisis.

## 8. Case Illustration: The Young Woman Who Had Learnt to Be Left

**Presentation.** Nadia is sixteen and has been placed in foster care for the second time in two years. She is referred by the school with "attachment difficulties, refuses to engage, and has already said she will not be here long." The placement is with a family she has met twice.

**The first session.** Nadia is late, and she is polite, and she is careful, and the practitioner notices the following things and writes them down. Nadia asks, in the first five minutes, how long the sessions are. She asks whether the practitioner will be there next week. She says, in the middle of the session, "I don't really do the talking thing, you should know that now." And she does not, at any point in fifty minutes, refer to anything that has happened to her.

The practitioner also observes Nadia's body, because the practitioner was trained to observe bodies, and Nadia's shoulders are raised and her voice is pitched slightly high and her hands are busy with the sleeve of her jumper throughout the session.

**The attachment matrix.** In the first family session, the matrix is read. Nadia is **dismissing** in the session: she does not seek the foster parents, does not look at them, answers their questions briefly, and presents as a young woman with no needs. The foster mother is **preoccupied**: she talks a great deal, she explains the house rules, she asks Nadia repeatedly how she is settling, she says "we just want you to feel at home", and she is visibly hurt by Nadia's flatness. And the practitioner observes the pattern in the room: the foster mother asks a question, Nadia gives a minimal answer, the foster mother asks again, and the second question is more emotional, and Nadia becomes more closed.

The matrix reading is: **the young person is managing the adults' anxiety, and she is doing it by being self-sufficient.** The foster mother's anxiety produces the second question, the second question produces the withdrawal, and the withdrawal produces the foster mother's hurt, and the hurt produces more checking. Nothing in the system is a behaviour problem. Everything in the system is the attachment system in operation, in a young person who learned in her first fourteen years that the reliable response to an adult who is anxious is to stop needing anything.

**The formulation.** The working model, as the practitioner reconstructs it from the school records, the previous placement's notes, and Nadia's own fragmentary account, contains a model of the other as unavailable and a model of the self as not worth the trouble. The specific history is not yet known and the practitioner does not invent it; the first task is to establish it, carefully, over time, and at Nadia's pace.

**The intervention.** Five elements.

*Element one: the foster parents' preparation.* Before the second session, the practitioner meets the foster parents alone and explains the attachment matrix reading in plain terms: "Nadia is not being cold. She is being careful. And the reason she seems cold is that she has learned that when adults worry, they ask again, and when they ask again, they get upset, and when they get upset she ends up having to manage their feelings." The practitioner tells them what the work will require: consistency, patience, a small number of bids being answered, and a great deal of repetition before anything changes. The foster mother cries at this point, and the practitioner does not interpret the crying, and this is itself a demonstration of the practice.

*Element two: the change in the foster parents' responding.* The specific instruction is: "Ask once. If she answers, that is the end of the question. If she says nothing, that is also the end of the question. And then, in the evening, tell her one thing you noticed about her that is not about school or about the house rules — that she ate all of the lasagne, that she was reading on the sofa, that she laughed at something — and then stop talking." The purpose of the evening message is to establish that the contact continues when the young person has not initiated it, without the young person having to do anything to end it.

*Element three: the placement question.* The practitioner addresses the placement's framing directly. The statement in the school referral that Nadia "will not be here long" is a prediction, and the prediction is a self-fulfilling one, and the practitioner says so to the team: "If we expect her to leave, we will read everything she does as evidence that she is leaving, and she will pick that up in about a week." The team agrees to a review at three months rather than at six weeks.

*Element four: the individual work.* The work with Nadia is slow and consists of three things. First, the practitioner is boring and reliable: the same time, the same room, the same ten minutes of talking about nothing at the start. Second, the practitioner names the bids when they occur, gently and without pressure: "You asked me twice whether I would be here next week." Third, the practitioner does not require any disclosure. The practitioner explicitly says: "You do not have to tell me anything about what has happened. That is not what this is for. What I am for is being here."

*Element five: the developmental work.* Two pieces. The first is the finding of an adult outside the family: a youth worker at the school, a specific and reliable person, and the practitioner introduces them and then steps back. The second is the introduction of the peer world, which Nadia has given up entirely, and the work is to get one, low-demand social activity — a youth group, a sports session, a shift at the school café — with the specific and deliberate aim of giving her a relationship with a peer that is not a performance.

**The outcome.** At three months Nadia has not disclosed anything about her history, and the practitioner regards this as a good outcome and says so to the team. She has stopped asking whether the practitioner will be there. She has come to three of the youth group sessions and has spoken to two of the other young people. The foster mother reports that "she's still not affectionate but she comes down for dinner now, and she told me about a song."

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the attachment framework in practice and it makes two points that a student should take away. The first is that the young person's "refusal to engage" was a competent and adaptive response to a history in which engaging was dangerous, and the label was describing the coping and calling it the pathology. The second is that the most important clinical event in the three months was not a disclosure and was not a breakthrough; it was the provision of a predictable adult and the removal of the double question, and the durable change in a young person with a disrupted attachment history is built out of exactly that.

## 9. Evidence and Research Support

### 9.1 The status of the evidence

Attachment theory has a large and rigorous research base, and the evidence for the interventions derived from it is good but narrower than the theory.

**The attachment research is highly reliable.** The strange situation and the Adult Attachment Interview are among the best-validated measures in the developmental sciences, with high inter-rater reliability, extensive normative data, and a large body of longitudinal research. The prediction of attachment classification into adulthood is among the more robust findings in the field.

**The longitudinal findings are strong.** Attachment classification in infancy predicts a range of outcomes at adolescence and in adulthood, with the largest effects in the socially and emotionally at-risk groups, and the effects are largest for the insecure classifications.

**The ABC intervention has good support** for infants and toddlers, and the video-feedback mechanism is understood.

**The CFTSI has good support** for young children with trauma and for their caregivers.

**The DDP has good support**, with a randomised controlled trial showing significant reductions in self-harm in the borderline population, and a body of mechanistic work on the PACE stance and on the development of the mentalizing capacity.

**Mentalization-based treatment has the strongest evidence base** of all the attachment-based approaches for adolescent borderline personality disorder, and Chapter 42 develops it.

**The evidence for attachment-based work with the broader adolescent population is thin.** This is the honest position: a young person with an insecure attachment pattern and no diagnosed disorder is unlikely to be referred to an attachment-based treatment, because the presentation is not severe enough, and the evidence for the general application of attachment principles to adolescent presentations is limited to the clinical trials of the specific protocols.

### 9.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The measures are Western and the categories may not transfer.** The cross-cultural literature is substantial and the most serious methodological finding is the context-dependence of the caregiver's behaviour in the strange situation, and the field's resolution of it is partial.

**The adult and the child measures are not the same construct**, and the moderate correlation between the two has been over-interpreted in the clinical literature.

**Attachment classification is a group-level description**, and the predictive power at the level of the individual is considerably weaker than the group-level figures suggest, and this is a point of statistical sophistication that is important for a practitioner who is being told that a young person's category predicts their future.

**The intervention research is concentrated in infancy and early childhood** and in the borderline population, and the adolescent range is comparatively under-researched.

**The outcomes measured are largely behavioural and attachment-related**, and the mental health and the quality of life outcomes of the attachment-based interventions are less extensively reported.

## 10. Strengths and Limitations of the Attachment Perspective

### 10.1 Strengths

**It is developmentally founded and empirically supported.** The theory is not a metaphor and its measures are among the best in the field.

**It explains why the same young person behaves differently with different adults**, which is one of the most practically useful things a theory can do for a practitioner.

**It provides a formulation of the young person as adaptive**, which is both more accurate and more useful than a formulation of the young person as defective.

**It is practical.** The bids, the responses, the consistency, and the repair are learnable and they are usable in any modality.

**It locates the target in the relationship**, which is where the developmental evidence points.

### 10.2 Limitations

**It under-accounts for the individual.** A young person with a neurodevelopmental condition, with a psychotic illness, or with a specific learning difficulty has difficulties that the attachment account does not explain.

**The categories are Western and their universality is contested**, and the practitioner who applies them cross-culturally without adjustment will misdescribe.

**The adult categories are frequently misapplied to young people**, and the adult attachment interview categories do not translate into the child categories.

**The relationship between attachment and the individual-differences measures is weaker than the clinical literature implies**, and the attachment story has sometimes been used to explain more than it can.

**The interventions are demanding.** The DDP requires a trained team and a stable placement, and the CFTSI requires trained parents and a video-feedback setup, and the services in which the young people who most need this work are found are frequently not resourced for it.

## 11. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 11.1 The cultural specificity of the attachment categories

The cross-cultural literature on attachment is one of the most methodologically instructive in the developmental sciences, and a student should know its central finding.

**The strange situation is not culturally neutral.** It was developed in a specific social context, and the classification depends on the cultural meaning of the stranger's behaviour and on the caregiver's familiarity with the procedure.

**The caregiver's response must be coded separately.** The most important methodological correction was to recognise that the strange situation measures the child's response to the reunion and that the caregiver's response must be coded independently, and that a caregiver's response that is coded as "insecure" in one setting may be coded as "secure" in another, because the meaning of the behaviour differs.

**The distributions differ across cultures.** Some studies have found higher proportions of the insecure classifications in certain settings, and the interpretation of these differences is contested, and the field's position is that the differences may reflect the circumstances of caregiving in those settings — the instability of the caregiving arrangements, the pressure on the caregiver, the absence of supports — rather than a deficiency in the caregivers.

**The categories may be a poor fit where caregiving is collective.** Where the primary caregiving figure is a kin network rather than an individual, the strange situation's assumptions about the singular attachment figure and about the exclusive relationship do not hold, and a practitioner who applies the categories will misdescribe the family.

**Practices that are optimal for attachment are not universally optimal.** The practices that produce a secure attachment — the consistent and sensitive response, the reliable caregiver, the stable routine — are good things in most circumstances, and in a setting where the family is under severe pressure they are additional demands on a caregiver who is already overwhelmed, and the clinical judgement required is to support the caregiver's capacity before expecting the practice of it.

### 11.2 Ethical considerations

**The therapeutic relationship as an attachment relationship.** The ethical status of the attachment relationship with the practitioner is a live question. The attachment-based approach treats the relationship as the treatment, which is powerful and which raises a question about the nature of the obligation when the relationship ends. A practitioner who has been the young person's secure base has a duty of care in the ending that goes beyond the ordinary termination, and the practice of the attachment-based approaches is to prepare for the ending from the beginning.

**The prohibition on the use of the relationship to secure compliance.** This is stated explicitly in the protocols and it is a genuinely different ethical position from the one in the behavioural tradition, and the two traditions can be in direct conflict in a case. A practitioner working with the behavioural approach of Chapter 26 and the attachment approach of this chapter must resolve the conflict explicitly, and the resolution will usually be that the attachment relationship is not to be used to produce the behaviour change, and that the behaviour change is not to be produced at the cost of the relationship.

**The status of the caregiver in placements.** A young person in a placement has a legal guardian who is not the attachment figure, and the conflict between the guardian's authority and the attachment relationship is a real and frequently mishandled difficulty.

**The young person's right to refuse.** The attachment approach must not become a means of compelling disclosure. A young person who declines to talk about her history has the right to do so, and the practitioner who pursues disclosure as a condition of the work has turned the secure base into a condition.

**Cultural and religious considerations in the caregiver's response.** The practices that the attachment literature recommends may conflict with a family's religious or cultural practices, and the practitioner should establish what those practices are before the intervention, and should not require the family to abandon them.

**Confidentiality and the caregiver's access.** The attachment-based approach requires the practitioner to share observations with the caregiver, and this must be negotiated explicitly with the young person.

**The avoidance of re-traumatisation.** The work with a young person with a disrupted history must not require the young person to revisit the history in order to prove that they have worked, and the practitioner must be prepared for the young person's own decision about how much to disclose, including the decision to disclose nothing.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Attachment** — A behavioural system organised around the child, activated by the need for protection, and functioning to provide a secure base for exploration and a refuge from threat.
- **The functions of the attachment system** — Protection, proximity maintenance, a secure base for exploration, affect regulation, and the basis for the self.
- **The behavioural control systems** — Attachment, feeding, and avoidance and exploration, each with its own functions and its own interaction with the others.
- **The three conditions of attachment** — Availability, sensitivity, and affective investment; all necessary and none sufficient.
- **Sensitivity** — The caregiver's accurate responsiveness to the child's signals, as distinct from a perfectly synchronised responsiveness, which produces a child with no capacity to manage the states that arise when the caregiver is unavailable.
- **Affective investment** — The caregiver's emotional investment in the child, and the child's recognition of it.
- **The critical period** — The period in the first eighteen months to two years during which the attachment relationship is formed.
- **The sensitive period** — The period after the critical period in which attachment can still be formed, but more rigidly.
- **Monotropy** — Bowlby's proposal that the infant forms a single, primary attachment and that other relationships are secondary.
- **The secure base** — The function of the attachment figure as the platform from which the child explores and to which the child returns.
- **Internal working model** — The set of expectations drawn from the primary relationship about the availability of others, about the response to one's needs, and about the worthiness of the self.
- **The three components of the working model** — A model of the attachment figure, a model of the self, and a model of the interaction.
- **The developmental trajectory of the working model** — Formation in the first three years, elaboration to six, deliberate reflection from six to eleven, and organised differentiation from eleven onwards.
- **The secondary revision of the working model** — The adolescent's revision of the working model, permitting multiple relationships of different kinds and mutability in the other.
- **Strange situation** — The laboratory procedure developed by Ainsworth and colleagues, consisting of eight episodes in which the caregiver and a stranger are present or absent.
- **Secure attachment** — The child who explores readily, is distressed on separation, is readily comforted on reunion, and does not avoid the caregiver.
- **Insecure-avoidant attachment** — The child who does not distinguish the caregiver from the stranger, shows little separation distress, and avoids the caregiver on reunion; a defensive strategy against unreliable availability.
- **Insecure-ambivalent attachment** — The child who is preoccupied with the caregiver, explores little, is highly distressed at separation, and is angry on reunion.
- **Disorganized attachment** — The contradictory, frozen, or dazed behaviour in the presence of a caregiver whose behaviour is frightening or whose history is unresolved; the "fright without solution".
- **Adult Attachment Interview** — The semi-structured interview about the adult's own childhood, scored for the quality of the discourse and producing the adult attachment categories.
- **Secure, preoccupied, dismissing, and unresolved** — The four adult attachment categories, derived from a different instrument from the child categories and only modestly correlated with them.
- **The attachment matrix** — A clinical instrument plotting the young person's behaviour in the session against the caregiver's attachment signals, read in four quadrants.
- **Attachment-based family therapy (ABC)** — The parent-mediated intervention for infants and toddlers, using video-feedback to teach the caregiver to see the child's bids.
- **Child and Family Traumatic Stress Intervention (CFTSI)** — The extension of the ABC work to the treatment of trauma in young children aged three to six and to their caregivers.
- **Dyadic developmental psychotherapy (DDP)** — The parent-child attachment intervention for attachment trauma and for emerging or established borderline personality disorder.
- **PACE** — The stance of playfulness, acceptance, curiosity, and empathy from which the dyadic developmental psychotherapy is delivered.
- **Follow your lead** — The responsive interaction technique in which the practitioner enters the young person's world and reflects back their intentions with delight.
- **The recognition of the bid** — The practice of noticing and naming the small signals by which a young person seeks proximity, contact, or reassurance.
- **Repair** — The practice of naming a rupture in the relationship and taking responsibility for it, without requiring the young person to reassure the practitioner.
- **The prohibition on withholding** — The requirement that the attachment figure be available precisely when their availability is not needed for the practitioner's purposes.
- **The preoccupation with the small** — The developmental tendency of the adolescent period that makes the attachment system both more activatable and more reorganisable.
- **The four adolescent attachment presentations** — The preoccupied, the dismissing, the disorganised, and the young person with no stable attachment figure.
- **Attachment and the school context** — The application of the attachment principles to the school relationship, the classroom, and the school-based support.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Attachment theory is the developmental foundation of much of this book's practice, and its central claim is that attachment is a behavioural system with four functions: protection, proximity maintenance, a secure base, and affect regulation, and with the basis for the self as its developmental product. The chapter traced the history from Bowlby's break with psychoanalysis through the ethological argument, the critical period and monotropy proposals, and the three controversies that remain live — the categorical versus dimensional question, the cross-cultural universality of the categories, and the relation between the child and the adult measures.

The three conditions of attachment were described and their separability was noted, because the three failures produce three different presentations and require three different interventions. The internal working model was described in its three components and its developmental trajectory, with the adolescent's revision of the model identified as the developmental task of the period and the clinical error of interpreting the adolescent's relationship difficulties as a regression.

The attachment patterns were described in both the child and the adult categories, with the warning that the two derive from different instruments measuring different constructs and are only modestly correlated. The attachment matrix was described as a practical clinical instrument, with the four quadrants read and their interventions given.

The five principles of attachment-based therapy were stated, the five main variants were described with their evidence, and the five core practices were set out: the recognition of the bid, the response, the repair, the prohibition on the use of the therapeutic relationship to produce compliance, the consistency of the response, and the work with the caregiver's own history.

The work with young people was developed through the developmental fit, the four presentations, the five components of the work with the caregiver, the developmental work with the young person, and the strict requirements of the relationship with the practitioner, including the prohibition on requiring reassurance and the honesty about the limits of the availability.

The case of Nadia demonstrated the whole framework. The referral's "refusal to engage" was read, on the attachment matrix, as a young person managing the adults' anxiety by being self-sufficient, and the double question in the foster home was identified as the mechanism. The intervention was the preparation of the foster parents, the change in their responding, the challenge to the placement's self-fulfilling prediction, the slow individual work that did not require disclosure, and the developmental work of finding an adult outside the family and a peer world. The outcome at three months was no disclosure and the practitioner treating that as a good outcome.

The evidence was presented with its structure: the attachment research is highly reliable and longitudinal, the intervention evidence is good but concentrated in infancy and in the borderline population, and the evidence for the general application of attachment principles to the adolescent range is thin. The limitations were named, including the statistical point that the prediction at the individual level is considerably weaker than the group-level figures suggest.

The cultural section addressed the cultural specificity of the categories, the context-dependence of the strange situation, the different distributions across cultures, the poor fit where caregiving is collective, and the point that the practices that are optimal for attachment are not universally optimal and may be additional demands on a caregiver who is already overwhelmed. The ethical section addressed the ethical status of the attachment relationship and the duty of care that persists beyond the ending, the prohibition on using the relationship to secure compliance and the resulting conflict with the behavioural tradition, the status of the guardian in a placement, the young person's right to refuse disclosure, the cultural and religious considerations in the caregiver's response, and the avoidance of re-traumatisation.

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 34.1 — The Attachment Matrix (75 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, observe — or reconstruct from a placement — a family session involving a young person and their caregiver. Code the young person's behaviour in the session on the secure, preoccupied, dismissing, and disorganised axes, and code the caregiver's on the secure, preoccupied, dismissing, and unresolved axes. Read the resulting quadrant and write the intervention that follows from it. Then write the specific observation that would make you revise the reading. The exercise teaches both the instrument and the humility that the instrument requires.

### Activity 34.2 — The Bid Diary (five days, individual)

For five days, record every small bid you can observe in one relationship — a family member's, a friend's, a colleague's — and what the response was. Record the bid, the response, and whether the response was prompt and congruent. Then, with a second person, swap diaries and read theirs, and identify the bids that you would have missed. The exercise builds the observation skill that the whole attachment-based practice depends on, and the second stage, reading another person's record, is where the learning of what a bid looks like actually happens.

### Activity 34.3 — The Repair Rehearsal (60 minutes, pairs)

One student plays a sixteen-year-old who has just been told something painful by the practitioner, and the other plays the practitioner who has mis-handled it. The young person withdraws. The practitioner's task is to repair: to name what happened, to take responsibility, and not to require the young person to reassure them. Then reverse roles. Then write down the three things the practitioner said, and assess each against the requirement that the repair not be a request for reassurance. Most practitioners, including the students, make the repair into a request for reassurance in the first attempt, and the exercise is designed to make that visible.

### Activity 34.4 — The Cultural Audit of a Category (60 minutes, individual)

Take a case from placement and apply the attachment categories, and then audit each judgement against the cultural and material context of the family. For every classification, ask: what does this caregiver's behaviour mean in this family, in this community, in this household, given the pressures they are under? Then write the revised formulation, using the caregiver's own account of the situation. The most common finding is that at least one of the four categories was an artefact of the practitioner's expectation rather than a description of the child, and the exercise is the most direct preparation for the cultural work in Chapter 48.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory.** Read Bowlby (1969) for the foundational statement and Bowlby (1988) for the clinical papers, which is the most useful single book for a practitioner. Then read Ainsworth and colleagues (1978) for the strange situation.

**For the developmental argument.** Read Cassidy and Shaver's handbook for the comprehensive state of the field, and Crittenden (2009) for the most usable developmental summary.

**For the clinical work.** Read the DDP manual for the PACE stance and the "follow your lead" technique, and read the CFTSI manual. Then read the video-feedback literature, which is the technique most applicable to practitioners who do not work with infants.

**For the evidence and the critique.** Read the longitudinal attachment studies, the DDP and MBT trials, and the critical reviews of the attachment measures. The critical reviews are essential: the field's own handling of its instruments' limitations is a model of professional self-criticism.

**For the cultural question.** Read the cross-cultural attachment literature, especially the work on the strange situation's context dependence and on caregiving in non-Western settings, and read the literature on attachment in families experiencing poverty and migration. The cross-cultural material is not a supplement to the theory; it is part of the theory.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is bowlby, Ainsworth, and the translation of attachment research into treatment. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is family patterns, attachment, boundaries, communication, stories, and relational resources. The mechanism to keep in view is how interaction sequences and shared meanings distribute distress, protection, responsibility, and possibility across a system. The characteristic professional risk is blaming families, exposing a young person in unsafe joint sessions, or ignoring material conditions and individual needs. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in family meetings, school consultations, care networks, and individual sessions informed by systems thinking, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Tobias is fifteen and is seen in an integrated school and health team for difficulty trusting adults after several service changes. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Tobias's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Tobias explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on family patterns, attachment, boundaries, communication, stories, and relational resources.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Tobias chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Tobias has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Tobias can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Attachment Theory and Attachment-Based Therapy to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
