
## 6. Emotion-Focused Work with Children and Adolescents

### 6.1 The developmental fit

Emotion-focused therapy is unusually well matched to the adolescent period, and a student should be able to give the reasons.

**Adolescence is the period of the highest emotional intensity in the human life course.** The affective neuroscience of the period is fairly consistent: the reactivity of the emotional system is at its peak, the regulation capacity is still developing, and the gap between the two is at its widest. A model that works directly with emotion is working with the adolescent's actual experience rather than around it.

**The developmental task of adolescence is the construction of a new self, and the emotions are the material.** The shame, the grief for the childhood, the anger at the limits, the anxiety about the future, the exhilaration of the first relationships — these are not symptoms. They are the content of the developmental work, and the model treats them as such.

**The adolescent's capacity to reflect on their own emotions is newly available.** The concrete operational stage of Piaget's sequence and the corresponding increase in metacognitive capacity mean that a young person at fourteen or fifteen can, in the right conditions, do the work the model asks. This is a substantially different picture from the child of eight, and it is why the model's adolescent adaptations are viable.

**Emotion-focused work is a good fit for the presentations that most often reach a school or community service.** The young person who is angry, who is ashamed, who is grieving, who is anxious, who cannot say what they feel, and who is in trouble — these are the presentations in which the model's core question is most directly useful: what is the core emotion, and what need has it arisen from?

### 6.2 The specific adaptations

**The assessment phase is longer and the targets are negotiated.** The model assumes an assessment in which the core emotion and the pattern are identified. With an adolescent, the practitioner should expect to spend two or three sessions on the assessment, and should be honest with the young person about the length, and should be explicit that the assessment is not a delay before the work but a part of it.

**The core emotion is often not the presenting emotion.** A young person who presents with anger may have a core emotion of shame, and a young person who presents with anxiety may have a core emotion of grief. The assessment's task is to get beneath the presenting emotion, and the presenting emotion is frequently the secondary emotion that is doing the blocking.

**The work with the parents is essential and is often the difference between success and failure.** This is the single most important developmental modification. The adolescent's capacity to be with their own emotions is being continuously undermined by the responses of the adults around them, and the work with the parents is a prerequisite for the individual work rather than an adjunct to it. A practitioner who works with a distressed adolescent in a household where the adults cannot tolerate the adolescent's distress is doing work that will not hold.

**The pace must be slower and the arousal lower.** The change point requires the client to sustain a high level of arousal, and a young person with a dysregulated nervous system — and many do — cannot sustain it, and pushing for the change point with a young person who is already at their limit produces a collapse rather than a change. The preparation phase of Chapter 40 is not optional for many of the young people who present.

**The resolution must be named and recorded.** The new emotional state at the end of the session should be explicitly noticed, and where possible written down, and a young person who has had a peak of distress followed by a moment of calm has had an experience that is worth keeping.

### 6.3 The presentations

Four presentations are particularly suited to the model.

**The young person with a chronic anger problem.** The anger is nearly always a secondary emotion blocking a primary emotion, usually grief or shame, and the treatment is to work beneath the anger rather than to manage it.

**The young person with a physical presentation.** A young person who comes to the clinic with headaches, with abdominal pain, with a rash, with a sleep disturbance, and with no medical explanation, may be presenting a primary emotion in a somatic form, and the somatic work of Chapter 43 is the natural companion to this chapter.

**The young person with an eating presentation.** The emotion-focused model has been applied to both the restrictive and the bingeing presentations, and the eating behaviour is typically functioning to manage a primary emotion that the young person cannot otherwise tolerate.

**The young person with a relational problem.** The model's interpersonal procedure, in which a specific interaction with a significant other is imagined and worked through, is particularly useful with the young person whose difficulties are concentrated in a relationship with a parent, with a peer, or with a partner.

### 6.4 The limits

**The model requires the capacity to be with a feeling.** A young person with a very restricted range, with high dissociation, or with a severe and longstanding trauma response may not be able to access and sustain the core emotion, and the preparatory work of Chapter 40 must come first.

**The model can be experienced as a demand to feel.** A young person who has been told by adults to "calm down" for fifteen years may experience the invitation to sit with a feeling as another demand, and the practitioner should name this possibility in advance.

**The model can be experienced as the practitioner doing the emotional work.** The stance of "being with" the client's emotion is a demanding stance for the practitioner, and the practitioner who is managing their own distress rather than sharing it will be detected, and a young person will stop bringing the feeling.

## 7. Case Illustration: The Young Woman Who Was Always Angry

**Presentation.** Tia is fifteen, and the referral from school describes a young woman who is "aggressive with staff, swearing, and defensive". Her mother describes a young woman who "explodes over nothing" and who has become "a different person since her grandmother died in July". The school has had three exclusions.

**The first session.** The practitioner begins with the emotion coaching, not with the assessment, and the reason is that the assessment will not proceed with a young person who is in the state Tia is usually in.

The practitioner says: "I want to start with something simple. You've come in looking like you're ready to fight, and I wonder if that's actually about something else." Tia says, sharply: "It's about whatever you want to make it about." The practitioner reflects: "So it's about not being messed with." Tia says: "It's about being *treated*." And then, unexpectedly, she tells the practitioner that the school has been telling everyone she is a "problem" and that she has read the comments in the file, and that her own mother has said "you were exactly like your dad" and that her dad is in prison.

**The second session.** The practitioner returns to the anger. Tia says: "Everyone expects me to be angry, so I am." The practitioner asks what she is underneath it, and Tia says: "Tired." And the practitioner works with the tired for a while, and Tia says: "I am so tired of being the one that's wrong, and I can't even be sad about Gran because then I'm the one who's sad and everyone's got enough."

**The assessment.** Over the next two sessions, the practitioner works on the assessment, and the assessment identifies the pattern with a precision that surprises Tia.

The core emotion is **grief**, for a grandmother who died in July after a long illness, and Tia had been the primary carer, having spent most of her fifteenth year in hospitals and at her grandmother's bedside.

The secondary emotion is **anger**, which is what Tia has been presenting, and the anger has been blocking the grief, and the anger has been doing two things: it has kept Tia from having to feel the grief, and it has been the only available response to a set of situations in which Tia is treated as the problem — including, most significantly, by her own mother, whose grief for her own mother has been expressed as anger at Tia.

The appraisal at the change point is the meaning of the loss: that the people she cared for are taken, and that nobody has asked her about it, and that being angry is the only way she has found to be listened to.

**The intervention.** Four elements.

*Element one: the reframe for Tia.* The practitioner does this explicitly and in a direct way: "The anger isn't the problem. The anger is what you have been doing instead of the grief, and it is the only way you have found to get anybody to listen. And I want to say something else, which is that the things people have said to you — the comments in the file, your mum saying you're like your dad — those are not descriptions of you. Those are them not knowing what to do with a young woman whose gran died."

*Element two: the work with the mother.* This is the part of the plan on which the outcome will turn, and it is the hardest. The practitioner sees the mother alone, and the mother's account is that she has been "strong for Tia" and has not cried in front of her. The practitioner asks the mother about her own mother, and the mother cries, and the practitioner stays with her, and this is the first time anyone has asked the mother about her own loss. The specific change requested of the mother is: to stop the comparison with Tia's father entirely, and to tell Tia once, directly, that she is grieving too, and to stop asking Tia to be "strong" or "the strong one".

*Element three: the emotional work with Tia.* The practitioner works toward the change point over two sessions, with the preparation work of the previous sessions doing the work of establishing that Tia can stay with a feeling. The peak arrives in the fourth session, when Tia says, in a voice the practitioner has not heard from her: "I miss her. I miss her so much and nobody will let me say it because they all think I'm angry." The practitioner stays with her, does not interrupt, and does not reassure, and Tia cries for a long time, and at the end of the session Tia is quiet, and the practitioner asks what she is noticing, and Tia says: "I think I'm just tired, but it's a different tired to before."

*Element four: the school.* The practitioner writes to the school with Tia's agreement, and the letter is short and it says that Tia is grieving, that the exclusions are not helping, and that the school could be part of the treatment by allowing Tia to be absent for two days for the funeral that Tia was not permitted to attend. The school agrees, and Tia attends the funeral, and the practitioner does not overstate the significance of this, because Tia is not yet over anything.

**The outcome.** Over the following two months, Tia has one further exclusion, which follows an incident in which a younger student made a comment about her grandmother. Tia's mother tells the practitioner that they have been to the cemetery together twice. The school reports that Tia is engaging in lessons. The anger has not gone, and the practitioner says clearly that it is not expected to go, and that what has changed is what it is for.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the model's central technique. The presenting emotion was not the core emotion; the core emotion was identified through the secondary; the change point required both the emotional and the cognitive components, and the practitioner had to build the conditions for the cognitive component — the appraisal that the loss meant and that nobody would listen to it — before the peak arrived; and the most important intervention was not with Tia at all but with her mother, whose grief and anger were sustaining the pattern. The model predicts that the individual work will not hold without the systemic work, and this is the model's most practically useful claim for work with young people.

## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The status of the evidence

Emotion-focused therapy has one of the better research bases in the psychotherapy literature, and the student should be able to describe its structure.

**The process research is the strongest part of the base.** The finding that emotional change in session predicts improvement, and that it predicts it better than cognitive change, has been replicated across a range of presentations and across a range of therapists, and it is one of the more robust process findings in the field.

**The controlled trials are good.** The randomised trials of emotion-focused therapy against cognitive therapy and against treatment as usual have reported significant effects, with the emotion-focused treatment at least equal to the cognitive treatment and sometimes superior, and the effect sizes are large.

**The brief treatment claim is supported.** The model was developed as a brief treatment, with a specified number of sessions, and the trials support the efficacy of the time-limited protocol.

**The evidence base is smaller than that of the cognitive and behavioural therapies**, and the reason is partly the difficulty of the fidelity measurement and partly the smaller number of practitioners.

**The adolescent evidence is limited.** Most of the research has been with adults, and the developmental adaptations have been developed largely from clinical experience and from the related emotion-coaching research with parents of young children. A student should be candid about this.

**The emotion coaching research base is good.** The parental emotion coaching research has a substantial evidence base for reducing the emotional dysregulation of young children, and the effects are mediated by the parents' own regulation capacity.

### 8.2 Limitations of the evidence

**The trials are mostly by the model's developers and their close colleagues**, and the allegiance effects are correspondingly large.

**The fidelity measurement is difficult**, and the procedures are detailed enough that delivery varies considerably.

**The adolescent and the clinical populations with the most complex presentations are under-researched.**

**The measure of emotion change in session is a judgement-based rating**, and the research on the predictive power of it is robust but the construct is not independent of the observer's expectation.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Emotion-Focused Therapy

### 9.1 Strengths

**It is direct and it is brief.** A specified number of sessions, a specified target, and a specified procedure are all things a young person can be told, and a young person who is tired of being talked at will often prefer it.

**It treats the emotion as data.** The model does not pathologise the feeling, and a young person who has been told that their anger is a symptom is often relieved to discover that the anger is a response.

**It is compatible with everything else in this book.** The emotion-focused work can be combined with behavioural, cognitive, systemic, attachment, and trauma-informed work, and the combination is often the most useful.

**It identifies the secondary emotion.** This is a genuinely useful clinical skill, and it applies well beyond this model: the presenting complaint is frequently not the target.

**It locates the need.** The move from "what is the problem?" to "what need is this emotion in service of?" is one of the most useful questions a practitioner can ask, and it is applicable in almost any context.

### 9.2 Limitations

**It requires a capacity that some young people do not have**, and the preparatory work it assumes is not always done.

**The model is under-specified in its account of the emotional system.** It is a clinical model of emotion rather than a developmental or a neurological one, and a student who wants the latter should read the affective science.

**It can be experienced as a demand to feel.** The work with the emotion has to be prepared for and negotiated.

**The research base is narrow.** The adolescent evidence in particular is thin.

**It is vulnerable to practitioner error.** The procedures are simple and the pitfalls are not.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 10.1 Culture and the expression of emotion

The model's account of emotion is universalist, and a student should be able to state where it is culturally bounded.

**The expression of emotion is culturally regulated.** In many cultures, particular emotions — anger, in particular, and in some contexts sadness and grief — are subject to strict regulation, and the display of them brings consequences. A young person from a family in which the expression of anger is dangerous will not access the anger in the consulting room, and the practitioner who works toward the change point regardless will produce a young person who complies in the room and shuts down outside it.

**The display rules differ and the practitioner must learn them.** The rules about what may be expressed, to whom, and in what form are learned early and are enforced by the family and the community. The practitioner's task is to establish them before the work, and the question to ask the family is not "how does this young person express anger?" but "what happens in your family when someone is angry?"

**The categories of the model are themselves culturally patterned.** The taxonomy of the primary, secondary, and solution emotions was developed in a specific clinical culture with a specific repertoire of expressed affect, and the mapping of a young person's experience onto it requires a translation, and the translation should be done with the young person rather than to them.

**Grief and its rituals.** The model works with grief, and the rituals of grief are culturally determined and often specific, and a young person whose family's mourning practice is elaborate and public will need the practitioner to work within it rather than to substitute a Western individual process.

### 10.2 The politics of the model

The model is individual. The central question — "what emotion is this, and what need is it in service of?" — locates the difficulty in the young person's own emotional life, and a practitioner who asks it relentlessly can inadvertently individualise a difficulty that is produced by the young person's circumstances.

The correction is straightforward and the practitioner should be able to state it: **the need is real and the response of the environment to the need is also part of the picture.** A young person whose need for safety is frustrated by a school that is unsafe is not to be helped by processing the emotion; she is to be helped by the school becoming safe. A young person whose need for a stable home is frustrated by unstable housing is not to be helped by attending to the grief.

### 10.3 Ethical considerations

**The consent to the work.** A young person should know what the treatment involves, including that the practitioner will be asking them to stay with difficult feelings, and this should be said in advance.

**The regulation of the arousal.** The change point requires a high level of arousal, and a young person with a dysregulated nervous system, with a seizure disorder, with a cardiac condition, or with a history of an overwhelming response needs the preparatory work of Chapter 40 first, and the practitioner must screen for this rather than discover it in the session.

**The limits of the practitioner.** The "being with" stance is a demanding one, and the practitioner needs their own support and their own regulation, and a practitioner who is dysregulated will dysregulate the client. This is a supervision matter and it is a matter of professional self-care.

**The relationship with the parents.** The work with the parents must be transparent to the young person, and the boundaries between what is shared and what is not must be explicit, and the young person must be told what is said about them.

**The prohibition on the emotional pressure of the practitioner.** A practitioner who is Determined to reach the change point in a particular session is applying pressure, and the pressure is a form of coercion, and it can produce a compliance that is not a processing.

**Confidentiality and the disclosure of the core emotion.** A young person may disclose a core emotion in a session that the practitioner shares with the parents, and the disclosure is the treatment, and it must be protected.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Emotion-focused therapy** — The brief, structured treatment developed by Greenberg and colleagues in which the client's emotional experience is the primary datum and the focus of the work.
- **Emotion as the primary datum** — The model's foundational claim that emotion, rather than cognition, behaviour, or the relationship, is the primary object of the work.
- **Emotion as a response to a need** — The account in which the primary emotions are organised in relation to the needs for secure attachment, autonomy, control, approval, worth, connection, and identity.
- **The needs framework** — The seven needs in relation to which the primary emotions are organised, and the clinical question of which need a given frustration has activated.
- **Emotion as adaptive** — The proposition that every emotion serves a purpose, and that the problematic emotions are misdirected, excessive, or aimed at the wrong target.
- **Primary emotions** — The adaptive responses to the frustration of a need: anger, sadness, fear, shame, joy, and the sensations that accompany them.
- **Secondary emotions** — The feelings that arise in the reaction to the primary ones and that function to block the primary emotion's processing: guilt, depression, shame, and anxiety.
- **The blockage** — The secondary emotion that prevents the processing of the core emotion, and the usual target of the work.
- **Emotion schemes** — A generalisation from a specific emotional response, and the level at which the model locates the enduring vulnerability.
- **Solution emotions** — Pride, confidence, calm, and the sense of the other as a potential ally, understood as the forms of emotional transcendence.
- **Emotional transcendence** — The highest form of emotional life, in which the self is expanded beyond the narrowness of the immediate situation.
- **The change point** — The moment in the processing of a core emotion at which the client is most amenable to change, and the model's central technical contribution.
- **The two components of the change point** — The emotional component, the experiencing of the core emotion at its peak, and the cognitive component, the accessing of the core appraisal.
- **The core appraisal** — The meaning of the situation for the core need, which determines the emotion and which must be accessed for a change to occur.
- **The emotional processing steps** — Attune and focus, confirm and clarify, the empathic congruence, facilitate the expression, and reach the resolution.
- **The "being with" stance** — The practitioner's congruent presence with the client's emotion rather than its management.
- **Emotion coaching** — The procedure of empathising, labelling, validating, and then problem-solving with the client, and the strategy of doing so with parents of young children.
- **The empathy step** — The practitioner's empathic statement of the client's emotional state, offered as a hypothesis the young person can correct.
- **The labelling step** — Naming the emotion with a non-pejorative label, introduced gradually.
- **The validation step** — Stating the legitimacy of the emotion in the situation, and of the emotion rather than the behaviour.
- **The problem-solving step** — Identifying the problem and then the choices, and the requirement that it come after the emotion is processed.
- **The parental emotion coaching hierarchy** — Empathise, validate, label and limit, and then problem-solve.
- **The thinking, feeling, and doing question** — The diagnostic question of whether the client's problem is primarily a thinking, a feeling, or a doing problem, and the intervention it determines.
- **The focus of attention procedures** — The set of techniques in which the client is guided in attending to a particular aspect of their internal experience.
- **The intrapersonal technique** — The processing of the client's experience of their own emotion in the presence of the practitioner.
- **The interpersonal technique** — The processing of a specific interaction with a significant other, imagined and worked through in the session.
- **The preparatory phase** — The work on regulation and stabilisation that must precede the processing phase for a young person with a dysregulated nervous system.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Emotion-focused therapy is a brief, structured treatment in which the client's emotional experience is the primary datum, and its founding observation — that clients change when they can identify, experience, and express their core emotions — was a direct response to the failure of the cognitive therapies with clients whose difficulties are not in their thoughts. The chapter traced the two foundations, the research programme in British Columbia that identified in-session emotional change as the best single predictor of improvement, and the reasons for the model's relatively limited clinical adoption, which are instructive: the requirement for a capacity to access feeling, the requirement for trained practitioners, and the under-specification of the account of emotion.

The core principles were developed: emotion as the primary datum, with the other elements in its service; the four components of the model's account of emotion, as a response to a need, as adaptive, as tolerable and changeable, and as transcending; the categories of primary, secondary, and solution emotions and the emotion schemes, with the observation that the clinical target is very often the secondary emotion because it is the secondary emotion that generates the presenting complaint; and the position on the mutual determination of cognition and emotion, with the practical diagnostic question of whether the problem is a thinking, a feeling, or a doing problem.

The change point was described in detail, with the concept, the two components, the practitioner's task as a sequence, and the five steps of the emotional processing. The critical technical point was made that a practitioner who produces the emotional component without the cognitive component has produced a discharge rather than a change.

The in-session emotion coaching was described with its four procedures and the parental hierarchy, and the two developmental modifications that make it usable with adolescents — the adolescent's own label and the adolescent's own solution, and the use of the coaching through the parent where the parent's response is undermining the adolescent's capacity.

The developmental fit was developed through the four reasons, and the specific adaptations were described: the longer and negotiated assessment, the identification of the core emotion beneath the presenting emotion, the essential work with the parents, the slower pace and lower arousal, and the naming and recording of the resolution. The four suited presentations and the three limits were given.

The case of Tia demonstrated the model. The presenting anger was identified as a secondary blocking a grief, the appraisal at the change point was the meaning of the loss and of not being listened to, the practitioner built the conditions for the cognitive component before the peak arrived, and the decisive intervention was with the mother, whose grief and anger were sustaining the pattern. The model's prediction that the individual work will not hold without the systemic work is its most practically useful claim for work with young people.

The evidence was presented with its structure: the process research is the strongest part of the base, the controlled trials are good, the brief treatment claim is supported, the parental emotion coaching research is good, and the adolescent evidence is limited and developed largely from clinical experience. The limitations were named.

The strengths and limitations were set out, with the strongest strengths being the directness, the brevity, the treatment of emotion as data, and the identification of the secondary emotion, and the principal limitations being the capacity the model requires, the under-specified account of emotion, the thin adolescent evidence, and the vulnerability to practitioner error.

The cultural analysis addressed the cultural regulation of emotional expression, the need to learn the family's display rules before the work, the cultural patterning of the model's own categories, and the cultural specificity of the rituals of grief. The politics of the model were addressed with the correction that the need is real and the environment's response to it is also part of the picture. The ethical requirements covered the consent to the work, the regulation of the arousal and the screening that it requires, the limits of the practitioner and the need for supervision, the transparency of the parental work, the prohibition on the emotional pressure of the practitioner, and the confidentiality of the disclosure of the core emotion.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 37.1 — The Secondary Emotion Audit (60 minutes, individual)

Take a real case from placement and write the presenting emotion, the possible core emotion, and the specific secondary emotion that is blocking the core one. Then write the evidence for each: what in the account supports the identification, and what would make you revise it. Then write the appraisal — the meaning of the situation for the core need — in one sentence in the young person's own words. The final step is the one that distinguishes the model from a general empathy exercise, and most students find it the hardest and the most useful.

### Activity 37.2 — The Emotion Coaching Rehearsal (60 minutes, triadic)

One student plays a nine-year-old who is furious because a friend would not share a toy; one plays the mother, who is herself at her limit; one observes. The mother practises the four-step hierarchy: empathise, validate, label and limit, problem-solve. The observer's task is to note which steps were skipped and to note the mother's own emotional state as she attempts each. The last observation is the important one, because it is what Chapter 40 and the parent work of Chapter 33 are for.

### Activity 37.3 — The Change Point Sequence (90 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, take a case and write out the full sequence to the change point: the core emotion, the blockage, the work with the blockage, the approach to the peak, the emotional component, the cognitive component, and the resolution. Then write the specific words the practitioner would use at each step. Then write what the practitioner would do if the young person did not reach the peak. The last part is the practical reality of the work, and it is the part that is least often discussed in the literature.

### Activity 37.4 — The Cultural Display Audit (60 minutes, individual)

Take a young person from a family whose emotional rules differ from your own and write the family's display rules: which emotions may be expressed, to whom, in what form, and what happens when the rules are broken. Then write what this means for the work: what the young person is likely to show in the session, what they are likely to withhold, and what the practitioner would need to establish before the change-point work. Then write the ethical implication. The exercise is the most direct preparation for the cultural work in Chapter 48.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the model.** Read Greenberg and Rice (2001) for the process work and Greenberg and colleagues (2003) for the clinical procedures, and read Greenberg (2015) for the contemporary statement.

**For the emotion coaching.** Read Cooper and Golan (2013) for the adolescent applications and the Gottman material for the parental work. The emotion coaching is the most practically usable element of the model and the easiest to learn.

**For the process research.** Read the studies on in-session emotional change and on the change point. The process research is what justifies the model and it is more convincing than the outcome trials.

**For the wider emotional science.** Read the affective neuroscience literature on the adolescent period and on the developmental trajectory of the emotional system, and read the developmental emotion regulation literature. The model is a clinical model and the science is what tells you when it is safe to use it.

**For the critique.** Read the critical accounts of the emotion-focused model's account of emotion and the evidence limitations, and read the cultural critiques of Western affect models. The model is more useful when its boundaries are known.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Emotion-Focused Therapy** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is greenberg's model of emotion regulation, the change point, and emotion-focused work with youth in distress. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work. The mechanism to keep in view is how threat, arousal, avoidance, meaning, and relational safety shape what a young person can feel, remember, and learn. The characteristic professional risk is moving into traumatic material before stabilisation, confusing calm with shutdown, or making disclosure the price of care. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in stabilisation sessions, trauma-informed schools and services, caregiver work, and specialist treatment pathways, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Amara is fifteen and is seen in a family support service for sleep disruption, irritability, and falling participation. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Amara's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Amara explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on safety, regulation, emotion, memory, relationships, and paced therapeutic work.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Amara chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Amara has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Amara can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Emotion-Focused Therapy

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Emotion-Focused Therapy** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Emotion-Focused Therapy** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Emotion-Focused Therapy**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Emotion-Focused Therapy to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
