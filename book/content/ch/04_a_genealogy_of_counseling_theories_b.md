## 6. The Systemic Turn, 1950–1975

The systemic turn is the most consequential intellectual development in the history of counseling, and it is the development most often reduced to a list of techniques.

### 6.1 The Palo Alto group

The intellectual origin of the systemic turn was the work of Gregory Bateson and the research group at the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto in the 1950s. Bateson's group, studying the communication of families of children diagnosed with schizophrenia, made a series of observations that together constituted a revolution in the understanding of human difficulty.

**The double bind** described the situation in which a person receives mutually contradictory messages, at two levels, and is thereby placed in a position in which any response is punished.

**The "I'm OK — You're OK" communication** described a metacommunication — a communication about a communication — by which one party defines the terms on which the other party's messages are to be interpreted. Bateson's insight was that the pathological communication was not in the content of the messages but in the rules governing their interpretation.

**The "everybody's against me" and "the smart one" positions** — a schizophrenic family described in terms of communication roles rather than of intrapsychic conflict.

**The Palo Alto strategic therapy** that followed from this work — Jackson's behavioural problems as learned communication, Haley's therapeutic double bind, Weakley's and Satir's and Minuchin's and Ackerman's divergent developments from the same group — reframed the symptom as a communication to be deciphered and, in the strategic approaches, as a problem to be solved by directive means.

### 6.2 Bowen's family systems theory

Murray Bowen's contribution was different in kind, and more durable. Bowen's genius was to connect the family system to the individual and to do so in a way that could be traced genealogically. His concepts — the differentiation of self, the levels of emotional process, the intergenerational anxiety continuum, the nuclear family emotional process, the family projection process, the emotional triangle, the sibling position, and the Bowen family systems theory as a general framework for the assessment of a person in the context of their emotional system — place the individual inside a multigenerational emotional field, and place that field inside a differentiation continuum ranging from the emotional fusion of the family of origin to the emotional cutoff of a highly differentiated individual.

Bowen's framework also produced the concept of the **genogram**, a three-generation map of relationships, emotional triangles, and boundaries, which is now one of the most widely used assessment tools in the field and is described in Chapter 30.

### 6.3 The structural turn

The structural family therapies of the 1960s and 1970s — Minuchin in Argentina and at Yale, and, independently, Salvador Minuchin's and Virginia Satir's and Salvador Minuchin's and Paul Ackerman's — differed from the strategic approaches in a crucial respect: they were **not** interested in the symptom's communicative function, but in the structure of the family that produced it. Minuchin's structural model described the family as a system with subsystems, boundaries, hierarchies, and rules of interaction, and the therapist's task as the restructuring of the boundaries and hierarchies. Minuchin's account of the "under-involved" and "enmeshed" family and of the developmentally appropriate handling of the adolescent's demand for autonomy from a family that cannot tolerate it is the direct ancestor of the contemporary work on parents and adolescents in Chapter 33.

### 6.4 The consequences of the systemic turn

The systemic turn changed the unit of analysis, the site of intervention, the conception of the symptom, and the training of practitioners all at once. It is also the point at which the profession's enduring ethical difficulties were created, and these are discussed in Chapter 3 and in Chapter 48: the redefinition of the symptom as functional, the possibility of working against an individual's stated wish, and the difficulty of protecting a vulnerable member of a system that is itself being treated.

## 7. The Integrative Turn, 1970–1995

The 1970s and 1980s saw a significant decline in the sectarianism of the 1960s and the emergence of a working pluralism.

**Theoretical integration as a technical problem.** The works of Larry Beauchamp, of William Glasser, of Lester Floor and the eclectic movement, and the work of the integrative theorists who followed them — the technical eclecticism of Arnold Lazarus, the theoretical integration of Wayne Watson and the authors of the 1991 volume on integrating therapies, and the later work of the "psychotherapy integration" research group — attempted to specify the conditions under which two theories could be combined without incoherence. The criteria that emerged, and that Chapter 51 adopts, are: that the integrated approach be grounded in a shared metatheory; that it be based on a shared understanding of the person and of pathology; that it use a shared set of concepts; and that it be based on a shared model of change, including a shared theory of the therapeutic relationship and a shared set of strategies for change.

**The common factors movement.** The clinical and theoretical argument that the common factors were the true determinants of outcome, advanced most vigorously by Jerome Frank and later by Bruce Liese and Bruce Wampold, became in the 1980s and 1990s a major research programme. The finding that the quality of the therapeutic alliance is the most consistent predictor of outcome across therapies and that specific techniques contribute less than their proponents claim was a genuinely important advance, and it was also, as Chapter 6 argues, frequently over-read.

**The training model and the counselselling model.** The 1980s and 1990s saw the consolidation of the two doctoral training models, the counselling psychology model and the clinical psychology model, within a single accreditation framework (the APA's 1995 accreditation standards) and a period in which the professional identities of counselors and psychologists in training became the subject of intense and occasionally bitter dispute. The Council for Accreditation of Counseling and Related Educational Programs, formed in 1994 and first accredited in 1996, created a separate accreditation pathway for the counseling professions, and with it a distinct professional identity with a distinct code of ethics. The history of that development is the history of the "licensure compact" question, and it continues to shape the profession's politics.

**The managed care era.** The arrival of managed care in the 1990s transformed the conditions of practice in the United States and had a substantial effect on theory, because it made short-term, symptom-focused, measurable interventions economically necessary, and because it made the "evidence-based" label a condition of payment. The consequences for theory were direct: the emphasis on specific, manualised, time-limited, outcome-monitored treatment that characterised the 1990s and 2000s was not solely a scientific development. It was also, and in part, a response to the economic conditions of the market.

**The evidence-based movement.** The parallel development of evidence-based practice, the empirically supported treatment movement, and the Cochrane-style systematic reviews of psychotherapy gave the field a genuine empirical foundation and simultaneously contributed to a form of intellectual narrowness that the contemporary field is only beginning to correct. The overstatement of the evidence base has been documented extensively, and the corrections are set out in Chapter 7.

## 8. The Third Wave, 1990–2015

The "third wave" describes a family of developments that began in the 1980s and 1990s and that share a common critique of the second wave: that by treating cognition, behaviour, and emotion as targets to be directly modified, the second-wave therapies had taken the symptoms of psychological difficulty too seriously and had inadvertently reinforced a model in which the self was a set of faulty components requiring correction.

The third wave is best understood as a **reformulation of the relationship between acceptance and change**.

**Acceptance and commitment therapy.** Steven Hayes and colleagues developed ACT from a relational frame theory in which the central human problem is described as "cognitive fusion" — the entanglement of one's thoughts and feelings with the sense of oneself, such that the content of a thought is taken to be the truth of the person, and the resulting struggle with unwanted internal experiences is itself the mechanism that maintains distress. ACT's central metaphor is the struggle switch: efforts to control unwanted thoughts and feelings produce rebound effects that increase rather than decrease distress. The ACT approach is to develop psychological flexibility, defined through six processes: acceptance, defusion, contact with the present moment, the self-as-context, values, and committed action. The clinical relevance of ACT for this course is that it is explicitly hostile to the assumption that symptoms must be reduced, and it has a growing evidence base with adolescents, including in school settings and in the treatment of disordered eating and of substance use.

**Dialectical behaviour therapy.** Marsha Linehan's 1993 manual described a comprehensive treatment for chronic self-harm and suicidality built on two core strategies — dialectical strategies that maximise acceptance and change simultaneously, and validation strategies that make clear to the client that their emotions and responses make sense, while also providing problem-solving strategies that teach new skills. The treatment assumes a client population for whom the usual therapeutic relationship is inadequate, and the behavioural analysis of the suicidal behaviour, the "problem-solving" orientation, and the consultation team model were all innovations with respect to what was standard in the 1990s. Chapter 21 examines the adolescent adaptations.

**Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and mindfulness-based stress reduction.** Jon Kabat-Zinn's work from the late 1970s at the Massachusetts General Hospital medical clinic, and Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale's 1990s randomised trial of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy in the prevention of relapse in recurrent depression, brought a distinctive practice into the field — a practice of non-judgemental present-moment awareness — and a distinctive theoretical claim, that changing one's relationship to thoughts is a route to change that does not require changing the content of thought. Chapter 22 addresses both the treatment and its developmental cautions.

**The functional contextual approach.** The functional contextual approach, developed principally by Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson and given its most systematic clinical expression in the contextual behavioural therapies, provides the theoretical account of the third wave's central claim: that psychological suffering is a function of the individual's struggle with internal experiences in the context of a life that is not behaving in accordance with the individual's values. The approach also supplies the "five senses of the therapist's behaviour" — evocation, freedom, presence, attunement, and potency — which is a valuable framework for the personal and relational practice of any orientation.

**The third wave's contribution.** The third wave's central contribution to the field has been to establish that the aim of therapy is not the eradication of symptom but the construction of a life worth living, and that a young person may be helped considerably by learning to live with distress that is not to be removed. Its central controversy is whether the emphasis on acceptance undermines the behavioural principle that symptom reduction is itself therapeutic, and the controversy has not been resolved.

## 9. The Contemporary Turn, 2010 to the Present

The most recent period has produced four developments that a graduate student in this course must know.

### 9.1 Trauma-informed and phase-oriented practice

The recognition of the prevalence of traumatic experience, the accumulation of research on the neurobiology of trauma, and the failure of the field to respond adequately to the needs of trauma-exposed populations have produced a distinct body of practice organised around three principles — safety, trustworthiness and transparency, and peer support — and around a phased model of treatment in which stabilisation precedes trauma processing, which precedes integration. The trauma work of Judith Herman, of Bessel van der Kolk, of Pat Ogden, and of the developers of EMDR, of prolonged exposure, of cognitive processing therapy, and of skills-based stabilisation protocols has produced the field's most important methodological advances of the last forty years. Chapters 38, 39, and 40 develop this material.

### 9.2 The somatic and the autonomic

The recognition that many of the presentations that bring young people to counseling are organised at the level of the body — hyperarousal, dissociation, somatic complaints, difficulty with regulation — has produced the somatic, sensorimotor, and polyvagal-informed approaches, and the movement to integrate affect and interoception into the theory of change. The developmental research on the maturation of the prefrontal cortex and of the autonomic nervous system, and the clinical work on the window of tolerance, have provided a neurobiological account that is directly relevant to the sequencing of interventions with young people. Chapters 37 and 43 develop this material.

### 9.3 Social justice, cultural responsiveness, and intersectionality

The reframing of multicultural counseling from a concern with the cultural background of the client to a concern with power, privilege, and the social production of distress has transformed the ethical requirements of the profession. The intersectional framework, the analysis of structural racism in the aetiology of adolescent mental health, the attention to the mental health effects of poverty, of racism, of immigration, of gender and sexual minority stress, and of disability, and the development of a distinctly anti-oppressive practice have made the accountability of the practitioner to the social context a central ethical demand. Chapters 48 and 49 develop this material.

### 9.4 The integration of the digital

Teletherapy, which was a necessity during the global pandemic and which is now a permanent component of the delivery system, has required a generation of practitioners to think about the medium as a theoretical variable: about the loss of the somatic and spatial cues of the in-person encounter, about the security of the home environment, about the presence of parents in the room, about the implications for confidentiality, about digital inequity, and about the question of whether the alliance can be formed in a medium that was never designed for it. Chapter 55 addresses this.

## 10. Four Lessons from the Genealogy

**Lesson one: every theory is a response to a specific problem, and the problem is usually still with us.** The cognitive therapies respond to the limits of behaviourism; the humanistic therapies to the limits of psychoanalysis; the systemic therapies to the limits of the individual focus; the third wave to the limits of symptom reduction as a criterion; the contemporary somatic and trauma work to the limits of the talk-based assumption. A student who can name the problem can predict the theory's blind spots.

**Lesson two: theoretical succession is driven by populations and contexts, not by refutation.** The family therapies emerged because individual therapy did not work well with the problems children actually brought; the third wave emerged because the second wave's methods were experienced by many clients as coercive; the somatic approaches emerged because talk-based therapy did not reach the bodily dimension of many presentations. A theory that is the best available approach for a population and a context is likely to be superseded by a theory that suits a different population or a different context, and the superseded theory is not thereby wrong.

**Lesson three: the techniques outlive their philosophies, and this is both a strength and a danger.** The most useful techniques in the field — exposure, behavioural rehearsal, thought records, the empty chair, the genogram, the miracle question, grounding — are used by practitioners of many orientations, and the portability of these techniques is a practical blessing. It is a danger because a technique detached from the theory that made it effective often lacks the rationale that made it work, and because a technique used outside its developmental range can be ineffective or harmful.

**Lesson four: the profession's recurring error is a claim of aetiological completeness.** Each new theory has tended to present itself as the correct account of the whole of psychological difficulty, and each has been shown to be an account of some part of it. The contemporary field's most mature position is the pluralistic and integrative one developed in Part VIII, and this chapter's history is the reason for it: the evidence of the genealogy is that every single-system account has been partial.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Genealogy** — An account of the descent of a theory or practice that asks what specific problem it is a response to, rather than simply when it appeared.
- **Residue of succession** — The visible retention in a later theory of assumptions inherited from the theory it displaced.
- **Theoretical succession** — The displacement of a dominant theory by a new one, generally driven by an unmet need rather than by refutation.
- **Prehistory of technique** — The philosophical assumptions encoded in a therapeutic procedure, which remain operative after the procedure is transferred between theories.
- **Moral treatment** — See Chapter 2; the nineteenth-century doctrine underlying the profession's founding moral claim.
- **The looking-glass self** — Cooley's concept of the self as constituted through the imagined perceptions of others; an ancestor of the constructivist account of identity.
- **Law of effect** — Thorndike's principle that behaviours followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened; the foundation of the operant tradition.
- **Two-factor theory of avoidance** — Mowrer's account of avoidance behaviour as acquired through classical conditioning and maintained by negative reinforcement; the foundation of the modern exposure therapies.
- **ABC model** — Ellis's rational-emotive model in which the activating event leads through beliefs to consequences, with the event itself not being the cause of the emotional consequence.
- **Personal constructs** — Kelly's theory that each person constructs an idiosyncratic personal theory of the world through a process of bipolar alternatives, and that psychological disorder is a consequence of a non-adaptive construct system.
- **Double bind** — Bateson's account of a communication in which a person receives mutually contradictory messages and is penalised for any response.
- **Metacommunication** — A communication about a communication, which in the Palo Alto formulation carries the operative rules of a relationship.
- **Family projection process** — Bowen's account of the transmission of emotional processes from parents to children.
- **Differentiation of self** — Bowen's concept of the degree to which a person maintains a distinct sense of self while remaining emotionally connected to others.
- **Genogram** — A multigenerational map of family relationships, emotional triangles, and boundaries, used for assessment.
- **Structural family therapy** — The approach, principally associated with Minuchin, that treats the family as an organisation with subsystems, boundaries, hierarchies, and rules.
- **Third wave** — The family of therapies from the 1990s onward that reformulated the relationship between acceptance and change, including ACT, DBT, and mindfulness-based approaches.
- **Cognitive fusion** — The ACT concept of the entanglement of thought with the sense of self that makes the content of a thought feel like the truth of the person.
- **Psychological flexibility** — The ACT construct describing the capacity to remain in contact with present experience and to act in accordance with values while unwanted internal experiences are present.
- **The five senses of the therapist's behaviour** — Evocation, freedom, presence, attunement, and potency; a framework from the functional contextual approach for the personal conduct of the practitioner.
- **Window of tolerance** — The range of arousal within which a person can engage in flexible, integrated response, above which the person is hyperaroused and below which the person is hypoaroused.
- **Polyvagal theory** — The contemporary neurophysiological account of the three-tiered autonomic response to threat, and of the social engagement system.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

A genealogical account of counseling theory asks not when a theory appeared but what problem it answered. Read this way, the history of the field is a sequence of displacements, each driven by an unmet need rather than by refutation, and each leaving a visible residue in its successor.

The field descends from the late nineteenth-century movements in clinical psychology, the measurement of individual differences, the child study movement, pragmatist philosophy, and psychoanalysis; it acquired its professional form between 1940 and 1960; it divided along the humanistic–behavioural axis in the 1950s; it was transformed by the cognitive turn, the systemic turn, and the integrative turn; and since 1990 it has been reshaped by the third wave, by trauma-informed and somatic practice, by social justice and intersectionality, and by the digital delivery of care.

Four general lessons follow: every theory answers a specific problem that is usually still with us; succession is driven by populations and contexts; techniques outlive their philosophies, which is both a practical blessing and a danger; and the profession's recurring error is the claim that any single account is complete. That last lesson is the historical justification for the integrative stance developed in Part VIII.

### Activity 4.1 — The Line of Descent (2 hours, individual, then seminar)

Choose one contemporary technique in your work — thought recording, exposure, the genogram, the miracle question, grounding, or any other. Trace it backwards through at least four steps to the moment of its invention, naming the problem it was invented to solve at each step. Write the account in 800 words. Then state which of those problems is still with you in practice, and what that implies about the technique's likely usefulness to you.

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the history itself.** Read the founding texts in chronological order, with attention to what problem each was written to solve. The order that will be most instructive is: Watson (1913), Freud (1900/1905), Adler (1927), Rogers (1942, 1957), Maslow (1943), Skinner (1953), Kelly (1955), Bateson (1956), Ellis (1962), Beck (1976), Minuchin (1974), Linehan (1993), Hayes (2004). Reading them in this order, a student will watch the field's central question — what is a person, and therefore what is a difficulty — being asked and answered in roughly nine different ways.

**For the historiography.** Read the professional history literature rather than the theoretical surveys: the accounts of the founding of the professional bodies, of the accreditation contests of the 1980s and 1990s, and of the managed care era. The most instructive of these accounts combine institutional history with an awareness of the political economy of mental health services.

**For the contemporary turn.** Read the primary works in each of the four contemporary developments, and then read one critical review of each, written by someone in a different theoretical camp. The critical reviews are what turn a reading list into professional judgement.

**For the exercises.** Activity 4.1 is the most valuable of the three. Do it for a technique you actually use, and bring the line of descent to the seminar; the discussion is invariably better than the individual writing.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **A Genealogy of Counseling Theories** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is a historical map from the late nineteenth century to the contemporary integrative era. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is conceptual clarity, evidence appraisal, therapeutic relationship, and disciplined formulation. The mechanism to keep in view is how a theory turns observations into explanations, predictions, interventions, and reviewable outcomes. The characteristic professional risk is mistaking an elegant explanation for evidence, or applying a preferred theory before the young person has been understood. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in the first counselling sessions, assessment meetings, and case discussions, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Tunde is fifteen and is seen in a community adolescent clinic for grief following a major family change. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Tunde's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Tunde explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on conceptual clarity, evidence appraisal, therapeutic relationship, and disciplined formulation.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Tunde chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Tunde has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Tunde can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for A Genealogy of Counseling Theories

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **A Genealogy of Counseling Theories** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **A Genealogy of Counseling Theories** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **A Genealogy of Counseling Theories**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain A Genealogy of Counseling Theories to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
