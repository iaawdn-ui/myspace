# Operant Conditioning and Contingency Management

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the principles of operant conditioning and the four contingencies, and apply them to a real situation.
- Describe the schedule of reinforcement typology and specify an appropriate schedule for a given clinical objective.
- Explain the difference between reinforcement schedules and token economies, and between token economies and behaviour contracts.
- Describe the full procedure of contingency management, from the specification of the target behaviour to the review of the plan.
- Explain the design and the use of behaviour contracts, and the legal and ethical limits of contracts with minors.
- Apply differential reinforcement, shaping, and fading to a clinical problem.
- Describe the management of a behaviour that is maintained by escape, and explain why punishment so often fails.
- Plan a contingency management intervention in a school or clinic, including the training of the adults who will carry it out.
- Critically evaluate the ethical and cultural dimensions of contingency management with young people.

## Chapter Overview

Operant conditioning is the branch of the behavioural tradition that deals with behaviour that operates on the environment. The vocabulary it has produced — reinforcement, punishment, extinction, shaping, fading, the schedules — is the shared language of most of applied behaviour analysis, most of the behavioural interventions for parents, and a good deal of school practice.

Its central insight is that behaviour is selected by its consequences. This is a claim about the environment rather than about the person, and it immediately suggests an intervention: if a behaviour is selected by a particular consequence, then changing the consequence changes the behaviour. Nothing about the behaviour itself needs to be inspected, interpreted, or treated.

The second insight is that the world in which the young person lives is a set of contingencies, and that these contingencies are usually maintained by adults who are not aware of them. The task of the practitioner is to map them, and the mapping frequently produces surprises: the behaviour that has caused the most concern is often the behaviour that is currently being reinforced, and the cooperation that has been requested is often the cooperation that is being withheld.

The third insight is more technical and more consequential than it first appears: the *schedule* on which a consequence is delivered determines the behaviour it produces, and the schedule is the variable that a great many real-world interventions get wrong. A token economy that pays for a behaviour once a week produces a very different young person from one that pays for it every time it occurs.

This chapter presents the operant principles in their technical form, the schedule typology in the detail in which it is actually used, and the three principal clinical applications — differential reinforcement and shaping programmes, contingency management in a setting, and behaviour contracts — with the adaptations that young people require and the ethical constraints that apply to each.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 Thorndike's cats and the puzzle box

The operant tradition begins in Thorndike's puzzle box, and the shape of the experimental work determined the shape of the clinical practice that followed. A cat that stepped on a pedal and escaped received, in Thorndike's design, a fish; a cat that failed to step received nothing, or received an unpleasant consequence. The cat learned over time, and the learning curve recorded the improvement.

Three features of this design are the direct ancestors of contemporary practice.

**The consequence is immediate.** The fish followed the pedal press within seconds. Everything in the modern operant literature about immediacy, and about the superiority of a small immediate consequence over a large delayed one, follows from this.

**The behaviour is measured by its frequency.** Thorndike timed the escape, and the recording of a rate of responding under specified conditions is the foundational technique of the whole applied tradition.

**The learning is inferred from the environment, not from the animal.** Thorndike's law of effect made no reference to intention, satisfaction as a felt state, or purpose. The cat did not act in order to escape; it escaped because acting in that way had paid off.

### 1.2 Skinner and the operant

Skinner's contribution was to make the analysis of behaviour into a research programme and then into a discipline. Three elements of his system are foundational for clinical work.

**The operant is defined by its effect.** An operant is a behaviour that operates on the environment and produces a consequence. This definition makes the behaviour itself, and not the mental state, the object of analysis, and it makes the analysis of a behaviour identical to the analysis of the environment in which it occurs.

**The three-term contingency.** The behaviour is always specified in relation to a **discriminative stimulus** (the occasion on which the behaviour occurs) and a **consequence**. The full relation is Sᴰ → R → Sᴿ: the antecedent signals that a particular response will be reinforced; the response occurs; the consequence follows. Clinical work with young people is, in the most useful sense, the specification and the alteration of three-term contingencies.

**The principle of the functional analysis of behaviour** — that behaviour is selected by its consequences and is therefore analysable in terms of the contingencies that produce and maintain it.

Skinner also introduced the systematic experimental apparatus, the operant chamber, in which behaviour is measured across a series of carefully manipulated conditions. The chamber is not a piece of equipment that a counsellor uses; it is the model of the clinical environment, in which the practitioner arranges conditions, measures behaviour, and manipulates consequences deliberately.

### 1.3 The transition to the clinic

The application of operant principles to human problems began in the 1940s and 1950s in three settings: the treatment of chronic mental illness in institutional settings, where the modification of Token Economy systems in the late 1950s was a landmark; the modification of classroom behaviour; and the parent-training literature of the 1960s, which developed the operant account of oppositional behaviour into the most practically influential behavioural treatment in the child mental health field.

The institutional work produced the first systematic contingency management programmes and, with them, the first ethical controversy, because the early Token Economy systems in psychiatric wards used the withdrawal of privileges as a primary tool and applied them inconsistently. The controversy led to the field's most durable contribution: the analysis of contingencies, which shows that a pattern of escalating behaviour is very often maintained by the escape contingency that the pattern itself creates.

The parent-training literature was the most consequential for the present book. The demonstration that oppositional and conduct behaviour in young children is frequently maintained by a pattern of adult escalation — the more the child escalates, the more the adult withdraws the demand, so that the escalation is rewarded — was one of the most practically useful findings in the entire behavioural literature, and it is the foundation of the parent training evidence described in Chapter 33.

### 1.4 Behaviour analysis in the schools

The application of operant principles in the classroom has a long history, and the three main approaches are worth distinguishing because they are still in use and they are often confused.

**Token economy** — a system in which desired behaviour is reinforced with tokens exchangeable for backup reinforcers, applied to a group or a class.

**Contingency management** — a negotiated restructuring of the reinforcement structure of a setting, in which the specific consequences for specific behaviours are defined, taught, and reviewed.

**Behaviour contracting** — a written agreement, between a young person and an adult or an organisation, specifying what each will do, with specified consequences, and with the young person's signature.

All three are forms of the same underlying operation, and all three appear in Chapter 28, which addresses their use in schools and clinics specifically.

## 2. Reinforcement in Clinical Practice

### 2.1 The specification of a reinforcer

A reinforcer is a stimulus that, when made contingent on a behaviour, increases the future frequency of that behaviour. The definition is behavioural, which means that the question "is this a reinforcer?" is answered empirically and not by appeal to what ought to be motivating. A young person may value an item that the practitioner considers trivial, and may be unmoved by an item that the practitioner considers valuable, and the practitioner's opinion is irrelevant to the question.

The practical consequence is that reinforcers must be established. Three methods are used together.

**Establishment by observation.** The young person is observed in a setting in which the behaviour in question is already frequent, and the consequences that follow it are recorded. A young person who talks continuously in one class and is rewarded with attention there has a reinforcer available.

**Establishment by interview.** The young person is asked directly what they want, what they like, what they will work for, what they would spend their money on, what they would do for nothing. The answers are taken seriously even when they seem trivial.

**Establishment by testing.** A small amount of the candidate reinforcer is delivered, and the effect on the behaviour is observed. This is the only method that settles the question, and it is under-used.

### 2.2 The reinforcer survey

The **reinforcer survey** is a structured interview in which the young person, the parents, and the teachers are each asked the same set of questions about what the young person likes, wants, finds interesting, spends time on, and will work for. The three sets of answers are compared.

The comparison frequently produces the most useful single finding in a behavioural assessment. The young person wants X; the parents are willing to give Y; the teacher can actually deliver Z. Where these three sets do not intersect, the plan will not work, and the discovery is usually made only by asking all three.

### 2.3 Natural reinforcers and the motivational operations of the setting

The most durable plans use consequences that are already part of the young person's life. A child who enjoys drawing will work for drawing time far more reliably than for a token exchangeable for sweets, because the drawing does not have to be procured, scheduled, and administered, and because the value of it does not decline with repetition in the way that the value of an administered treat does.

This is the practical form of the account of **motivating operations** given in Chapter 23. The relevant question is not "what does this young person like?" but "what is scarce in this young person's current environment, and what would be abundant if it were provided?" Scarcity is what makes a stimulus reinforcing, and the practitioner's best move is often to provide something that is plentiful in the young person's world outside the clinic and scarce in the clinic.

### 2.4 Reinforcement and the young person's dignity

A systematic and sustained programme of reinforcement has an obvious dignity cost, and the practitioner should anticipate it. A young person who is rewarded for talking, and paid for it in tokens, may learn to talk for the tokens and may find the relationship with the practitioner humiliating.

Four practices reduce the cost without reducing the effectiveness.

**Reinforce the behaviour, not the person.** The young person earns the consequence for the behaviour, and the practitioner never indicates that the value of the young person is conditional.

**Use a range of reinforcers**, so that the interaction is not a transaction.

**Move towards natural consequences** as rapidly as possible, because natural consequences carry no dignity cost at all and generalise better.

**Fade the artificial system deliberately**, as a planned phase of the treatment, rather than abandoning it, which is experienced as a loss.

## 3. Schedules of Reinforcement

### 3.1 The four schedules

The schedule on which a reinforcer is delivered determines the behaviour it produces. Ferster and Skinner's typology is the standard description, and it remains the most useful single tool in the behavioural repertoire for matching a contingency to an objective.

**The fixed-ratio (FR) schedule** delivers the reinforcer after a fixed number of responses. FR 5 means the fifth response is reinforced, and then the count restarts. The characteristic pattern is a **post-reinforcement pause** followed by a high, steady rate: the pause after the reinforcement, then rapid responding until the next reinforcer. FR schedules produce high rates of response with brief pauses.

**The variable-ratio (VR) schedule** delivers the reinforcer after a variable number of responses, unpredictable to the organism. The characteristic pattern is a high, steady rate with no pauses, and this is the schedule that produces the most persistent behaviour. Gambling, and the use of slot machines, are the familiar examples, and the reason that a gambling schedule is so resistant to extinction is that the organism cannot distinguish a long run of losses from a schedule that has merely been quiet.

**The fixed-interval (FI) schedule** delivers the reinforcer for the first response after a fixed time period has elapsed. The characteristic pattern is a **scallop**: a pause after the reinforcement, then a gradually accelerating rate of response as the time approaches. FI schedules produce moderate rates with a distinctive temporal pattern, and they are the schedule that generates the "waiting until the last moment" behaviour that teachers and employers find so familiar.

**The variable-interval (VI) schedule** delivers the reinforcer for the first response after a variable time period. The characteristic pattern is a moderate, steady rate with no pauses, and this is the schedule that produces the most persistent and most stable behaviour of the four.

### 3.2 The schedule and the clinical objective

The typology is directly actionable, and the following table is the working summary that a student should carry into practice.

| Schedule | Behaviour produced | Clinical application |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed ratio | High rate, brief pause after reinforcement | Token systems, work-based reinforcement, discrete-trial programmes |
| Variable ratio | High, steady rate, highly persistent | Attendance in a group, completing a session, sustained effort tasks |
| Variable interval | Moderate, steady rate, most stable | Ongoing attention, monitoring, being available, homework checking |
| Fixed interval | Moderate rate, scalloped, "last minute" | Periodic check-ins, scheduled reviews, homework collection |

Two clinical consequences follow immediately. The first is that a *daily* reward for a behaviour that occurs many times per day is a fixed-ratio schedule and produces a pause after each reward, and a practitioner who has set a token economy with a small daily bonus will see the young person do the behaviour in a burst at the end of the day and not at all during it. The second is that a young person who is checked "every week" will do the work at the end of the week, and the fix is not more motivation but a shorter interval.

### 3.3 Reinforcement for acquisition and for maintenance

The schedule used during acquisition is not the schedule used during maintenance, and confusing the two is a common design error.

**Acquisition** is best served by a **continuous reinforcement** schedule: every instance of the behaviour is reinforced. The risk is that the behaviour becomes dependent on the reinforcement, and that the young person has not learned to perform it in the absence of the contingency.

**Maintenance** requires moving to a **thinning schedule**, in which the reinforcer is delivered less and less frequently, while the behaviour continues. The thinning schedule is the mechanism by which the behaviour becomes self-sustaining, and the rule of thumb is that the behaviour must be well established and consistent before thinning begins.

**The thinning rule.** The schedule should be thinned only after the behaviour has been established at a high and consistent rate, and the thinning should be gradual rather than abrupt. A behaviour that is thinned too early extinguishes, and the practitioner will misread the extinction as evidence that the behaviour is not under the young person's control.

**The transition to natural consequences.** The final stage is the transfer of the behaviour to the natural contingencies of the young's life, and the goal of the whole procedure is to reach it.

### 3.4 The extinction of over-learned behaviour

Extinction is not always the goal, and the practitioner should distinguish three situations.

**Extinction is appropriate** when the behaviour should stop and no alternative is required — the punching, the shouting, the out-of-seat behaviour that has no function other than its own continuation.

**Extinction is inappropriate** when the behaviour is positively reinforced. A young person who is being praised for elaborating on their day will not stop elaborating, and the attempt to extinguish the talking will produce a marked increase in the talking, which is the **extinction burst** and which the young person and the practitioner will both find alarming.

**Extinction is dangerous** when the behaviour is maintained by negative reinforcement. This is the case that most requires a functional analysis, and it is the subject of Section 5.

### 3.5 Differential reinforcement

**Differential reinforcement** is the reinforcement of one behaviour in a way that distinguishes it from another, and it is the technique of choice wherever a behaviour must reduce without the relationship deteriorating.

Three forms are used.

**Differential reinforcement of other behaviour (DRO)** — reinforcing any behaviour other than the target. It is the least specific form and is used when the target behaviour is dangerous and must be suppressed immediately, as in the case of self-injury.

**Differential reinforcement of an incompatible behaviour (DRI)** — reinforcing a behaviour that cannot be performed at the same time as the target. A young person who is learning to keep their hands occupied is reinforced for keeping their hands occupied, which is incompatible with hitting.

**Differential reinforcement of the target behaviour (DRT or DRA)** — reinforcing the target behaviour in the presence of the target, and reinforcing an alternative behaviour in its absence. This is the form that produces the most durable change, and it is the form most often required when a young person is working on a skill that is difficult in the situations in which it is most needed.

Differential reinforcement has a further advantage that matters in a clinical relationship: it is a way of reinforcing a young person *without* reinforcing the problem, and it therefore permits the practitioner to maintain a positive relationship with the young person at the same time as working on the problem behaviour.

## 4. Shaping and Fading in Clinical Use

### 4.1 Shaping revisited

The procedure was described in Chapter 23 and is repeated here in its clinical form, because the clinical details are what make it work.

**Specify the target behaviour precisely.** The written specification should describe what the young person will do, how the practitioner will know it has occurred, and what the target rate or duration is.

**Identify the starting approximation.** This should be a behaviour the young person already does, at least occasionally, and reliably enough to be reinforced.

**Order the approximations.** The ordering should reflect the difficulty, not the logical sequence. A common error is to order the approximations as the steps in the physical task, which ignores the fact that an earlier step may be the difficult one.

**Specify the reinforcer for each approximation**, and make it immediate.

**Define the advance criterion** — the number of consecutive correct performances at one approximation that justifies moving up. Without an advance criterion, the practitioner moves up when they feel ready, and the behaviour degrades.

**Define the step-back rule** — the conditions under which the practitioner returns to the previous approximation. This is the safeguard against the "too good to last" pattern in which a practitioner pushes a young person forward, the behaviour deteriorates, and the whole plan is abandoned.

**Define the maintenance criteria** — when the plan moves from shaping to thinning.

### 4.2 Prompting and fading

A prompt is any stimulus that assists the initiation or performance of a behaviour, and the fade plan is not optional. The hierarchy runs from the least intrusive to the most: a natural cue, a gesture, a verbal direction, a visual prompt, a modelling demonstration, a physical or hand-over-hand prompt, and finally a physical prompt.

**The fade plan must be written down before the prompt is introduced.** The fade is slower than the practitioner expects, and a plan that is not written down will be abandoned on the day.

**Fading is usually slower for a young person with a learning difficulty or an intellectual disability**, and the fade criteria should be adjusted before the plan begins rather than during it, because a young person whose behaviour deteriorates during a failed fade experiences it as failure and the relationship is damaged.

**Fading is faster in a natural setting than in a clinic**, and the transfer of the prompt from the clinic to the school is a specific problem, because the school staff will not have been trained in the prompt hierarchy.

### 4.3 Prompt dependence and its prevention

A behaviour that is maintained by a physical or a verbal prompt is not the behaviour the practitioner wanted, and the signs of prompt dependence are recognisable. The young person performs the behaviour only when prompted; the performance does not increase across sessions; and the behaviour disappears when the prompt is faded.

The prevention is procedural: fade systematically, confirm that the behaviour persists after the fade, and treat a failed fade as information about the approximation rather than as a failure of the young person.

## 5. Escape-Maintained Behaviour

Escape-maintained behaviour is the most important single category in applied practice, and the management of it is the point at which the operant analysis earns its keep.

### 5.1 The pattern

A young person who is asked to do something and who escalates — arguing, shouting, breaking something, running — is performing a behaviour that reliably terminates the demand. The escalation is not a failure of the behaviour; it is the behaviour working. The more severe the escalation, the faster the demand ends, and the more reliably the behaviour is reinforced.

The circularity is exact: the adult's response to the escalation is the maintaining contingency, and the adult's response is usually made in the belief that the escalation is the problem.

### 5.2 The clinical procedure

The management of escape-maintained behaviour proceeds in a defined order, and the order matters.

**Step 1: Establish that the behaviour is escape-maintained.** This requires data, and the data come from the ABC record, the incident log, and the interview. The practitioner looks for the pattern: escalation followed by withdrawal of the demand, and consistency of that pattern.

**Step 2: Identify the demand.** What is the specific demand, and what is the young person's response to it? A young person who cannot tolerate a demand they do not understand will escalate, and the escalation may be a request for help.

**Step 3: Plan the response.** The adult's response to the escalation is the treatment, and the plan must specify it in advance, in writing, and it must specify it for the whole team, because the plan fails if one adult in the corridor responds differently.

**Step 4: Teach an alternative.** A young person cannot be expected to comply with a demand that has no tolerable alternative. The alternative is usually a request for help, a break in a specified form, or a graded reduction in the demand, and it must be made available at the same time as the demand.

**Step 5: Fade.** The demand is increased gradually, and the alternative is faded as the compliance is established.

### 5.3 The differential reinforcement of compliance

**Differential reinforcement of compliance** — reinforcing compliance with a demand and ignoring escalation — is the core technique. It must be implemented with care, because the "ignoring" must be genuine: the practitioner must not look away, not speak, and not give any signal, because a signal of attention is a reinforcer.

The technique has a predictable course and the practitioner should prepare the young person for it. The behaviour increases in the first few sessions, because the young person is testing and the testing produces a burst. Then it declines, sometimes sharply, and the decline is a sign that the extinction is taking hold rather than a sign that the plan has failed.

### 5.4 Why punishment fails here

Punishment of escape-maintained behaviour fails systematically, and the reason is worth stating because it is the most practically useful thing in this section.

Punishing the escalation does not teach compliance; it teaches that the escalation has a cost, and the young person learns to escalate in a way that is less detectable or less costly. Meanwhile, the escape remains available and the avoidance behaviour, which is the behaviour that is actually maintaining the problem, is untouched. And because the practitioner has escalated the response, the young person has learned that adults in this situation become larger, which may increase the aggression in other settings.

The correct target is the avoidance, and the correct method is to make the demand tolerable and to reinforce the compliance, not to make the escalation expensive.
