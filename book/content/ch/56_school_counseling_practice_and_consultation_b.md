
## 7. Crisis and Postvention

School crises include student death, suicide, violence, accidents, community disasters, and public incidents amplified by social media. Counsellors help coordinate factual communication, support for affected groups, identification of vulnerable students, family liaison, staff support, and referral.

Postvention after a suicide requires specialist guidance: avoid glamourising details, monitor contagion risk, support grief, communicate safely, and provide sustained follow-up. Rumours and online material should be addressed without repeating harmful content unnecessarily.

Crisis work also includes returning to ordinary routines. Predictability is regulating. Memorial practices should be inclusive, voluntary, culturally sensitive, and consistent with guidance.

## 8. Safeguarding and Multi-Agency Work

School counsellors often receive disclosures because they are accessible and trusted. They must know local safeguarding procedures, record accurately, avoid investigative interviewing beyond their role, and communicate promptly with designated leads.

Multi-agency work requires consent where possible, clear purpose, minimum necessary information, and attention to the young person's experience of meetings. A student should know who will attend, what will be discussed, and how their views will be represented.

Advocacy may involve special educational needs assessment, disability accommodations, exclusion prevention, alternative provision, housing or benefits referral, health appointments, and protection from bullying or discrimination.

## 9. Behaviour, Attendance, and Belonging

The counsellor's contribution to behaviour and attendance is formulation, not merely consequence delivery. Useful questions include: What skill is missing? What is being avoided or obtained? Where does the behaviour not occur? What adult responses help? What curriculum, sensory, language, health, transport, or peer factors operate?

Restorative approaches can repair harm where participation is voluntary and safety permits. They should not force victims to confront perpetrators or substitute for safeguarding.

Attendance work combines early identification, family partnership, practical barriers, school belonging, health, graded return, and monitoring. Public attendance shaming and purely punitive approaches often deepen avoidance.

## 10. Case Illustration: The Counsellor Who Refused the Report

**Situation.** A head of year asks the counsellor to report whether a fifteen-year-old “admitted” leaving school grounds and to recommend a sanction.

**Response.** The counsellor explains the role boundary: counselling content cannot be converted into disciplinary evidence, except where safeguarding or agreed information sharing applies. The counsellor offers consultation instead: review supervision patterns, reasons students leave at lunch, safe spaces, and a support plan based on information the student agrees to share.

**Consultation.** Data show most departures occur after a crowded lunch period and before double science. The student, consulted separately with consent, identifies noise, hunger due to long queues, and anxiety about entering a lesson late after lunch. The school adjusts lunch access, creates a quieter eating space, and permits direct entry to science for two weeks. The counsellor works individually on panic and planning.

The refusal protects trust while still helping the institution solve its problem.

## 11. Programme Evaluation and Advocacy

School counselling should evaluate:

- access and waiting time;
- student-defined goals and experience;
- symptoms and functioning;
- attendance, participation, and attainment where relevant;
- equity of referral and outcome;
- teacher and family consultation impact;
- crisis response and referral completion.

Data should be reported in aggregate and used to improve systems, not to pressure counsellors into selecting easy cases. Student voice should inform service design, including room location, referral routes, session length, digital access, and confidentiality communication.

Advocacy arises from patterns: repeated bullying in one location, inaccessible homework platforms, exclusion disparities, lack of interpreters, or timetable structures that prevent sleep and eating. The counsellor brings data, student perspective, and feasible proposals to leadership.

## 12. Ethics and Professional Identity

School counsellors manage dual loyalties to student, family, school, profession, and law. Written role statements, supervision external to line management where possible, record separation, and clear information-sharing agreements reduce conflict.

Counsellors should not conduct therapy with students they also discipline, grade, or investigate. Where small schools make overlap unavoidable, disclose limits and seek supervision and alternative referral options.

Caseload and time must permit safeguarding and crisis duties. A service measured only by number of sessions may neglect prevention and consultation where population impact is greater.

## 13. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Consultation** — Collaborative indirect service that helps adults alter environments or interactions around a young person.
- **Tiered support** — Organisation of universal, targeted, and intensive interventions.
- **Postvention** — Coordinated support after a death or crisis to reduce harm and support recovery.
- **Role clarity** — Explicit definition of the counsellor's functions, limits, and information-sharing practices.
- **Student voice** — Meaningful participation of students in decisions and service evaluation.
- **Restorative practice** — Voluntary processes focused on understanding harm, accountability, and repair.
:::

## 14. Chapter Summary

School counselling is both intimate and institutional. It protects a confidential relationship while changing classrooms, corridors, policies, and partnerships around the student. Its credibility depends on refusing incompatible roles, consulting collaboratively, and using evidence to advocate for environments in which fewer students need rescue.
