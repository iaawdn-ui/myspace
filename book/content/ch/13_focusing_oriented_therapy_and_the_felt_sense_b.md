## 8. Evidence and Research Support

### 8.1 The experimental foundation

Gendlin's original experiments, and the subsequent replication work, constitute a real body of evidence, and a student should be able to state it accurately.

**The design.** In the standard experimental procedure, participants are briefly instructed in a specific bodily task and then asked to find a bodily location and a quality of their current experience, in the manner described in the principles. Control conditions include instructions to think about the problem in a more conventional manner, to analyse the emotions involved, or to do nothing. Outcomes are measured before and after by self-report, and in some studies by physiological measures.

**The findings.** Participants who located the felt sense in the manner Gendlin described showed greater improvement in their reported emotional state and in their sense of the situation than participants in the control conditions, and the effect was present in the single-session design. The effect size in the original and replicated studies is moderate, which is a substantial effect for a single-session intervention with no practitioner involvement, and it is one of the more robust single-session effects in the psychotherapy literature.

**The limits.** The studies were conducted with university students, in the 1970s and 1980s, with self-report outcome measures, and the research group that produced them is small. The effect has been difficult to replicate in clinical populations, and the standard criticism — that the effect may be a expectancy effect, since the procedure is unusual and the participants are being given something they have not had before — has not been fully answered, although the presence of the comparison conditions is designed to control for it. The studies are nonetheless among the more interesting in the field, and the finding that a bodily attention task produces a measurable change in emotional state is a real and useful thing to know.

### 8.2 The clinical outcome evidence

The clinical evidence for focusing as a therapy is considerably thinner than the experimental evidence for the felt sense task, and the two should not be conflated.

A small number of controlled outcome studies of focusing therapy, mostly from the 1980s, reported favourable outcomes, particularly with the presenting problems of trauma, anxiety, and depression, and a small number of studies have compared focusing with client-centred therapy with equivalent outcomes. A substantial clinical literature exists in the form of case material, and the theory's own tradition is heavily case-based. The reviews consistently rate the clinical evidence as limited.

### 8.3 The somatic and trauma literature

The most substantial evidence for the theory in practice comes from the somatic and trauma-informed fields, in which focusing-derived concepts have been widely adopted, and in which the associated techniques — grounding, resourcing, and the "window of tolerance" work — have a considerably stronger evidence base than focusing itself. The student should be careful to note the direction of the borrowing: the somatic field has adopted focusing's concepts and produced evidence for a broader set of practices, and the evidence for the specific focusing method is weaker than the evidence for the stabilisation work in which its concepts are embedded.

### 8.4 What is weak or absent

**The felt sense as a measurable construct.** The construct has resisted reliable operationalisation, and the measures of experiential processing used in the research are of variable quality. The theory's central claim — that the felt sense is the locus of change — is difficult to test, for the same reason that the Gestalt theory is difficult to test: a claim about a first-person process resists the conventional designs.

**The developmental evidence.** The evidence for focusing with children and adolescents specifically is very thin, notwithstanding the theoretical plausibility of the developmental applications and the long history of the practice with young people. The applications to younger children are widely described and poorly studied.

**The comparative evidence.** There is no reliable evidence that focusing is superior to other bona fide therapies for any condition.

## 9. Strengths and Limitations of Focusing-Oriented Therapy

**Strengths.** It identifies a phenomenon that is real, that is common, that is accessible without training, and that most therapies neglect. It provides a technique that works with young people who cannot talk, and the technique is unusually well suited to the somatic presentations that dominate contemporary practice. It is teachable: a practitioner can learn the method in a small number of sessions, and the low-resourced variant is usable in a school or community service with a single session. It respects the young person's autonomy more fully than almost any other method in the field, since the young person is doing the work and the practitioner is providing a quality of attention. It has a real experimental foundation, which is unusual for a humanistic technique. And it converges with the somatic, trauma-informed, and contemplative literatures, which makes it an unusually well-supported position in a field that is currently converging on somatic and body-based practice.

**Limitations.** The clinical outcome evidence is thin, and the theory's central claim is difficult to test. The method requires a practitioner who can tolerate silence and wordlessness, which is a demanding skill and is not developed in most training programmes. It offers little in the way of structure, and a young person who requires structure — which is many young people, and most young people in crisis — will not find it sufficient. The method's assumption that the young person has access to a felt sense is not always true, and the practitioner needs a way of working with a young person for whom it is not accessible. And the theory's account of the person, though empirically anchored, remains a first-person account, and it says relatively little about the social and structural determinants of a young person's difficulty.

## 10. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

**The body and cultural difference.** The theory's foundational claim is that the felt sense is accessible to the person, and this is not culturally universal. In cultural contexts in which emotion is located in the head or in the heart in a distinctly verbal way, in which the body is not treated as a source of knowledge about the self, in which physical expression is heavily regulated, and in which the boundary between self and environment is understood differently, the instruction to attend to a bodily felt sense may be experienced as strange, or as an invitation to a form of self-exposure that is not culturally sanctioned. The culturally responsive practice is to ask the young person how they know what is going on with them, and to follow their answer.

**The risk of the body's authority.** The contemporary somatic literature has been critical of the assumption that the body is an unambiguous source of truth about the person, and the focusing theory's claim that the felt sense is the location of the change carries this assumption. A young person who has been told for years that their body is unreliable, that their symptoms are "all in their head," or that their trauma responses are exaggerated, will have learned to distrust their own bodily signals, and the method's first task may be the restoration of that trust rather than the discovery of a felt sense.

**The ethical value of the method.** The ethical value of the method should also be stated, because it is considerable. It is a method that gives a young person access to their own resources without requiring them to disclose a narrative, to accept a formulation, to comply with a programme, or to become verbally articulate. It is a method in which the young person is the authority and the practitioner is the facilitator, which matters enormously with young people who have been told all their lives what they are doing wrong. And it is a method that can be used with a young person who has not yet trusted an adult enough to talk, which is frequently where the work needs to begin.

**The ethical requirement of the practitioner as a container.** The method places a substantial demand on the practitioner, and the demand is partly emotional. A practitioner working with a young person's wordless experience is exposed to a good deal of affect, and the practitioner must be able to sustain their own congruence while remaining available. The ethical requirement is that the practitioner attend to their own state, seek supervision, and not undertake this work with a young person if their own resources for it are depleted. This is a requirement that the professional codes express only obliquely, and that the theory does not express at all.

## 11. Case Illustration: A Boy and the Weather

**The referral.** Arjun, twelve, is referred by his school for "anger and withdrawal." He has had three suspensions in a term, is failing two subjects, and his form tutor reports that he "shouts" and then "goes completely silent" and "will not look at anyone." The mother reports that he has not spoken to his father in three weeks following an argument. There is no history of mental health treatment.

**The first session.** The practitioner — who has been trained in focusing and works in a school-based service with a low-resourced format — begins with a short piece of grounding, and then asks Arjun where in his body he notices the difficulty. He does not answer for a while and then says, "My chest." She asks what it is like. He says, "Like a storm." She asks him to stay with it for a moment and to notice whether it is changing. He says: "It doesn't stop."

**The low-resourced protocol.** The practitioner uses the structured low-resourced instructions. She asks him to find the place exactly, and he points to the middle of his chest. She asks him to describe the quality, and he says it is "like the weather before rain" and "it goes black." She asks him to stay with it. There is a long silence of perhaps forty seconds, and the practitioner says nothing. Then he says: "It's lighter." She says: "Tell me more about that." He says: "It's like the sky after a storm. Still wet but you can see."

**The shift.** The practitioner does not say "what happened?" and does not ask about the argument with his father. She stays with the felt sense, and she asks him to stay with the lightness. He does, for several minutes, and during that time she observes that his posture changes, that his breathing changes, and that his face changes. When the session ends she asks him to notice what has changed, and he says: "I don't know. But it's different."

**The second session.** Arjun comes back and, unprompted, says: "I've been thinking about the storm thing." The practitioner asks him what he has been thinking. He says: "I think it's about waiting. At home it's always waiting. Waiting for my dad to come home, waiting for my mum to come back from work, waiting for him to stop being angry. And I'm waiting too."

**The formulation.** The developmental formulation is now available. Arjun's "storms" are the bodily representation of a life organised around a state of suspended waiting, and the waiting is the interrupted gestalt, or, in the language of this chapter, the felt sense of a situation that has not changed and cannot be changed by him. The suspensions at school are the behavioural form of the same state. The anger and the silence are the two poles of his management of an intolerable and unchangeable situation.

**The intervention.** The intervention is developmental and practical, and it follows from the formulation rather than from the technique. Arjun's life contains one adult — his mother — who works nights and one — his father — who is often angry. The practitioner arranges for a conversation with the mother and, with Arjun's agreement, with the father, and the conversation is about the household's schedule, the waiting, and the specific question of what happens at home in the evenings.

The focusing work itself is continued, and the specific use of it is that it gives Arjun a way of experiencing the difference between the storm and the sky after the storm. The practitioner teaches him a brief version of the method — which the theory predicts will be the most durable form of the intervention — and he uses it at home. He tells his practitioner, in the sixth session, that he used it in the car on the way to school, and that his dad had been shouting about something else and "I did the weather thing in my head and it went light and I didn't have to shout back."

**The developmental qualification.** The practitioner is careful about the sequence. She does not attempt the parental work until the relationship can carry it, and the parental work is not a confrontation of the father but a conversation about the timetable. The developmental literature is clear that the adolescent's renegotiation of the attachment relationship requires that the adult be available and reliable, and that the confrontation of a parent's behaviour before that has been established is likely to be experienced as a repetition.

**The ethical dimension.** Two ethical requirements were engaged. The first is the duty of care in relation to a family system in which a twelve-year-old is waiting for a parent to stop being angry: the practitioner has a duty to consider whether the household conditions constitute a safeguarding concern, and to raise it if the assessment indicates it. She assesses and concludes that they do not, but she records the assessment and the reasoning. The second is the consent question: the parental work requires Arjun's agreement to its shape, and his agreement was sought explicitly, and the practitioner was prepared for it to be refused, in which case she would have continued with the individual work and the school work.

**The teaching point.** The technique produced the entry into the material, and the theory produced the formulation, and the developmental frame produced the intervention. None of the three was sufficient alone. A practitioner who used the focusing technique without the developmental frame would have had a boy who could access a felt sense and nothing to change in his life. A practitioner who had the developmental frame without the focusing technique would have had a family meeting with a boy who could not say what was happening to him.

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Felt sense** — The living, bodily, whole, and largely undefinable experience of one's current situation; the location in which Gendlin claimed psychological change occurs.
- **Experiencing** — Gendlin's term for the whole, live process of a person's moment-to-moment experience, as distinct from the conceptual or verbal level.
- **Experiential focusing** — The process of attending to and holding the felt sense, and the sense of a shift within it.
- **The inner edge** — The boundary between the felt sense and the conceptual, and the term for the felt sense itself as the place of the live process.
- **The shift** — The experience of a change in the felt sense, which is the process element of the experiential process.
- **The direction of the felt sense** — The sense of a movement or orientation within the felt sense; a blocking of which is a common finding and an important clinical target.
- **Triple structure** — Gendlin's description of the felt sense as comprising a bodily location, a sense of a whole, and a sense of a direction.
- **Six principles of focusing** — The distinctions between the felt sense and the content of the problem, bodily location, wordlessness, the shift as the process, bodily sense of a process and direction, and the felt sense versus the conceptual.
- **The special kind of listening** — Gendlin's description of the practitioner's attention: warm, wide, attending to the whole and to the wordless, and free of interpretation.
- **Patient waiting** — The practitioner's stance during a focusing session, in which the shift is allowed to occur without intervention.
- **Low-resourced focusing** — A single-session, practitioner-guided form of focusing designed for settings with limited time and resources; the variant most usable in schools and brief services.
- **Intensive focusing** — A form in which the practitioner guides the process more actively, for people who have difficulty locating the felt sense unaided.
- **Focusing as a practice** — Teaching the young person the method for their own use; the most durable form and the one most consistent with the theory.
- **The felt sense as a locus of change** — The theory's central claim: that change occurs in the felt sense, and that the felt sense is where the person has access to their own resources.
- **The person as a process** — The account of the self as a process rather than a structure; Gendlin's version converges with the contemporary person-centred formulation.
- **Somatic symptom as held meaning** — The focusing account of a physical symptom as the localised felt sense of an unresolved situation.
- **Focusing in the body** — The technique of the body scan and the sentence stems used to locate the difficulty bodily.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Eugene Gendlin's discovery of the felt sense was made in 1946 in the course of a psychotherapy research project, and the finding was not accepted by the research team. The subsequent two decades established the phenomenon experimentally and developed the theory of experiencing.

The felt sense is a living, bodily, whole, and largely undefinable experience of one's current situation, distinct from emotion, from bodily sensation, and from thought. The theory's central claim is that psychological change happens in the felt sense and that the felt sense is the locus of the person's own resources for change. This places the theory at the intersection of the humanistic, somatic, phenomenological, and contemplative traditions, and it converges with the contemporary person-as-process formulations and with the somatic trauma literature.

The six principles and the five-step procedure make the theory teachable, and the low-resourced variant makes it usable in schools and brief services. The developmental fit with young people is unusually good: the method does not require verbal sophistication, it works with the body's primacy in emotional processing, it respects the young person's autonomy, and it is compatible with play-based work with younger children.

The experimental evidence for the bodily attention task is real and moderately strong, and the clinical outcome evidence for focusing as a therapy is considerably thinner. The theory's central claim is difficult to test, for the same structural reason that the Gestalt theory is difficult to test. The case of Arjun illustrates the theory's particular contribution: the focusing technique produced the entry into the material, the theory produced the formulation, and the developmental frame produced an intervention that changed the conditions of a young person's life.

## 14. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Summarise how the felt sense was discovered, and explain why the research team did not accept it.
2. Define the felt sense, and explain how it differs from an emotion, from a bodily sensation, and from a thought.
3. State the theory's central claim about the locus of change, and explain the three practical consequences that follow from it.
4. Describe the triple structure of the felt sense, and explain the clinical use of the third element.
5. State the six principles of focusing, and give an adolescent example of each.
6. Describe the five steps of the focusing procedure, and state the practitioner's role in each.
7. Explain the significance of the "shift," and why the practitioner should acknowledge it when it occurs.
8. What does the theory say about somatic symptoms, and how does this relate to the medical assessment requirement?
9. State the developmental modifications of focusing for a six-year-old, an eleven-year-old, and a sixteen-year-old.
10. In what ways is the practice of attending to a bodily felt sense culturally marked, and what is the risk of the body's authority being assumed?
:::

## 15. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 15.1 — The Felt Sense Practicum (90 minutes, pairs)

One student locates a bodily sensation in their own body and holds it, without describing it, for ninety seconds. The partner observes and then describes what they observed — the changes in the face, the breathing, the posture, the drift of attention. Then they swap. The exercise develops two things: the ability to hold a felt sense, and the ability to observe one in another person, both of which are required in practice.

### Activity 15.2 — Low-Resourced Focusing in a Real Setting (2 hours, individual preparation, 1 hour seminar)

Prepare and deliver a single low-resourced focusing session to a peer who presents a mild, real difficulty. Use the structured instructions exactly as they are given in the manual. Afterwards, both of you write two paragraphs: what the peer experienced, and what you experienced. The second paragraph is the one to bring to the seminar, because the practitioner's experience of the session is usually the more surprising and the more instructive.

### Activity 15.3 — The Body's Untrustworthiness (60 minutes, written, then pairs)

Consider a young person who has been told for years that their body is unreliable — that their symptoms are "all in their head," that their reactions are exaggerated, that their body has failed them. Write a half-page on what the focusing method would ask of this young person, and on what the practitioner would need to do first. Then consider a young person from a cultural context in which bodily attention is not the ordinary mode of emotional processing, and write the equivalent. The two cases are different, and the comparison is the exercise.
:::

## 16. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the theory.** Read Gendlin (1978) first — it is short, practical, and will make the theory usable — and then read Gendlin (1996) for the clinical application. Read the 1946 research account in secondary sources as well, because the story of the finding's rejection is a useful corrective to a profession's tendency to accept only what it has already validated.

**For the developmental application.** Read Gendlin (1990), which was written for parents and teachers and is full of material that a student can use directly with a school or a family. Then read the somatic and trauma-informed literature on grounding and resourcing, and note the concepts that have been borrowed.

**For the experimental evidence.** Read the original experimental studies and one contemporary replication or review, and read the methodological critiques. The effect is real and the interpretation is genuinely contested, and a student who can state both is in a stronger position than one who can state only the first.

**For the somatic integration.** Read the material on the window of tolerance and on stabilisation, and identify the focusing-derived concepts in it. This exercise is a good preparation for Part VI, where the somatic and trauma work is developed in detail.
