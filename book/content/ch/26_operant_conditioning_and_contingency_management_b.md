
## 7. Contingency Management in Clinical Settings

### 7.1 The definition

**Contingency management** is the deliberate and negotiated restructuring of the reinforcement structure of a setting, in which the specific consequences for specific behaviours are defined in advance, taught to everyone involved, and reviewed at stated intervals. It is broader than a token economy, which is one technique within it, and broader than a behaviour contract, which is another.

The defining features of contingency management are that it is **specified** rather than improvised, that it is **negotiated** with the people whose behaviour it governs, that it is **transparent** — everyone knows the rules — and that it is **reviewed**, with a stated end point, rather than being left to run indefinitely.

### 7.2 The procedure

**Step 1: Specify the target behaviours.** In a clinical setting, a small number of clearly defined behaviours, chosen because their change matters. A common clinical specification is: attending sessions; completing a specified task; not using substances during the session; contacting the practitioner between sessions.

**Step 2: Specify the consequences.** For each target behaviour, the consequence that follows it and the consequence that follows its absence. Both directions must be specified, because a contingency that only defines rewards is not a contingency.

**Step 3: Identify the reinforcers.** By the survey method of Section 2, and by testing the candidates.

**Step 4: Specify the schedule.** The interval and ratio, and the thinning plan.

**Step 5: Specify the rules for the exceptions.** A contingency that does not specify what happens when the rules are broken cannot be administered, and the specification should cover illness, absence, crisis, and the days when the young person is not capable of complying.

**Step 6: Train the staff.** Every person who will deliver a consequence must know the rules, and the training should be a rehearsal rather than a briefing. This step is the one most often omitted and the one most responsible for the failure of contingency management programmes.

**Step 7: Review at stated intervals.** A contingency that is not reviewed becomes an unexamined routine, and unexamined routines drift.

**Step 8: Fade and terminate.** Every contingency management programme should have a planned end. The goal of a token economy is not a young person who needs tokens; it is a young person who no longer needs the system, and a system with no end point is a system that has become the plan.

### 7.3 Contingency management with a young person in difficulty

The context of a young person's life makes contingency management unusually delicate, for three reasons.

**The young person's environment is shared.** The school, the home, and the clinic are three environments with three sets of contingencies, and if the three do not align, the young person will select the environment with the most generous contingencies, which is usually neither the one that is best for them nor the one they live in.

**The stakes of the consequences are uneven.** A young person in a foster placement or in a residential setting may be subject to a set of consequences that were never negotiated, some of which are severe and some of which are applied inconsistently, and the practitioner who introduces a negotiated system into that environment may find that the negotiated system is the least powerful one in the young person's life.

**The consequences are often experienced as unjust.** A young person who is used to a system in which the rules change according to the mood of the adult will experience a transparent system as a novelty, and the novelty is usually welcome, and the welcome is itself useful data.

### 7.4 The negotiated alternative

The **negotiated alternative** is a technique developed in the family literature and adopted more widely than it deserves. In it, the young person is asked to propose what they would be willing to do in exchange for what they want, and the practitioner negotiates from the young person's proposal rather than from a preset offer.

The technique is powerful because it produces a commitment, and it is limited because the young person's proposal will usually be worse for the practitioner than a preset offer, and the pressure to accept it is difficult to resist. The balanced practice is to take the young person's proposal seriously, to state the constraints honestly, and to make the final agreement one that the practitioner could actually deliver. A young person who has negotiated a reward the clinic cannot provide has been set up to fail, and the failure will be attributed to the young person.

## 8. Behaviour Contracts

### 8.1 The structure

A **behaviour contract** is a written agreement specifying what each party will do, and the consequences of their doing or not doing it. The standard elements are: the parties; the specific behaviours promised by each party; the duration of the agreement; the specified consequences; the review date; and the signatures.

The feature that distinguishes a contract from a token economy is **reciprocity**: the adult also makes promises, and the consequences apply to the adult as well. A contract in which the young person promises to attend three sessions and the practitioner promises to be there at the agreed time, with a stated consequence if the practitioner is not, is a different instrument from one in which only the young person is bound.

Reciprocity also serves a clinical purpose. It converts the young person from the position of a subject of the system to the position of a participant in it, and the young person who has a say in the design of a contract is markedly more likely to be honest about it in the review.

### 8.2 Contracts with minors

A contract with a young person is an agreement and not a legal instrument, and this must be clear. A contract with a minor cannot be enforced in the way an employment contract can, and a practitioner who presents it as binding is misrepresenting its status.

Three limits should be observed.

**The consequences must be within the adult's power to deliver.** A consequence that depends on another authority — a school's attendance policy, a benefit agency, a court — is not a contractual consequence and should not be written as one.

**The contract must not contract for the removal of a young person's rights.** A contract that trades a young person's right to see a friend, or their right to privacy, or their right to complain about a staff member, for behaviour is an abuse of power regardless of how it is framed, and no behavioural formulation justifies it.

**The contract must be voluntary.** A young person who is required to sign a behaviour contract as a condition of continued attendance in a school or a service has not entered into an agreement.

### 8.3 The uses of a contract

Contracts are well suited to a young person who is older, who is able to understand the terms, and whose behaviour is a matter of a stable disagreement rather than of a clinical symptom. They are particularly useful in three situations.

**Ambivalence about treatment.** A young person who is ambivalent about therapy can be helped to commit to a defined and limited number of sessions through a contract, and the contract makes the commitment concrete and reviewable.

**Ambiguity about what is expected.** A young person who does not know what is expected of them cannot be held to it, and the contract specifies it.

**Conflict between a young person and a school.** A contract between a young person and a school, with the school also bound, can change the terms of an argument that has become entrenched.

Contracts are poorly suited to young children, to young people in acute crisis, and to situations in which the central difficulty is the young person's safety.

### 8.4 The review

Every contract must have a review, and the review is the part that most often does not happen. The review is a shared examination of what happened, and it is where the agreement is amended, extended, or ended. A contract reviewed only when it breaks down is not a contract; it is a set of threats.

## 9. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Would Not Eat

**Presentation.** Ahmed is eleven and has been referred by his school because he has been refusing to eat in the dining hall, and he now eats a small amount at home before school. He is very thin, and his mother is frightened. The school has tried a variety of approaches, including a "healthy lunch club" for children who are not eating, and a reward chart for "eating a whole sandwich", and both have failed.

**The assessment.** The behavioural assessment establishes three things.

*The behaviour.* Ahmed does not eat in the dining hall. At home he eats, and he eats a restricted range. The mother reports that he eats pasta, rice, plain chicken, and nothing else, and that this has been the case for two years, and that it started after a year in which he was "sick" and "couldn't keep food down".

*The antecedents.* The observation in the dining hall is unremarkable in a way that is itself informative. Ahmed sits with a group of boys, he does not talk much, nobody is attending to him, and the food in front of him is disliked. There is no peer pressure, no coercion, and no obvious social consequence. He simply does not eat.

*The consequences.* When he does not eat, nothing happens. He leaves the dining hall, goes to the corridor, and is alone for twenty minutes, and he is not spoken to.

**The hypothesis.** The behaviour is maintained by the **avoidance of the dining hall**, and the avoidance is maintained by the relief of leaving, and — this is the crucial finding — the young person has no experience of what eating there would be like, because he has not done it for two years. The condition that the avoidance prevents is not "eating" but "eating in that room, with those people, with that food."

There is a second and more serious question, which the behavioural assessment raises and must not conceal. The restriction began after a period of illness in which he could not keep food down, and the behavioural account cannot say what that illness was, whether there was vomiting, whether there was fear of vomiting, or whether the restriction is now maintained by something that is not a contingency at all. The practitioner must not proceed with the behavioural plan until the medical history has been established, and the referral to the paediatric service must be made first, because a restrictive eating pattern in an eleven-year-old requires medical assessment before any behavioural intervention.

**The plan.** The plan has two stages, and the order matters.

*Stage one: the medical and the assessment of the eating disorder.* The paediatric assessment, the eating disorder screening, and the family work that supports it. The behavioural work is deferred.

*Stage two: the graded return to eating in the dining hall.* This is an exposure and a shaping programme together, and it uses the principles of Chapter 25.

The hierarchy is built with Ahmed, and it is built in the small dimensions rather than the large one. The steps are: sit in the dining hall for two minutes with a plate of food and leave; sit for five minutes; sit with one friend; have a drink; have a sandwich and leave; have a sandwich and stay for the whole lunch period. The reinforcer for each step is time in the corridor with his friend afterwards, which the school can deliver and which the reinforcer survey confirms is what Ahmed values.

The shaping is at the level of a single sandwich, and the advance criterion for each step is three consecutive successes.

The **fading** is built in from the start. The school agrees that the "healthy lunch club" arrangement ends, and that Ahmed returns to the dining hall rather than being assigned to a separate space, because a separate space removes the very cue that is being treated. The school also agrees that the adults do not comment on his eating, do not encourage, do not praise, and do not remark when he does not eat. The comments that were intended as encouragement are, in the operant analysis, attention that is contingent on the behaviour, and the praise for "eating a whole sandwich" is positive reinforcement of a behaviour that is reinforced only in that setting and nowhere else, which is why the reward chart produced no durable change.

**The family work.** Ahmed's mother is asked to make two changes. She stops cooking around his restriction and begins to serve the same food for the family, without comment, without negotiation, and without a separate "safe" meal for him. And she stops the "try just a little bit" routine, which is the most common and least effective of the responses to a restricted eater, and which has the effect of making every meal a negotiation in which the food becomes the arena.

The mother's anxiety about this plan is substantial, and it should be, and the practitioner should treat it as the central clinical task rather than as a compliance issue. A mother who is frightened of her son's health is being asked to stop the thing that is currently reducing her anxiety, and the anxiety will find another outlet. The plan therefore includes a regular appointment with the mother for as long as it takes.

**The outcome.** Ahmed progresses through the hierarchy over approximately eleven weeks, with two relapses, one following an illness and one following a conflict at home. The restriction at home narrows over the same period, which is expected and which is a useful sign. His weight improves slowly. The paediatric service remains involved, and the multidisciplinary plan is reviewed monthly.

**The interpretation.** The case illustrates the discipline's central clinical discipline: the question was not how to make Ahmed eat but what his not eating was accomplishing, and the answer — the relief of leaving a room he was not ready to be in, maintained by a contingency nobody had designed — changed the intervention entirely. It also illustrates the discipline's central clinical danger: the behavioural analysis was elegant, and it was the wrong analysis, because the behaviour had a medical cause that the contingency analysis could not see. The practitioner who had proceeded directly to the graded exposure would have treated a young person's illness as a habit.

## 10. Evidence and Research Support

### 10.1 The status of the evidence

The evidence base for operant interventions is large, and it is strongest where the intervention is specified, the behaviour is operationally defined, and the outcome is measured. The most important evidence findings are as follows.

**Parent training based on operant principles has a strong evidence base** for oppositional behaviour, conduct disorder, and disruptive behaviour in the school setting, and this is among the most replicated findings in the child behavioural literature. The Cochrane reviews of parent training for conduct disorder and oppositional behaviour report substantial and durable effects, and the findings are among the most robust in the entire child mental health evidence base.

**Token economies have strong evidence for institutional and educational settings**, particularly for the reduction of disruptive behaviour in classes of students with behavioural difficulties, and the effect sizes in the meta-analyses are large.

**Differential reinforcement has strong support** for the reduction of specific behaviours and for the teaching of alternatives, and it is a component of nearly every successful behavioural programme.

**Schedules of reinforcement have strong experimental support**, and the translation of the schedule findings into clinical practice is well established in the applied literature.

**Contingency management for substance use has some of the strongest effects in the entire psychological literature**, with effect sizes for the treatment of stimulant and opioid use that are larger than those for any other treatment for these conditions, and Chapter 47 develops the ethical and practical questions that the strength of these effects raises.

### 10.2 The evidence with young people specifically

Three findings are specifically relevant to this book.

**Parent-delivered contingency procedures outperform practitioner-delivered procedures** for the younger age range, in which the contingencies that matter are in the home and the parents are the only people who can change them.

**The fidelity of the implementation determines the outcome**, and the studies that measure fidelity and find it low report null results that reflect the implementation rather than the intervention.

**Contextual factors moderate the effect.** Parent training works better when the child has a comorbid condition, which is a counterintuitive and well-replicated finding that is best explained by the observation that the presence of more severe behaviour increases the parents' motivation and the salience of the training.

### 10.3 Limitations of the evidence

**The literature is dominated by young children and oppositional behaviour.** The evidence for contingency management with adolescents, particularly in outpatient settings, is thinner than the evidence with parents of young children, and the field's adolescent applications are more often extrapolated than demonstrated.

**The outcomes are almost always behavioural**, and the measures of the young person's own experience of the intervention, of the family's experience, and of the durability of the change in naturalistic settings are much less systematically reported. This is the gap that the 1993 revisions of the applied research criteria were directed at, and it is still not fully closed.

**Many of the studies are conducted in settings with distinctive features** — residential schools, specialist classrooms, hospital wards — and the generalisation to a general school or a general outpatient clinic is a real question.

**The allegiance effects are substantial.** Where the researcher is a member of the behavioural tradition, the effects are larger, and this pattern is one of the most consistent findings in the entire evidence base, described in Chapter 7.

## 11. Strengths and Limitations of Contingency Management

### 11.1 Strengths

**It is specified, teachable, and reproducible.** A contingency management programme can be written down, handed to a team, and implemented with fidelity, and this is not true of most psychotherapies.

**It addresses the environment rather than the person.** The intervention is directed at the contingencies, which are in the adult's gift, and this is a considerable advantage where the young person's internal states are not.

**It produces rapid, measurable change.** Contingency management is often the most rapidly effective intervention available for a specific behaviour, and rapid change is important for a young person who is demoralised by a long history of unsuccessful attempts.

**It preserves the relationship.** Differential reinforcement permits the practitioner to reinforce the young person while working on the problem, and this is a substantial advantage over approaches that require the practitioner to withhold attention.

**It is portable across settings**, and it can be implemented by parents, teachers, and ward staff, with training.

### 11.2 Limitations

**It requires resources and consistency.** The programme requires trained staff, a stable environment, and the sustained attention of a practitioner. In a service with high turnover and low continuity, the failure is a failure of implementation and will be misread as a failure of the approach.

**It can be experienced as coercive.** A young person who is being managed by a written system of rewards and consequences may experience it as the loss of personal agency, and in some cases the experience will be accurate. The ethical section addresses this directly.

**It addresses the behaviour and not the context that produced it.** A contingency programme that reduces a young person's offending behaviour while leaving the conditions that generated it untouched has solved a symptom, and the practitioner should be alert to the possibility of a displaced problem.

**It can create dependence on the system**, and the fade plan is not optional.

**It says nothing about the young person's inner life**, and a young person whose behaviour is maintained by shame, by self-hatred, or by a conviction of their own unworthiness will not be helped by a system that addresses the contingencies of the behaviour.

## 12. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 12.1 The reward and the market

The contingency management framework is often criticised for importing a market model into relationships, and the criticism has a real core. A young person who learns that affection, attention, and privilege are commodities that must be earned by performance has learned something that is not only false but corrosive, and the literature on the effects of reward-based parenting is the empirical counterpart of the moral objection.

Four principles follow for the practitioner.

**Never contract for what should not be traded.** Basic attention, genuine warmth, and access to help should not be contingent on performance. The practical form of this is that a practitioner must not withdraw attention or warmth as a consequence, and must not ration approval.

**Reinforce the behaviour, never compliance with the practitioner.** The distinction is subtle and important: reinforcing a young person's honest report, or their effort, or their self-regulation, is different from rewarding them for agreeing with the practitioner.

**Use natural consequences wherever possible.** A natural consequence carries no dignity cost and generalises better, and the progression from artificial to natural is a progression towards autonomy.

**Be aware that the system changes the relationship.** A young person who is being managed by a token system will behave differently in the sessions, and the practitioner should be honest with themselves about the fact that some of the improvement observed in session is contingent on the session.

### 12.2 Cultural considerations

**Reinforcers are cultural.** The reinforcer survey must be conducted in the young person's own frame of reference, and the practitioner must expect that the young person's values may differ from the family's, from the school's, and from the practitioner's. In a context where material rewards are scarce, a token may be a very powerful reinforcer; in a context where material rewards are abundant, it may be a very weak one, and the practitioner who has assumed otherwise will build a plan on a contingency that does not exist.

**The family's own systems of reward and sanction are already operating.** A family in which compliance is rewarded with togetherness and independence is rewarded with freedom operates a contingency system, and a clinic-based programme that runs counter to it will be defeated by it. The most useful clinical question is not "what reinforcer will work?" but "what does this family already reward, and how can the plan be built on that?"

**The honour and the stigma of the systems.** In many communities, a token economy or a written contract introduces a public record of the young person's behaviour that is experienced as a humiliation. Where the young person's peers can see the system, the design must protect the young person's dignity, and in some settings the system should be invisible to peers.

**The authority to set contingencies.** In some settings, the assumption that a professional has the standing to determine what a young person will be rewarded for is itself a cultural assumption, and the resistance it generates is rational and should be understood on its own terms.

### 12.3 Ethical requirements

**Informed assent and the right to withdraw.** A young person must be able to decline to participate in a contingency programme, and the consequences of declining must be stated in advance. A young person who is in a residential or a school setting where the programme determines some of their access must be able to decline without penalty.

**Proportionality.** The magnitude of the consequences must be proportionate to the situation. A young person in a residential setting should not have a disproportionately large stake in a system over behaviour that is trivial.

**The prohibition on contingent aversive procedures.** The contemporary practice does not use contingent aversive stimuli.

**The prohibition on the deprivation of a young person's rights.** No system may trade access to education, to healthcare, to food, to rest, to privacy, to advocacy, or to complaint for behaviour.

**Protection against the failure of the system.** A contingency programme that a young person cannot meet has failed, and the response to the failure must be an adjustment of the programme rather than an increase in the consequences. The principle should be stated to the young person in advance: if the plan is not working, the plan changes.

**Documentation and review.** The programme, the rules, the review points, and the outcomes should be documented, and the young person should be able to see the record.

## 13. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Operant** — Behaviour that operates on the environment and is selected by its consequences; distinguished from the respondent, which is elicited by a stimulus.
- **Three-term contingency** — The relation in which a discriminative stimulus signals that a response will be reinforced, the response occurs, and a consequence follows.
- **Discriminative stimulus (Sᴰ)** — The antecedent stimulus that signals that a particular response will be reinforced and that another will not.
- **Consequence (Sᴿ)** — The event that follows a response and that determines the future probability of that response.
- **Reinforcer** — A stimulus that, when made contingent on a behaviour, increases the future frequency of that behaviour.
- **Reinforcement value** — The degree to which a stimulus is preferred, which determines the amount of behaviour that can be acquired and maintained by its delivery.
- **Motivating operation** — An event that establishes or abolishes the reinforcing effectiveness of a stimulus.
- **Natural reinforcer** — A consequence that is already part of the young person's environment, requiring no administration and generalising well.
- **Reinforcer survey** — A structured interview with the young person, the parents, and the teachers, each asked the same questions about what the young person values, for the purpose of identifying a usable reinforcer.
- **Fixed-ratio (FR) schedule** — Reinforcement delivered after a fixed number of responses; produces a high rate with a post-reinforcement pause.
- **Variable-ratio (VR) schedule** — Reinforcement delivered after a variable number of responses; produces a high, steady, and highly persistent rate with no pauses.
- **Fixed-interval (FI) schedule** — Reinforcement delivered for the first response after a fixed time period; produces a scalloped response pattern.
- **Variable-interval (VI) schedule** — Reinforcement delivered for the first response after a variable time period; produces a moderate, steady, and very stable rate.
- **Scallop** — The characteristic acceleration of responding across a fixed interval as the reinforcement time approaches.
- **Post-reinforcement pause** — The brief cessation of responding that follows a fixed-ratio reinforcement.
- **Continuous reinforcement** — Reinforcement of every instance of a behaviour; appropriate to acquisition and unmaintainable as a permanent arrangement.
- **Thinning schedule** — The gradual reduction in the frequency of reinforcement, used to transfer control from the schedule to the natural environment.
- **Shaping** — The systematic reinforcement of successive approximations to a target behaviour.
- **Advance criterion** — The specified number of consecutive correct performances required before moving to the next approximation.
- **Step-back rule** — The specified conditions under which the practitioner returns to a previously successful approximation.
- **Prompt** — A stimulus that assists the initiation or performance of a behaviour.
- **Prompt hierarchy** — The ordering of prompts from least to most intrusive: natural cue, gesture, verbal direction, visual prompt, modelling, hand-over-hand, physical.
- **Fading** — The systematic withdrawal of a prompt across successive trials, with reinforcement transferred to the unprompted behaviour.
- **Prompt dependence** — A state in which the behaviour occurs only when the prompt is delivered and does not persist when the prompt is faded.
- **Differential reinforcement of other behaviour (DRO)** — Reinforcement of any behaviour other than the target behaviour.
- **Differential reinforcement of an incompatible behaviour (DRI)** — Reinforcement of a behaviour that cannot be performed at the same time as the target behaviour.
- **Differential reinforcement of other behaviour and the target (DRT/DRA)** — Reinforcement of the target behaviour in the presence of the target stimulus and of an alternative in its absence.
- **Differential reinforcement of compliance (DRC)** — Reinforcement of compliance with a demand, combined with the extinction of escalation.
- **Extinction** — The discontinuation of reinforcement, producing a decline in the behaviour.
- **Extinction burst** — A temporary increase in the frequency or intensity of behaviour at the beginning of extinction.
- **Escape-maintained behaviour** — Behaviour that is maintained by the removal or postponement of a demand; the escalation is the behaviour working, not failing.
- **Contingency management** — The deliberate and negotiated restructuring of the reinforcement structure of a setting, with specified consequences, trained staff, and stated review points.
- **Token economy** — A system in which desired behaviour is reinforced with tokens exchangeable for backup reinforcers.
- **Backup reinforcer** — The item or privilege for which tokens are exchanged.
- **Behaviour contract** — A written agreement specifying what each party will do and the consequences, with a review date and signatures.
- **Reciprocity in a contract** — The principle that the adult also makes promises and is also bound by the stated consequences.
- **Negotiated alternative** — A technique in which the young person proposes what they will do in exchange for what they want, and the agreement is negotiated from that proposal.
- **Contingency fading** — The planned and staged withdrawal of a contingency as the behaviour becomes self-sustaining.
- **Antecedent behaviour consequence (ABC) data** — The observational record of the sequence surrounding a behaviour, used to generate and refine functional hypotheses.
- **Dignity cost** — The interpersonal cost to the young person of being managed by a reward and consequence system.
- **Contingency management for substance use** — The application of reinforcement principles to the treatment of substance use disorders, with unusually large measured effects.
- **Fidelity of implementation** — The extent to which an intervention is delivered as designed, measured and reported alongside the outcome.
:::

## 14. Chapter Summary

Operant conditioning is the branch of the behavioural tradition concerned with behaviour that operates on the environment, and its central claim is that behaviour is selected by its consequences. The claim is simultaneously the theory, the method, and the clinical proposition: if a behaviour is selected by a particular consequence, then changing the consequence changes the behaviour, and nothing about the behaviour itself needs to be interpreted in order to intervene.

The chapter traced the lineage from Thorndike's puzzle box, through Skinner's three-term contingency and functional analysis, into the ward-based token economies of the 1950s, the parent training literature, and the classroom applications. The specification of a reinforcer was treated as a clinical task requiring three methods — observation, interview, and testing — and the reinforcer survey was presented as the most economical way of finding a usable reinforcer, with the important observation that the young person's, the parents', and the school's answers frequently do not intersect, and that the failure of the plan is usually determined at that intersection.

The schedule typology was presented in the detail in which it is used, with the characteristic response patterns of the four schedules and the clinical objectives for which each is appropriate, and the two practical consequences were drawn out: that a small daily bonus is a fixed-ratio schedule and produces end-of-day bursts rather than continuous performance, and that a young person checked weekly will work at the end of the week. The distinction between acquisition and maintenance schedules was developed, with the thinning rule and the transition to natural consequences, and the three forms of differential reinforcement were set out with the clinical argument for differential reinforcement of compliance, which is the technique that permits a practitioner to work on a problem behaviour while maintaining a positive relationship.

The management of escape-maintained behaviour was treated at length, on the grounds that it is the most practically important single category in applied practice. The circularity was described — the adult's response to the escalation is the maintaining contingency — and the five-step procedure was set out, and the systematic failure of punishment in this case was explained rather than merely reported: punishment teaches the young person that adults become larger, leaves the avoidance untouched, and does not teach compliance.

Contingency management was defined as the negotiated restructuring of a setting's reinforcement structure, and its eight-step procedure was specified, with the training of staff identified as the step most often omitted and most often responsible for programme failure. Behaviour contracts were described with the principle of reciprocity, and the three limits that apply to contracts with minors were stated: that the consequences must be within the adult's power to deliver, that no contract may trade a young person's rights, and that the contract must be genuinely voluntary.

The case of Ahmed illustrated the discipline's central discipline and its central danger. The functional analysis of his refusal to eat was elegant, and it was wrong, because the restriction had begun with an unexplained illness and the contingency analysis could not see it. The multi-disciplinary sequence, the medical assessment preceding the behavioural work, the graded hierarchy, the elimination of the "healthy lunch club" arrangement, the removal of the attention that the adults believed was encouraging, and the mother's anxiety as the true clinical task, are all described in the case.

The evidence is strong for parent training, for token economies in educational settings, for differential reinforcement, and above all for contingency management in substance use. The limitations are the dominance of the younger age range and of oppositional behaviour, the predominance of behavioural over experiential outcomes, the dependence on distinctive settings, and the substantial allegiance effects.

The ethical analysis addresses the market objection to reward-based practice seriously and derives four principles from it: never contract for what should not be traded, reinforce the behaviour rather than the practitioner's preferences, prefer natural consequences, and be honest about the effect of the system on the relationship. The cultural analysis addresses the cultural specificity of reinforcers, the family's existing systems of reward, the public humiliation that a visible system can produce, and the question of the professional's standing to set contingencies. The ethical requirements are listed in full, and the principle that a programme a young person cannot meet is a programme that must change rather than a young person who must be punished closes the section.

## 16. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 26.1 — The Schedule Audit (75 minutes, individual)

Take a real behaviour in a placement or in your own life and identify the actual schedule on which its consequences are delivered. Work out the fixed or variable ratio or interval, the typical magnitude of the consequence, and the delay between the behaviour and the consequence. Then redesign the schedule to produce the behaviour you actually want, and write down what the young person or other person would experience as different. The exercise teaches that most real-world problems with contingency management are problems with the schedule rather than with the reinforcer.

### Activity 26.2 — Reinforcer Survey with Triangulation (90 minutes, field-based)

Conduct a short reinforcer survey with a young person, a parent, and a teacher, using the same ten questions for each. Tabulate the three answers and identify the intersection and the divergences. Then test one candidate reinforcer by delivering it contingent on a specific behaviour and observing what happens. Most students find that the intersection is smaller than they expected, and that the divergences are the most informative part of the exercise.

### Activity 26.3 — Escape-Maintained Analysis (90 minutes, small group)

In groups of three, take a real behaviour that is described as "non-compliance" or "defiance" in a placement. Map the escalating sequence in detail: what does the adult say, what does the young person do, what does the adult do next, and what does the young person do then. Identify the point at which the demand is withdrawn and the consequence that produces. Then write the five-step plan for the case, with the differential reinforcement of compliance and the alternative behaviour specified. The most common finding is that the escalation is a well-rehearsed and effective request for help.

### Activity 26.4 — Writing a Behaviour Contract (75 minutes, individual)

Draft a behaviour contract for a realistic situation with a young person, with the adult also bound by specific promises. Specify the review date, the exceptions, and the consequences of each party's failure to comply. Then mark the draft, and identify the two places in it where you have contracted for something that should not be traded, and rewrite them. The second half of the exercise is the one that matters, and most drafts fail it in at least one place.

### Activity 26.5 — The Fade Plan (60 minutes, individual)

Take a prompting procedure you have used or observed and write a full fade plan: the prompt hierarchy from least to most intrusive, the number of trials at each level, the advance criterion, the step-back rule, and the schedule for the transfer of the prompt from the clinic to a school or home setting. Then write what you would do if the behaviour deteriorated at the third level. The transfer step is the one that most hand-written plans omit and the one that most often causes the treatment to fail.

## 17. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the principles.** Read Skinner (1938) and then the Ferster and Skinner (1957) schedules volume in excerpt, and read the Milner (1953) laboratory chapters on reinforcement and extinction. The laboratory texts are worth the effort, because the clinical vocabulary was built in them and a student who has read them will find the clinical literature much easier.

**For the clinical craft.** Read the parent training literature, which is the most practically useful behavioural literature for work with young people, and read the parent guides that accompany it. Then read the applied texts on differential reinforcement, which contain the practical detail the theory does not.

**For the escape-maintained pattern.** Read the classic functional analysis of oppositional behaviour with a child and family, and then read the critical literature on the behavioural perspective with families, which is where the discipline's most serious self-criticism is to be found.

**For the evidence.** Read the Cochrane reviews of parent training and the meta-analyses of token economies in educational settings, and read the contingency management literature in substance use, which is the most methodologically mature part of the applied evidence base.

**For the ethics.** Read the ethical guidelines for the behavioural approaches and the literature on reward-based parenting, and read the critical accounts of the token economy in institutional settings. The historical material is the best preparation for the consent and documentation work in Part VIII.
