
## 7. Ecological Assessment

An ecological assessment can be organised as a visual map with the young person at the centre and rings for family, peers, school, services, community, culture, policy, and time.

For each ring, ask:

- What pressures operate here?
- What resources operate here?
- Who has power?
- What relationship connects this ring to the others?
- What can be changed, by whom, and how quickly?
- What must be endured safely while longer change is pursued?

The map should include online environments and informal supports: extended kin, neighbours, coaches, faith-neutral community groups where relevant, youth workers, cultural organisations, libraries, and safe public spaces.

Assessment also examines service ecology. Multiple professionals may each hold a partial plan. The young person repeats their story, receives contradictory advice, and attends appointments that conflict with school. Coordination is a clinical intervention.

## 8. Multi-Level Intervention

### Individual level

Skills, therapy, health care, sleep, learning support, assistive technology, and opportunities for mastery.

### Relational level

Parent coaching, family communication, mentoring, peer mediation where safe, restorative conversations, and repair with teachers.

### School level

Belonging, accessible curriculum, anti-bullying systems, fair discipline, staff training, flexible pathways, and student voice.

### Community level

Youth provision, sport, arts, libraries, safe transport, volunteering, employment experience, and culturally specific resources.

### Policy and institutional level

Housing, income support, disability access, interpreter services, exclusion reform, health access, and data monitoring for inequity.

The counsellor need not personally deliver every intervention. Professional competence includes referral, consultation, advocacy, letter writing, meeting facilitation, and follow-through.

## 9. Case Illustration: The “Resilient” Young Carer

**Presentation.** Ruth, sixteen, is praised by school for excellent attendance and care of her younger brother, who has complex medical needs. She asks for counselling because she has stopped applying to sixth-form courses and cries on the bus.

**Ecological formulation.** Ruth's strengths are real: organisation, empathy, academic ability, and a trusted aunt. The costs are also real. She administers medication before school, translates medical information, sleeps lightly in case her brother needs help, and has no unstructured peer time. Her mother works nights and is exhausted. A school attendance award has unintentionally become pressure never to be ill or late.

**Intervention.** The counsellor resists congratulating Ruth into further endurance. A care assessment is requested; respite and a young-carers service are explored; the hospital provides an adult interpreter and written care plan so Ruth is not the clinical intermediary. School offers a later start after hospital nights and a private route to report absence. Ruth joins a weekly art group protected from caring duties. Her mother and aunt create an emergency rota so Ruth is not the default responder.

Ruth applies to sixth form. The change is not that she becomes more resilient. The ecology becomes less dependent on extracting resilience from her.

## 10. School and Community Applications

Schools can build resilience by ensuring every student has at least one known trusted adult, predictable routines, fair discipline, accessible challenge, extracurricular belonging, anti-bullying enforcement, and early practical help. Universal provision matters because targeted programmes can stigmatise and miss students whose adversity is hidden.

Community resilience requires investment before crisis. Libraries, youth clubs, safe parks, transport, sports, arts, and culturally led organisations are mental health infrastructure even when not labelled as such. Counsellors should know local assets and help young people enter them, including addressing cost, travel, disability access, and fear of being an outsider.

## 11. Evidence and Limitations

Longitudinal research consistently identifies supportive relationships, effective caregiving, self-regulation, school engagement, and community resources as predictors of better adaptation under adversity. Interventions are most credible when they strengthen these ordinary systems.

Resilience research has limitations. Definitions and outcomes vary; many studies are observational; and positive adaptation in one domain may conceal distress in another. Cultural meanings of success differ: independence, academic achievement, family contribution, spiritual life, and collective wellbeing may be weighted differently, though this textbook does not prescribe faith-based goals.

The central ethical limitation is political. Resilience language can shift responsibility from institutions to children. The discipline is to pair every individual strength with an environmental question: What support makes this strength sustainable? What burden should adults remove?

## 12. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Resilience** — Successful adaptation under adversity through interacting individual and environmental processes.
- **Microsystem** — Immediate settings in which a young person directly participates.
- **Mesosystem** — Connections among microsystems, such as family–school communication.
- **Exosystem** — Indirect settings affecting development, such as parental employment conditions.
- **Macrosystem** — Cultural, legal, and economic structures shaping development.
- **Chronosystem** — The dimension of time, transitions, and historical context.
- **Promotive factor** — A resource associated with positive outcomes across risk levels.
- **Developmental cascade** — Spreading effects of change across domains and time.
:::

## 13. Chapter Summary

Ecological and strengths-based practice widens the counsellor's field of vision. The young person remains an agent, but not an isolated one. Good practice identifies risk without fatalism, names strengths without exploitation, and intervenes at the level where change is both possible and just.
