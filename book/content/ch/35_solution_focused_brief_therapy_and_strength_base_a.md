# Solution-Focused Brief Therapy and Strength-Based Practice

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Explain the development of the solution-focused school and the clinical problems that produced it.
- State the core assumptions of solution-focused practice and explain the clinical argument for each.
- Describe the five core skills: the miracle question, exception questions, the past question, the coping question, and the goal.
- Describe the scaling questions and explain their clinical use.
- Explain the concepts of the solution and the exception and their relationship to the problem.
- Apply the solution-focused approach to a case involving an adolescent and a family.
- Critically evaluate the cultural and ethical dimensions of solution-focused work, including the questions of language, of hope, and of the treatment of the problem.

## Chapter Overview

The solution-focused school began with a clinical observation that sounds trivial and is not. In the late 1970s, the family therapists in Milwaukee asked a family what had changed since their first appointment, and the answer was frequently: *nothing much*. The question was so ordinary that it reorganised the field, because it exposed the fact that the therapists had been working exclusively on the problem and had never asked what was working.

The approach that grew out of that question is a short-term, future-oriented, and deliberately non-exploratory therapy. Its core discipline is the **exception** — the times when the problem did not occur, or occurred less, or was less intense — and its argument is that the exceptions contain the solution, because the factors that are present in the exceptions and absent in the problem are the factors that produce the change.

The approach has become one of the most widely used in the world, and it is worth understanding why. The reasons are partly clinical and partly economic. It is short, which matters in services with waiting lists. It is compatible with a wide range of theories, so it can be used as a common language in a team. It does not require the client to have insight, which matters with young people. And it produces a specific, concrete, and time-limited goal, which is exactly what a family in crisis needs.

The approach's limits are equally real and worth stating at the outset. Its emphasis on the future can be experienced by some young people as a refusal to engage with a present that is intolerable, and the field's own term for this — **the tyranny of the positive** — was coined by a critic of the approach. Its language is relentlessly positive, and there are contexts in which positivity is a form of denial. And the evidence base, while good, is concentrated in specific populations and specific contexts.

This chapter presents the theory, the skills, the applications with young people, the evidence, and the critical analysis.

## 1. Historical Context

### 1.1 The Milwaukee brief therapy project

The brief therapy project at the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee in the late 1970s was a research programme into the common factors in psychotherapy, and it was led by Steve de Shazer, whose background was in behaviour change and in communication theory.

Two features of the project were decisive. First, it was **empirical in a way that family therapy had not been**: the therapists systematically recorded the sessions, and they recorded the specific verbal and nonverbal behaviours of the therapists, and they analysed what the therapists actually did that distinguished the sessions in which clients improved. Second, the analysis found that the therapists' **questioning** accounted for a substantial proportion of the variance in outcome, and that the questions that predicted improvement were questions about the future, about the exceptions, and about the client's own resources.

The finding was not that the therapeutic relationship did not matter; the project's later work found that the relationship was a precondition. The finding was that, once the relationship was established, the specific behaviours of the therapist predicted the outcome, and that those behaviours were largely a matter of the questions asked.

### 1.2 The solution-focused brief therapy

The clinical practice that followed developed the model's techniques into the five core skills, and the practice was taught in a distinctive way: through a formal **training in listening**, in which the trainee learns to identify the client's own solution language as it appears in the conversation and to build the session around it. The training is unusually demanding and it is unusually effective.

The school's influence has been enormous and is best seen in the fact that solution-focused language is now used in services that have no connection to the school: in motivational interviewing, in coaching, in school counselling, in the management of chronic conditions, in the work with homelessness, and in the school-based prevention programmes of the 2000s.

### 1.3 The wider strength-based movement

Solution-focused practice is part of a larger movement that includes the strengths perspective in social work, the resilience literature in developmental science, the positive psychology that emerged in the 1990s, and the practice-based evidence movement. The shared proposition is that the questions asked of a client, a family, or a community determine the kind of information that the system produces, and that a system that is asked only about problems will produce only problems.

The proposition is not merely rhetorical. It has a technical consequence that a student should be able to state: **the questions a practitioner asks determine what the practitioner can see.** A practitioner who asks only about the problem will see the problem, and will become an expert on the problem, and will find that the problem is resistant, and will conclude that the client is resistant.

## 2. The Core Assumptions

### 2.1 The assumptions

The solution-focused approach rests on a small number of assumptions, and each of them has a clinical consequence.

**Every client has resources and strengths.** The assumption is not that the client is well or that the problem is not real; it is that the resources for the change exist somewhere, and the clinical task is to find them.

**Clients are the experts on their own lives.** The practitioner does not interpret, does not diagnose, and does not tell the client what is happening. The practitioner asks questions that help the client notice what they are already doing that works.

**Change has already happened.** This is the assumption that most distinguishes the approach and the one that most needs explanation. The client's life contains times when the problem was better, smaller, absent, or different, and these times are the raw material of the work. The exceptions are not a distraction from the problem; they are the solution in embryo.

**The future is the focus of the work.** The emphasis is on the next small step rather than on the last big one.

**The small can be a catalyst.** A small step that is achievable and that the client can verify produces a momentum that is disproportionate to its size, and the reason is that the small step is also evidence.

**Cooperation is the key.** The practitioner and the client work together on the problem, and the practitioner brings the question and the client brings the answer.

**The problem is separate from the person.** The person is not the problem, and this is the reframe that the approach offers to every client.

### 2.2 The solution and the exception

The **solution** is the behaviour, or the state of affairs, that the client wants more of. It is defined by the client, in the client's own words, and it may be entirely outside the problem: a young person who is being bullied may want to be "not frightened in the corridor", and that is the solution, and it is not the same as "not being bullied", and the distinction matters because the second may not be within the client's control.

The **exception** is a time when the problem did not occur, or occurred less severely, or was managed differently, and the exception is the search for the causal factors. The practitioner asks: what was different? What did you do? Who was there? What was happening in the other part of your life? The answers identify the factors that are present in the exception and absent in the problem.

**The exception is not the same as the solution.** The solution is what the client wants more of. The exception is a time when the client got some of it. The distinction is clinically important because the exception is evidence and the solution is the target, and a client who is asked to find the solution before the exceptions have been explored will often offer an aspiration rather than a description.

## 3. The Five Core Skills

### 3.1 The miracle question

**"Suppose that for some reason the problem got solved. What would be different?"** and then, if the client needs it, **"and what would you be doing?"** and **"and what would other people notice?"**

The miracle question is the signature technique of the approach, and its logic is worth understanding. It is a question about the future, it is deliberately large, and it is deliberately counterfactual. The counterfactual does the work: asking a young person to imagine the problem solved produces a description of the consequences of the solution, and the description is of a world in which the problem is not there, which is information the client has and the practitioner does not.

**The therapist's discipline.** The therapist must resist the strong pull to ask the question in a way that suggests the answer, to ask a "realistic" version, or to move on to the next question. The miracle question works because it is asked as a genuine open question and because the client is allowed to answer it with something entirely impractical, and the impractical answer is often the most informative.

**With young people.** The miracle question is often better asked in the future tense and in the concrete: "If the thing with school got sorted out, what would your Tuesday look like?" The concrete form produces more usable detail.

### 3.2 Exception questions

Exception questions ask the client to describe a time when the problem did not happen, or happened less, or was handled better.

**"Was there a recent time when this was not a problem?"** and then, when the client has found one, **"What was different then?"** and **"What did you do that was different?"** and **"Who else was involved?"** and **"What was going on in the rest of your life at that time?"**

The exception questions have two functions. They **locate the solution** — because the factors present in the exception are the factors that make the difference. And they **disrupt the problem's hold** — because the problem's apparent all-encompassing quality is itself a distortion, and the exception is the data that corrects it.

**The therapist's discipline with exceptions.** The therapist must find a *recent* exception, and must resist the client's tendency to produce a very old one or one that is so distant that it is not usable. And the therapist must treat the exception as informative rather than as a lucky accident, because the treatment of the exception as an accident teaches the client that exceptions are rare and the search is hopeless.

**The exception and the small step.** The exceptions also identify the small steps, and the small steps are what the client does between sessions.

### 3.3 The past question

**"Was there a time when this was better than it is now? Or a time when it was even worse?"**

The past question serves a specific purpose: it locates the trend. A client who can identify that the problem has been improving slowly, however slightly, has a very different relationship with it from a client who experiences it as static or worsening, and the first client can be asked what has been helping.

The past question is also the technique for the young person who presents with a sense of hopelessness, because the question does not presume improvement: it asks about a time when it was *worse*, and the direction of travel is then established from the client's own material.

### 3.4 The coping question

**"What have you been doing to cope with this?"** and **"What is it that keeps you going?"** and **"How have you managed to keep going?"**

The coping question is the approach's answer to the question of resources, and it has a specific and important target: the coping that the client has developed is a *skill*, and the skill is usually already partly generalised, and the therapist's task is to make it explicit and to extend it.

**With a young person who is in crisis, the coping question is often the first productive question in the entire session**, because it honours what they have done, and because the answer is usually more concrete than the description of the problem.

### 3.5 The goal

The **goal** is the specific, achievable, and time-limited target that the client sets, and the setting of a goal is the last and most important of the core skills.

**A well-formed goal** is: specific, so that the client will know when it has been achieved; achievable, so that the client believes it; and bounded in time, so that it can be checked.

The boundedness in time is the element most often missed, and it is what makes the goal a tool rather than a wish. "By the end of next week, I will have texted my cousin twice" is a well-formed goal. "I will be better" is not.

**The therapist's discipline.** The therapist does not set the goal, and does not improve it, and does not suggest a goal when the client is silent. A goal that the therapist has proposed and the client has accepted is not a solution-focused goal.

### 3.6 Scaling questions

The **scaling** technique asks the client to rate a dimension on a scale, and it is the most flexible of the school's techniques.

**"On a scale of zero to ten, where zero is the worst it has ever been and ten is the best you can imagine, where are you now?"** and then, **"And what would one point higher on the scale look like?"** and **"And what would one point lower look like?"**

The three questions are the whole technique, and each does something different. The **current rating** locates the client. The **"one point higher"** question identifies the immediate next step, and it is remarkable how much more specific a one-point shift is than the miracle question, because the one-point shift is within the client's own frame of reference for the scale and is therefore grounded in their own experience of the dimension. The **"one point lower"** question identifies what the client fears, and it is the question that surfaces the risk, and it is the question that the therapist should ask when the client is becoming complacent.

**Scaling in the session.** The scaling technique is also used on the *session* itself: "On a scale of zero to ten, how useful was that last ten minutes for you?" The practice of scaling the session and scaling the goal is one of the most distinctive and most immediately usable elements of the approach, and it is a good way for a student to begin practising the model.

### 3.7 Solution talk and problem talk

The most reliable single indicator of whether a conversation is solution-focused is the language it uses, and the distinction is worth learning as a listening skill.

**Problem talk** describes the problem in the third person, in the general, in the past, and in the abstract: *the thing with school*, *my son and his behaviour*, *I am always fighting with her*, *things have been really bad lately*. It is generalised, it excludes the client, and it contains no specific moment and no verifiable detail.

**Solution talk** describes a specific moment, in the present or the recent past, with the client in the first person, and with a detail that can be verified: *on Tuesday I got up at eight and caught the bus*, *last week I told my tutor I was coming back and then I left*, *I ate lunch in the canteen on Thursday and Finn was there*.

The practitioner's task in the conversation is to shift the talk from the first to the second, and the shift is accomplished not by telling the client to be more specific but by modelling the question that invites it. "Tell me about the problem with school" produces problem talk. "Tell me about the last time you went to school and it was okay" produces solution talk. "What did you do this morning?" produces solution talk.

Two practical notes follow. First, the shift is often achieved with a single word — the past tense for a future question, the first person for a third person. Second, and more importantly, the shift is a *social* process rather than a technical one: the client will begin to use solution talk when they experience that the practitioner is interested in the times things went better, and this is experienced as a change in what the practitioner wants, not as a change in the client's own thinking.

### 3.8 The practice in other settings

The solution-focused approach has been adopted more widely outside the therapy room than any other model in this book, and a student preparing for practice should know how it transfers.

**In schools.** The approach is used in school counselling with a striking consistency, and the reason is that it is quick, that it produces a written goal that the young person can take to the classroom, and that it does not require the young person to disclose. A school-based solution-focused session with a young person who is refusing to attend can be complete in fifteen minutes and can yield a goal that the form tutor can act on.

**In motivational interviewing.** The two approaches share a great deal: the focus on the client's own language, the focus on discrepancies between the stated values and the current behaviour, the resistance to telling the client what to do, and the eliciting of the client's own reasons for change. The scaling question and the "one point lower" question are structurally similar to the confidence ruler in motivational interviewing, and a practitioner who knows both will find the combination very powerful.

**In health and chronic illness.** The approach has been applied to diabetes management, to chronic pain, to medication adherence, and to weight management, and the clinical logic is the same: the exception search identifies what makes the behaviour possible, and the goal is a behaviour.

**In the justice system.** Solution-focused brief therapy has a substantial evidence base in community justice settings, and the reason is both clinical and practical: the clients are often hostile to the institution, they are often mandated, and the approach asks them no more than it needs to ask and produces a concrete action.

**In homelessness services.** The approach has produced some of the most encouraging results in the brief intervention literature, in a population that is typically difficult to engage and typically dropped out.

**With families in poverty.** The strengths-based practice literature, which grew out of the solution-focused work, has made a distinctive and important contribution: it has shown that the conventional deficit-based assessment systematically misdescribes families who are poor, because the conventional instruments ask about problems and the families have plenty, and the strengths-based instruments ask what is working and the families also have plenty, and the difference in the resulting assessment is a different family.
