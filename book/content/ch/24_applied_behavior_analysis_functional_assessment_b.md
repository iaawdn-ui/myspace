## 7. Evidence and Research Support

### 7.1 The status of the evidence for the method

The evidence for applied behaviour analysis is unusual in structure. The method itself — the logic of the functional analysis — is not an intervention and is not directly subject to randomised evaluation. What has been evaluated, extensively, are the interventions that the method produces, and the overall conclusion of the systematic reviews is that behavioural interventions based on a functional assessment are effective across a wide range of behaviours and settings, with effect sizes that are among the largest reported in the psychological literature.

The more useful evaluation for a student concerns the components. Three findings recur.

**Function-based interventions outperform non-function-based interventions.** The reviews consistently find that interventions built on a functional analysis produce larger and more durable effects than interventions built on a behaviour-reduction logic. This is the single most important empirical finding in the applied literature and it is exactly the finding the theory predicts.

**The functional analysis itself is not always necessary.** For many common problems, a well-conducted descriptive assessment combined with a clear rationale produces results comparable to those from a full experimental analysis, and the reviews find no reliable superiority of the experimental design for all problems. This is an important and humbling finding, and it should temper the discipline's enthusiasm for its own most elaborate technique.

**The quality of the implementation determines the effect.** Studies that measure treatment fidelity and find low implementation fidelity report null results that reflect the implementation rather than the intervention. The field's insistence on fidelity measurement is, in effect, a refusal to draw conclusions from data it knows to be contaminated.

### 7.2 The methodological contribution

The methodological contribution of applied behaviour analysis to the wider profession is considerable, and three items deserve naming.

**Single-case experimental designs.** The logic of demonstrating a functional relation in an individual case, using repeated measurement across conditions, is a genuine and rigorous methodology, and its influence extends far beyond the discipline.

**Fidelity measurement.** The systematic measurement of whether an intervention was delivered as intended, and the reporting of that measurement alongside the outcome, is a standard that the whole profession has adopted.

**Operational definitions as a professional discipline.** The insistence on defining behaviour in terms that another person could count, and the practice of recording those definitions, has made it possible for professionals in different settings to talk about the same behaviour. The language of applied behaviour analysis is now the shared language of much of clinical practice, and it is a more precise language than the one that preceded it.

### 7.3 Limitations of the evidence

Three limitations should be stated.

**The literature is dominated by specific target behaviours**, many of them in institutional settings, and the generalisation to the kinds of problems that dominate counselling practice in the community is not straightforward.

**Many studies lack independent replication**, and the fields in which the discipline has been most active have attracted more scrutiny than replication.

**The outcome measures are almost always behavioural**, and the measures of the young person's quality of life, of the family's experience, and of the durability of the change in naturalistic settings are less systematically reported, and the 1993 revisions of the applied research criteria were directed at precisely this gap.

## 8. Strengths and Limitations of the Functional Assessment Approach

### 8.1 Strengths

**It is testable.** The distinguishing feature. Most of the profession's diagnostic formulations are not capable of being refuted by data, and a formulation that cannot be refuted cannot be evaluated.

**It is practical.** The method produces a specific, implementable plan, and it produces the data by which the plan can be monitored.

**It is transparent.** The plan can be written down, explained to the young person and the family, and handed to a teacher.

**It treats the young person as an individual.** A functional analysis produces an explanation that is specific to one person in one context, which is what a young person is entitled to.

**It generates the alternative behaviour from the analysis.** The most useful feature of a function-based approach is that the replacement behaviour is derived from the function, and is therefore likely to be effective and is likely to be taught in a way the young person can use.

### 8.2 Limitations

**It is only as good as the data.** The method's precision is a promise it cannot always keep. Poor operational definitions, low-fidelity recording, and observers who are not trained produce confident conclusions from unreliable data.

**The hypotheses can be narrow.** The four functional classes are a convenient taxonomy, and the pressure to fit a case into one of them is real. Behaviour that serves multiple functions, or a function that is not in the taxonomy, is a persistent source of error.

**It can be used to justify control.** This is the serious criticism, and it has been made from inside the discipline as well as outside it. A functional analysis of a young person's protest behaviour can produce a technically correct plan for extinguishing the protest, and the technical correctness of the plan says nothing about the justice of it. The discipline's answer is that the ethical criteria govern the choice of targets, not merely the choice of technique, and that the young person's assent and quality of life are criteria of success. That answer is right in principle and requires constant attention in practice.

**It under-accounts for context.** A functional analysis conducted in a contrived setting may not generalise, and the setting demands of the analysis are themselves a simplification of a complex situation.

**It requires resources.** The method asks for trained observers, structured recording, stable environments, and the cooperation of several people. In a service with high caseloads and low continuity, it is expensive, and the honest practitioner must sometimes say so.

## 9. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 The young person's own account

A young person who is being assessed is usually the last to be consulted and the first to be surprised. The practice of functional assessment with minors has been the subject of sustained criticism, and the criticisms are worth taking seriously.

**The asymmetry of power.** The assessment is conducted by an adult with authority over the young person, and it is directed at changing the young person's behaviour for the benefit, as often as not, of adults and institutions. The young person may have no understanding of why the assessment is happening, and may not be in a position to decline it.

**The young person is not a passive object of analysis.** A young person who is being observed in a corridor knows they are being observed, and will behave accordingly, and the behaviour the observer records will be shaped by the observation. A young person who knows that the analysis is looking for the function of a behaviour can be, in the most benign sense, strategic about it.

**Assessment should include the young person's own account.** Where the behaviour has a communicative function, the young person's own description of it is the most direct evidence of the function available, and it should be sought as early as possible, in a form that does not require the young person to explain themselves fluently. A young person who says "I do it so they send me home" has told you the function, and no amount of ABC recording will improve on it.

### 9.2 Assent, withdrawal, and the limits of the method

The discipline's ethical requirements, which are more developed than those of most of the profession, are worth stating in full because they are a model of how such requirements should work.

**Informed consent and assent.** The parent or guardian consents to the assessment; the young person assents, and assent requires the capacity to decline. For a young person who cannot understand the procedure, the assessment should not proceed in its full form.

**The right to withdraw.** The young person may withdraw at any point, and the consequences of withdrawal are stated in advance, so that withdrawal is genuinely available and not punished.

**The least restrictive effective procedure.** The least intrusive method that can produce the required result is used, and the consideration of alternatives is documented.

**Aversive procedures are not used.** The contemporary discipline does not use contingent aversive stimuli as a planned component of treatment.

**Protection from harm.** Procedures are designed so that they do not produce harm, and the conditions involving deprivation or isolation are used only under specified safeguards and with a full risk assessment.

**The participant's quality of life as an outcome.** The 1993 criteria require that the intervention improve the participant's quality of life, and this is the most novel and most important of the requirements, because it makes the young person's own experience of the treatment a criterion of its success rather than an afterthought.

### 9.3 The conditions that require particular care

Two conditions in the traditional functional analysis require specific safeguards and should never be run without them.

**The alone condition** involves leaving a young person alone, which for a young person with a history of abuse, with separation anxiety, or with self-injury is a substantial risk. The condition should be brief, should be conducted with a clear observation protocol and an immediate response plan, and in many cases should be replaced by a condition that is observationally similar without requiring separation.

**The demand condition** requires the deliberate presentation of demands to a young person, and where the young person has trauma-related responses, presenting demands in an unrepresentative way can produce a re-experiencing. The demands should be ordinary, should be brief, and the young person should be able to end the condition.

**The attention condition** requires a planned interaction in which a young person's behaviour is met with attention. Where the behaviour is aggression, this condition requires a specific safety protocol, and where the young person has been harmed by adults who were supposed to protect them, the condition can itself be a trigger. This is one of the points at which the applied literature and the trauma literature meet, and the trauma-informed position is that the attention condition with a survivor of adult maltreatment should be modified or replaced.

### 9.4 Cultural considerations

**The behaviours that get referred.** The referral pattern is itself culturally patterned, and a behavioural analysis conducted in isolation from the referral pattern can miss the context. A behaviour referred because it is disruptive in a classroom may be a behaviour that is a form of communication about something the family and the community have already identified as a problem and that the school has not.

**The function is a cultural attribution.** The same behaviour has different functions in different settings, and the practitioner who transfers a hypothesis from one setting to another without re-testing it is making a cultural error as well as a technical one.

**The contingencies of the wider community.** The reinforcement structure that the family operates within — the ways in which respect, knowledge, and belonging are distributed — will determine whether a clinic-based plan survives its contact with home. A plan that competes with the family's own logic of reinforcement will be defeated by it, and the defeat will be blamed on the family.

**The professional authority of the assessor.** In many settings, the assumption that an outside professional has the standing to set a young person's contingencies is itself a cultural assumption. A practitioner who arrives with a plan that has been designed without any consultation of the family is likely to encounter resistance that is rational and that should be understood on its own terms.

## 10. Case Illustration: The Young Person Who Became Quiet

**Presentation.** Priya is fifteen and attends a school that has asked for a behavioural assessment because, in the words of the referral, "she has become withdrawn and is not engaging." The school's concern is framed as a management problem: Priya is now spending her breaks in the library rather than in the corridor, she is declining to join in a weekly group session that her form tutor has organised, and she has stopped attending the school football team, which she had played in the previous year.

Her mother reports a different history. She says that Priya became "a different person" after a school trip in the previous March, that she now will not sleep in her own room, that she has started spending her nights in her mother's bedroom, and that she has twice been found in the bathroom at three in the morning.

**The first hypothesis, and why it was insufficient.** The first functional hypothesis, generated from the school's information, is that Priya's behaviour is escape-maintained: she avoids the corridor because of a peer, avoids the group session because of social anxiety, and avoids football because of the heat. The hypothesis is not unreasonable, and the evidence for it is thin. The problem is a methodological one and it is instructive: the hypothesis has been generated from the referral, and the referral is a description of a change in behaviour, not a record of the circumstances in which the behaviour occurs.

**The descriptive assessment.** The assessment proceeds in four stages.

*Record review.* The school incident log, the attendance register, the behaviour log, and the pastoral notes are gathered. The pattern that emerges is that Priya's attendance rose slightly over the year while her participation in anything requiring her to be approached by a group of peers fell sharply, and that the specific date of the change in the pastoral notes is the date of the trip. The attendance data are important because they are the kind of data that would have been overlooked: Priya is not absent. She is present and she is participating in nothing.

*Interviews.* The mother is interviewed with the specific questions — what happens right before Priya comes out of the bathroom at three in the morning, what happened on the trip. The form tutor is interviewed about the corridor at break and the group session. Priya is interviewed, and this is where the assessment is most easily and most consequentially mishandled, because the obvious move is to ask her why she has stopped playing football, and the answer she gives is a non-answer that will be read as evasion.

*The young person's own account.* Priya is asked what has changed, and she says, "Everyone was so happy about it that I couldn't say anything." Asked what she means, she says that there were photos, and that someone said they would send the photos around, and that "now people look at me different." She is not willing to say more. This is the single most informative datum in the entire assessment and it is the datum that a purely behavioural account of a sixteen-year-old's withdrawal would never have obtained.

*Direct observation.* The counsellor arranges to be present in the corridor, the library, and the changing room at break over several days, recording the antecedents and the consequences of Priya's behaviour in the ABC format, and observing who approaches her and what follows her refusals.

**The ABC data.** The record is consistent. Priya's behaviour — spending her break in the library, declining the group session, declining football — is preceded by a consistent antecedent: an approach by one or more peers, sometimes with a question, sometimes with a comment. It is followed by a consistent consequence: the peers leave. In the case of the group session, she is called out of it; the coach reports that he is "used to it now."

**The revised hypothesis.** The behaviour is maintained by **escape from peer attention and from situations in which attention might be drawn to her**, and the escape is reliably produced because the peers have learned that insisting has no effect. The school has inadvertently reinforced the withdrawal by accepting it. The library has become a place where nothing is asked of her.

The hypothesis is *not* that Priya is depressed, and it is *not* that she is anxious in the general sense, and it is *not* that she needs a group session. The hypothesis is that she has learned that withdrawing is effective, and that the learning has been reinforced by a well-intentioned school, and that the reinforcement is now sustaining a pattern that is making her worse.

**The intervention.** Four elements.

*The data collection stops.* This is deliberate and it is the element that is hardest to sell, because it looks like doing nothing. The ABC recording was in the corridor and the library; it stops, because the young person has a right not to be observed for the purpose of being changed.

*The contingency is restructured.* The form tutor and the pastoral team agree a protocol: the group session becomes optional, with a stated alternative activity of equal social value; the football coach's invitation changes from a direct request, which invites a visible refusal, to an open, drop-in arrangement, so that attending is no longer an event that can be declined; and the library is staffed by a person rather than being unsupervised, so that the peers' attention can be redirected without a scene.

*No one asks her to explain.* The counsellor does not ask Priya to talk about the trip, does not ask to see the photographs, and does not ask her to confront anyone. The assessment produced a clear account of the contingencies; the contingencies are what is being changed, and the young person is not required to produce a narrative to justify the change.

*Safety and referral.* The night-time waking, the sleeping in the mother's bed, and the bathroom episodes are assessed as a separate matter, and the counsellor speaks to the mother about what the young person may be experiencing and what the mother can do, and the mother is supported in arranging a clinical referral for the trauma-specific assessment described in Chapter 38. The school counsellor and the trauma clinician coordinate, with the mother's and Priya's knowledge.

**The outcome.** Over the following two months, Priya's participation is measured with the school's ordinary registers, which is itself part of the plan, since a plan that generates a special measurement that stops when the plan ends is a plan that cannot be evaluated. Participation in the drop-in football increases. The group session remains declined, and this is accepted, because the hypothesis was about the contingencies and not about the specific activity. The night-time waking continues, and it is not a school matter.

**The interpretation.** This case demonstrates the central methodological point of the chapter: a functional analysis of Priya's behaviour would have produced a *correct* analysis of her school withdrawal and a plan for managing it, and the plan would have been appropriate, and it would have been insufficient. The behaviour at school was a coping response to something that happened elsewhere. The behavioural method was the right tool for the school question, and the school question was not the whole question, and a practitioner who had run the analysis and delivered the plan and stopped would have treated the part of the problem that was visible as though it were the problem.

The case also illustrates the point about the young person's own account. Priya's single sentence about the photographs contained the functional information that the ABC data would otherwise have taken six weeks to approach, and it contained the referral information that no behavioural analysis would have produced. The assessment that included it was better than the assessment that did not, and the reason is not that behavioural data are inferior; it is that behavioural data are data about behaviour, and the meaning of the behaviour was located elsewhere, in a photograph, on a trip, in a group of peers.

## 11. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Applied behaviour analysis (ABA)** — The discipline concerned with the analysis of functional relations between behaviour and the environment and with the development, from that analysis, of technologies for changing behaviour.
- **The 1968 criteria** — The five conditions that applied research must satisfy: an important problem, a measurable behaviour, a systematic environmental manipulation, a clear relation between manipulation and behaviour change, and a behaviourally defined outcome.
- **The 1993 revisions** — The additions of quality-of-life, generalisability, and social-validity requirements to the applied research criteria.
- **Unitarian Position** — The claim that a single set of principles, expressed at different levels of description, is sufficient to analyse all behaviour including complex human behaviour.
- **Functional relation** — A statement of covariation between behaviour and specified environmental conditions, established under experimental control.
- **Operational definition** — A precise, observable, measurable specification of a behaviour, including its context, its recording unit, and its intensity threshold.
- **Topography** — The physical and observable form of a behaviour, as distinct from its function.
- **The ABC model** — The recording framework for the Antecedent, the Behaviour, and the Consequence of a behaviour.
- **ABC narrative** — A prose record of the full sequence of events surrounding each occurrence of a behaviour.
- **ABC tally** — A compact tabular record of coded antecedents and consequences, used for aggregate analysis.
- **Functional hypothesis** — A specific and testable statement of what a behaviour accomplishes, formulated with the consequence, the setting, and the antecedent conditions.
- **Descriptive functional assessment** — A structured assessment using interviews, observation, and record review, without manipulating the environment; it can generate a hypothesis but cannot confirm one.
- **Hypothesis-testing observational design** — A design that tests a specific prediction using patterns in naturally occurring data, without experimental manipulation.
- **Prediction method** — The simplest hypothesis-testing design, in which a prediction derived from the hypothesis is checked against existing records.
- **ABC correlation method** — A design that examines whether the behaviour is reliably associated with particular consequences, compared against the base rate of those consequences in the setting.
- **Structured ABC method** — A design in which the recording procedure is deliberately modified to increase the reliability and detail of the antecedent and consequence data.
- **Base rate** — The frequency with which a consequence occurs in the setting independently of the behaviour; the comparison against which any contingency effect must be assessed.
- **Experimental functional analysis** — A single-case design in which the consequences following the behaviour are manipulated across conditions in order to test the functional hypothesis.
- **Traditional functional analysis** — The full experimental design with play, alone, demand, attention, and control conditions.
- **Brief functional analysis** — A shortened version of the experimental design, typically five-minute conditions, that can be completed in one or two sessions.
- **Practical functional analysis** — A modified experimental design developed for naturalistic settings and for use by caregivers and school staff.
- **Control condition** — A condition in which the consequence of interest is delivered non-contingently, to distinguish contingent delivery from mere presence.
- **Attention function** — The hypothesis that a behaviour is maintained by the delivery of social attention from others.
- **Escape or avoidance function** — The hypothesis that a behaviour is maintained by the removal or postponement of a demand or an aversive stimulus.
- **Access-to-tangibles function** — The hypothesis that a behaviour is maintained by the obtaining of a physical item or activity.
- **Automatic reinforcement** — Maintenance of a behaviour by its own consequences, without reference to the social environment.
- **Setting demands** — The automatic, attention, escape/avoidance, and combined demands that describe the circumstances under which a behaviour occurs, as distinct from the functional classes.
- **Play condition** — The condition in which the environment is arranged for the behaviour to occur freely, used to identify automatically maintained behaviour.
- **Alone condition** — The condition in which the young person is briefly left alone with an observer present; it requires explicit safeguards.
- **Demand condition** — The condition in which demands are made and escape is contingent on the behaviour, used to identify escape-maintained behaviour.
- **Provisional treatment** — A treatment implemented on the basis of a descriptive assessment, with the hypothesis explicitly treated as provisional and subject to continued testing.
- **Treatment fidelity** — The systematic measurement of whether an intervention was delivered as intended, reported alongside the outcome.
- **Single-case experimental design** — A design establishing a causal relation in an individual case through repeated measurement across conditions, with designs denoted ABAB, A-B-A-B, and BCAB.
- **Interobserver agreement** — The percentage of occasions on which two independent observers code the same behaviour identically; the basic reliability check of behavioural measurement.
- **Least restrictive effective procedure** — The requirement that the least intrusive method capable of producing the required result be used, with the consideration of alternatives documented.
- **Assent** — The young person's own agreement to participate, which requires the capacity to decline and which is distinct from the parent's consent.
- **Session-based data** — Data recorded in discrete, defined observation periods, as distinct from continuous recording.
- **The three-term contingency** — The behavioural relation in which a discriminative stimulus, a behaviour, and a consequence are specified together.
- **The behaviour analyst's ethical obligation to the participant** — The requirement that the participant's assent, welfare, and quality of life are criteria of the success of the intervention.
- **Outcome measurement** — The specification in advance of what will be measured, how, and over what period, in a form that can be compared across participants and across time.
:::

## 12. Chapter Summary

Applied behaviour analysis is a discipline and a practice, and the practice is the disciplined application of the analysis of functional relations to the behaviour of a specific individual in a specific setting. The founding definition of 1968 committed the field to being a natural science, to the analysis of functional relations, and to deriving its technologies from the science rather than from intuition, and the 1968 and 1993 sets of applied research criteria remain the sharpest instruments available for evaluating behavioural treatment research.

The method has four elements, and the name of each one is worth remembering: the operational definition, which fixes precisely what is being counted and how; the observation and recording system, which gathers the data; the functional hypothesis, which states what the behaviour accomplishes in a testable form; and the test, which checks the hypothesis against the data. A clinician who can produce a hypothesis but not a test has not completed an assessment, and the discipline is unusually clear about this.

The hierarchy of methods runs from the descriptive assessment, which gathers information and generates hypotheses but cannot confirm them; through the hypothesis-testing observational designs, which examine predictions in naturally occurring data; to the experimental functional analysis, which manipulates the consequences across conditions and can establish a functional relation. The chapter described the standard conditions, the practical and ethical difficulties of each, and the brief and practical modifications that do most of the clinical work in contemporary settings.

The four functional classes — attention, escape or avoidance, access to tangibles, and automatic reinforcement — and the three setting demands describe the circumstances under which a behaviour occurs and what it produces. The chapter emphasised that a functional analysis establishes what a behaviour *does* and says nothing whatever about what it *means*, and that both of these statements are simply true, and that the behavioural and humanistic traditions are joined at this point rather than divided by it.

With young people, the assessment must take account of the setting-specificity of function, the density and type of the contingencies available to them, the dominance of peer consequences, and the involvement of the adults who will carry the plan. Where a full analysis is not feasible, a provisional treatment is legitimate provided the hypothesis is treated as provisional and the review point is agreed in advance.

The evidence supports the interventions the method produces, and the most important finding in the reviews is that function-based interventions outperform non-function-based ones — which is exactly what the theory predicts. The experimental design is not always necessary, and the discipline's own reviews are candid about this. The methodological contributions — single-case designs, fidelity measurement, and the discipline of operational definition — have influenced the whole profession.

The ethical analysis is the sharpest in the book, because the field's history with children is genuinely troubled. The honest account is that serious errors were made, that they were substantive, and that the discipline's current standards are a direct product of them. The requirements that follow — assent and the right to withdraw, the least restrictive effective procedure, the protection of the young person during the alone and demand conditions, the treatment of the participant's quality of life as an outcome, and the inclusion of the young person's own account of the behaviour — are the field's own answer, and they are a model for the rest of the profession.

## 14. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 24.1 — Operationalising a Behaviour (60 minutes, individual)

Take a behaviour you have observed in placement and write three versions of its operational definition: the first at a level you would consider obviously too vague, the second at a level you think is defensible, and the third at a level of precision that would be impractical to record. Then write the recording unit for each and say which is the most likely to produce reliable data and why. The activity teaches the central lesson of the method, which is that the definition is not a preliminary to the work but a large part of the work itself.

### Activity 24.2 — The Record Review (90 minutes, individual or pair)

Obtain a school or service record for a period of at least a term — exclusion logs, incident reports, attendance records, nursing notes, or counselling session records — and analyse it for pattern rather than for totals. Plot the occurrences against time of day, day of week, and location, and look for the anomalies. Most records contain a pattern that nobody had noticed, and the exercise is the cheapest and most under-used component of a functional assessment. Report the pattern in three sentences and state what you would observe next.

### Activity 24.3 — The ABC Hypothesis Table (75 minutes, small group)

In groups of four, take a behaviour from a placement and complete the ABC analysis for a single observed occurrence. Then, working as a group, write the functional hypothesis in the required form — consequence, setting, and antecedent conditions — and then write the specific prediction that the hypothesis makes about the data. Finally, write the single observation that would refute the hypothesis. The final step is the one that separates a hypothesis from a guess, and groups consistently find it harder than the earlier steps.

### Activity 24.4 — A Provisional Plan with a Review Point (60 minutes, individual)

Design a provisional intervention for a behaviour for which a full analysis is not feasible. Write the plan, the data collection procedure, the fidelity check, and — crucially — the pre-agreed review point, with the date and the specific question to be asked at it: "has the hypothesis been tested?" Then write what you will do if the answer at the review point is no. The last part is the part that distinguishes a working professional from a person who has found a plausible explanation and stopped looking.
:::

## 15. Annotated Reading Pathways

**For the discipline.** Read Baer's founding paper and the 1973 and 1974 Unitarian Position papers in excerpt, and read a current introductory text on applied behaviour analysis. Then read the 1993 revision of the applied research criteria, which is short and which reframes the whole field.

**For the method.** Read the functional analysis literature from the 1980s, in which the standard design was established, and then read the brief and practical analysis literature, which is what you will actually use. A student who reads only the original literature will have the impression that functional analysis is a laboratory procedure.

**For the ethics.** Read the discipline's ethical guidelines in full, and read the critical literature on the history of the field's work with children. The pairing is the preparation for the consent and assent work in Part VIII and for the forms in Appendix A.

**For the evidence.** Read a systematic review of function-based interventions and a Cochrane review, and read a treatment fidelity study alongside them. The difference in the framing of the two is instructive.

**For the practice.** Read a single-case design handbook, and design a study of a real behaviour in a placement using a reversal or withdrawal design. Designing one is the fastest way to understand what the discipline's discipline actually consists of.


## Practice Laboratory and Extended Worked Example
This laboratory turns **Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment** into decisions that can be observed, supervised, and reviewed. The chapter's central concern is the ABC model, functional analysis, and the construction of hypotheses about why a behaviour occurs. In adolescent work, understanding the model is only the first step. The practitioner must also decide what to assess, what to postpone, which adults or institutions need to act, how consent will be maintained, and what evidence would show that the work is helping rather than merely continuing.
The organising focus for this part of the course is functional analysis, learning history, contingencies, mastery, and measurable behaviour change. The mechanism to keep in view is how antecedents, consequences, skills, avoidance, and environmental design select and maintain behaviour. The characteristic professional risk is using control without assent, reinforcing compliance rather than wellbeing, or ignoring the meaning and function of behaviour. Keep those three sentences available during case discussion. They prevent a common form of drift in which a practitioner uses familiar language from a theory while the actual session is being governed by urgency, institutional pressure, or the wish to make distress stop quickly.
### A twelve-part application map
The following map is not a rigid manual. It is a way of checking whether the work has a beginning, a middle, a reviewable middle-late phase, and an ending. Some young people need only selected elements; others require a much longer stabilisation period or referral before the central method is appropriate.
| Phase | Practitioner task | Young person's experience | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Contract | Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, choices, session rhythm, and complaint or advocacy routes. | “I know what this is and what may be shared.” | Consent/assent record and the young person's own summary. |
| 2. Map contexts | Gather home, school, peer, digital, health, cultural, and material contexts relevant to the referral. | “Adults are not treating one setting as my whole life.” | A context map with missing information identified. |
| 3. Identify patterns | Describe sequences, triggers, responses, consequences, strengths, and exceptions in observable language. | “The problem has a pattern; I am not simply the problem.” | Baseline notes, diaries, measures, or functional records. |
| 4. Share formulation | Offer a tentative explanation in plain language and invite correction from the young person and, where appropriate, family. | “This account fits, partly fits, or needs changing.” | A dated formulation with disagreements recorded. |
| 5. Agree targets | Select a small number of meaningful, feasible, and reviewable goals. | “These goals are mine as well as the service's.” | Goal ratings and functional outcomes. |
| 6. Build capacity | Teach, rehearse, model, or arrange the relational and environmental supports required before demanding change. | “I have a way to begin, not just an instruction.” | Observed practice, mastery, and confidence ratings. |
| 7. Apply the method | Use the chapter's core procedures in classrooms, homes, clinics, and other real environments where behaviour occurs, adapting dose and pace to development and state. | “The work is challenging but understandable and stoppable.” | Session records, fidelity notes, and client feedback. |
| 8. Transfer to real life | Plan practice in the settings where change must survive, with adults and peers given clear, limited roles. | “This is not only something I do in a counselling room.” | Home, school, or community evidence of generalisation. |
| 9. Review obstacles | Treat non-completion, rupture, or deterioration as information about fit, sequencing, context, or risk. | “A setback leads to inquiry, not blame.” | Revised formulation and documented changes. |
| 10. Involve systems | Use consultation, advocacy, family work, or care coordination where the maintaining conditions lie outside the individual session. | “Adults are changing some conditions too.” | Meeting actions, school adjustments, and referral outcomes. |
| 11. Consolidate | Name the strategies, relationships, and environmental changes that produced improvement. | “I can explain what helps and why.” | Relapse or setback plan in the young person's language. |
| 12. End or transfer | Review gains, unfinished needs, future help routes, and information transfer with consent. | “Ending is planned; I am not disappearing from care.” | Closing summary, measures, and follow-up arrangements. |

### Session-by-session guide
**Session one: make the work intelligible.** Begin with the young person's account before offering the theory. Ask what they have been told about the referral, what they expect, what would make sessions unbearable, and what a useful change would look like in an ordinary week. Explain confidentiality in concrete categories: what remains in counselling, what may be summarised for agreed support, and what may need to be shared for safety. End by checking the explanation back in the young person's words. A technically correct contract that the young person cannot repeat has not yet been understood.
**Session two: widen the lens without losing focus.** Map the problem across settings. A difficulty that appears only under public evaluation differs from one that follows the young person everywhere. Record sleep, health, learning, language, family routines, peer contact, digital pressures, and previous help. Ask what adults currently do that helps even slightly, and what they do with good intentions that accidentally maintains the pattern. This session should produce questions, not premature certainty.
**Session three: build a shared formulation.** Offer no more than three linked mechanisms at first. For example: a trigger, an interpretation or bodily response, a protective action, and a consequence that brings short-term relief but longer-term cost. Draw the sequence together. Ask the young person to rename any professional term that feels wrong. If parents or school staff will receive a version, agree its content and purpose. The formulation is a working map, not a verdict.
**Session four: choose the first experiment.** Select a step small enough to be attempted before the next meeting and important enough to matter. Define who will do what, where, for how long, and how the result will be noticed. Rehearse in session if the step is interpersonal. Predict obstacles without treating them as excuses. A young person who cannot attempt the step has provided information about grading, support, timing, or goal ownership.
**Sessions five and six: practise, review, and adjust.** Review actual attempts before introducing new material. What happened first, what followed, and what was learned? Strengthen successful components and revise those that depended on unrealistic adult consistency. If family or school responses undermined the plan, do not solve the problem by asking the young person to try harder inside unchanged conditions. Use consultation or a joint meeting with a narrow purpose.
**Sessions seven and eight: deepen without flooding.** By this stage the practitioner may work more directly with emotion, memory, belief, family sequence, behaviour, identity, or relationships, depending on the model. Deepening does not mean intensity for its own sake. Watch arousal, dissociation, shame, and post-session functioning. A session that produces dramatic disclosure followed by school absence, family conflict, or increased self-harm requires reformulation and possibly a slower phase.
**Sessions nine and ten: generalise and distribute support.** Move the work into ordinary life. Identify which teacher, parent, carer, mentor, coach, peer, or community adult can support the new pattern without becoming a therapist. Keep instructions brief and observable. The young person should know exactly what information those adults hold. Generalisation fails when every adult improvises a different version of the plan.
**Final sessions: consolidate authorship and plan for setbacks.** Review the original goals and the evidence of change. Ask the young person to explain, in their own words, what the problem does, what helps, what early signs matter, and what they will do first if it returns.Practise a likely setback rather than treating relapse as failure. Close with a summary that the young person has seen, a clear route back to help, and transfer of relevant supports to family, school, or community systems.
### Worked example for seminar discussion
Kofi is fifteen and is seen in a community adolescent clinic for school avoidance and rising panic. The referral describes the problem in institutional language and recommends that counselling should improve motivation. Kofi's own first statement is different: “Adults keep asking what is wrong with me, but nobody asks what happens just before it goes wrong.” That sentence becomes the assessment task.
The practitioner first establishes safety, consent, and context. Kofi explains that the difficulty is worst when tired, observed by peers, and uncertain about what an adult expects. At home, concern is expressed through repeated questioning; at school, public correction increases urgency. A previous professional focused only on the visible behaviour and recommended consequences. The new formulation instead asks how state, interpretation, relationships, and consequences interact, consistent with this chapter's focus on functional analysis, learning history, contingencies, mastery, and measurable behaviour change.
The first intervention is deliberately modest. Kofi chooses one setting in which to test a new response, one adult who will respond predictably, and one way of recording what happens. The practitioner rehearses the step and agrees what will count as useful information even if the attempt fails. Parents are not given session content; they are given a narrow role: reduce repeated prompting, notice the agreed attempt, and report practical obstacles at a planned review. School receives only the functional adjustment Kofi has approved.
At review, the result is mixed. The chosen step works twice and fails once after a night of poor sleep and a peer comment. A weak practitioner might call this inconsistency. The formulation instead becomes more precise: capacity is state-dependent, peer audience changes the task, and adult responses need to be steadier. The next step is redesigned rather than enlarged. This is the clinical discipline the laboratory is intended to teach: evidence from the young person's life revises the plan.
By the later sessions, Kofi can describe the pattern without adopting the referral label. Improvement is measured not only by symptom frequency but by participation, recovery time, willingness to seek help, and the adults' ability to respond without escalation. The ending plan names early signs, first responses, trusted people, and the point at which professional help should be sought again. The case remains fictional and simplified, but it demonstrates the movement from explanation to negotiated action.
### Family, school, and cultural translation
Translate the theory into the languages of the systems around the young person. Parents need to know what to do differently on Tuesday evening, not only what the model is called. Teachers need observable adjustments: timing, audience, instruction format, feedback privacy, break arrangements, or response to early signs. Community and health professionals need the specific decision requested from them. A plan that requires everyone to understand the whole theory will fail; a plan that assigns each person one coherent action may succeed.
Cultural translation is not achieved by inserting a stereotyped example. Ask how the young person and family explain the difficulty, who is trusted, what privacy means, which adult decisions are collective, what language carries the relevant concepts, and what previous experiences with services shape caution. Check whether the intervention asks the least powerful person to absorb all change. If the school timetable, housing, transport, discriminatory response, or family workload is maintaining distress, consultation and advocacy belong in the treatment plan.
Developmental translation requires attention to language, abstract reasoning, attention, memory, autonomy, and dependence. A method designed for adults may need shorter tasks, visual materials, movement, caregiver involvement, school coordination, and more explicit consent. Adaptation is not dilution when the mechanism is preserved and made usable; it is dilution when only the vocabulary is borrowed.
### Common errors and supervision questions
- Using the theory's label as an explanation: “She does that because she is resistant, avoidant, dysregulated, or unmotivated.” Ask what was observed and what else could explain it.
- Selecting an intervention because it is familiar to the practitioner rather than matched to mechanism, evidence, development, and consent.
- Involving family or school so broadly that the young person's confidential space disappears, or excluding them so completely that the environment defeats the work.
- Interpreting lack of progress as client failure before checking fidelity, dose, practical barriers, alliance, risk, and formulation accuracy.
- Moving faster because the service has few sessions, without making the compression explicit or arranging step-down support.
- Recording speculation as fact, especially about motives, trauma history, identity, family intent, or risk.
- What exactly did the young person say they wanted, and how does the plan preserve that aim?
- Which mechanism is the current intervention supposed to change, and what would count as evidence?
- What has been ruled out medically, educationally, culturally, and in safeguarding terms?
- Who else must change a response for the young person's new behaviour to survive?
- What is the practitioner feeling pulled to do—rescue, punish, withdraw, persuade, avoid—and how is that being supervised?
- If only three sessions remained, what should be consolidated rather than newly opened?
- What information does the young person believe is private, and does the record match that understanding?
### Outcome check
Review at least four kinds of outcome. First, **experience**: does the young person feel safer, understood, and more able to choose? Second, **function**: what is happening in learning, attendance, family life, friendship, sleep, and participation? Third, **mechanism**: has avoidance reduced, regulation improved, communication changed, mastery increased, family accommodation shifted, or support become more reliable? Fourth, **system**: are adults and institutions responding differently, and will those changes survive staff absence or term transitions? A symptom score may contribute to each judgement; it cannot replace them.
End the laboratory by writing a five-sentence plan: the young person's goal; the formulation in ordinary language; the next intervention and who will carry it; the main ethical or cultural caution; and the date and evidence for review. If those five sentences cannot be written clearly, the theory has not yet become practice.


### Advanced seminar exercises for Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment

**Compare two explanations.** Write two competing explanations of the same adolescent difficulty using **Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment** and one other approach from this book. Each explanation must identify a mechanism, an intervention, a predicted early sign of improvement, and an observation that would weaken the explanation. The exercise prevents theoretical loyalty from replacing reasoning: a model earns its place by the decisions it changes.

**Audit the first three sessions.** Design the first three contacts for a fictional adolescent. State what will be explained, what will not yet be attempted, which adults may be involved, and what record will be created. Pay particular attention to assent, privacy, developmental language, and the difference between the referrer's goal and the young person's goal. Many treatment failures are contracted in the first meeting long before a recognisable technique is used.

**Find the maintaining loop.** Draw a loop with at least five points: context, bodily state or appraisal, protective action, other people's response, and longer-term consequence. Mark where **Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment** would intervene and where a family, school, health, cultural, or practical intervention is more appropriate. Require every arrow to be supported by an observation or labelled as a hypothesis.

**Practise the difficult conversation.** In triads, rehearse explaining the approach to a sceptical adolescent, a worried parent, and a teacher who wants a quick behavioural result. The practitioner must use ordinary language, state limits, invite correction, and avoid promising outcomes. Observers record where jargon, coercion, or false certainty enters. Repeat the conversation after feedback; fluency under challenge is a professional skill.

**Examine power and access.** Ask who defined the problem, who attends, who can refuse, whose record is believed, who pays or travels, whose language is used, and who carries the burden of change. Consider disability, poverty, race, gender, sexuality, care experience, migration, and school status where relevant to the fictional case. Identify one institutional action that should accompany any individual intervention.

**Plan the review.** Choose four outcomes: the young person's experience, daily functioning, the mechanism targeted by **Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment**, and the response of the surrounding system. Specify when each will be reviewed and what decision a disappointing result would trigger—reformulate, adjust dose, involve others, consult, refer, pause, or end. This turns evaluation from a discharge ritual into clinical governance.

**Written consolidation.** In 250 words, explain Applied Behavior Analysis: Functional Assessment to a fourteen-year-old, a parent, and a teacher without using the theory's technical label. The three versions should preserve the same core idea while changing vocabulary, examples, and responsibilities. If the explanation only works when the listener accepts the professional's authority, it is not yet clinically usable.
