## 9. The Discipline of Theory: What Commitment Requires

Holding a theory is easy. Holding one responsibly is a discipline, and it has observable components that can be assessed in supervision and taught. The following are the minimum requirements of a theoretically grounded practice.

### 9.1 Articulation

The practitioner must be able to state, in ordinary language, the theory they are working from, in a form that a colleague or a supervisor could understand and, if necessary, disagree with. A useful test is to ask the practitioner to explain their theory to a client in two minutes without using technical vocabulary. Failure to do so indicates that the theory has been taken as a slogan rather than understood as a set of propositions.

### 9.2 Situated commitment

A theoretical position is always situated: held by a particular practitioner, in a particular setting, at a particular time, in relation to a particular population. The same practitioner may work cognitively with one adolescent and systemically with another, and may find that the appropriateness of this variation depends on factors they can articulate. Situated commitment is not the same as inconsistency. The inconsistency that matters is the failure to notice the change.

### 9.3 Awareness of the assumptions

Every theory rests on assumptions about human nature, about the relation between individual and environment, about the mutability of the person, and about the sources of authority from which the practitioner draws. The counselling profession has been badly served by practitioners who have not examined their own assumptions, particularly in relation to culture, gender, disability, and the presumed authority of the professional. Chapter 48 and Chapter 49 examine these assumptions in detail, and the cultural formulation interview reproduced in Appendix A provides a structured instrument for testing them against a particular client.

### 9.4 Awareness of the limits

A practitioner must know the conditions under which the theory does not apply. Every theory has a population for which it was developed, a population on which it was tested, and a population about which very little is known. In the current field, that problem is acute for most theories, because the overwhelming majority of outcome research has been conducted with adult, white, outpatient, non-suicidal samples. The transfer of any theory to a twelve-year-old boy with multiple disabilities, or to a seventeen-year-old refugee with a history of torture, requires an explicit act of extrapolation that ought to be acknowledged in the formulation rather than concealed.

### 9.5 A route to revision

The practitioner must be able to describe what evidence would cause a revision of the formulation, and how that evidence would be gathered. A formulation from which no possible observation could disconfirm it is not a formulation but a story. This is the practical link between theory and the measurement-based practice discussed in Chapter 53.

## 10. Writing a Personal Theoretical Position

The exercise below is the first of the assignments in this course and should be completed before the first seminar. It is deliberately short, and it is deliberately difficult, because the difficulty is in the writing rather than the reading.

### 10.1 The seven elements

A personal theoretical position is complete when it contains the following, in no more than 1,500 words.

1. **The theory or theories.** Name the tradition or traditions from which the position draws, and say explicitly whether it is a single theory, an integration, or a technical eclecticism.
2. **The model of the person.** State what you believe a person is, in one paragraph, in language without jargon. This is the assumption on which everything else rests, and it should be stated in terms you would be willing to defend to a client.
3. **The account of difficulty.** State how difficulties develop and, more importantly, how they are maintained. The maintenance account is the one that matters clinically, and a position that explains origin but not maintenance is not yet a clinical theory.
4. **The mechanism of change.** State what you believe actually causes improvement in counseling, and in what terms. A mechanism that cannot be observed cannot be evaluated.
5. **The implications for practice.** State three things you would do differently as a result of your position, and three things you would not do.
6. **The limits.** State the circumstances under which you would expect your position to be inadequate, and what you would do in those circumstances.
7. **The disconfirming evidence.** State what you would have to observe for you to revise the position, and where you would look for it.

### 10.2 Why the seventh element is the most important

Students typically write elements one to six fluently and then evade the seventh. The evasion is itself diagnostic. A practitioner who cannot name the evidence that would disconfirm his or her position has a position that is not a theory in the scientific sense but a commitment, and the professional obligations discussed in Section 5 cannot be discharged by a commitment.

### 10.3 A worked example of one element

To show what is required, consider element 3 for a student who has adopted a behavioural position. A weak statement is: "Behaviour is learned through reinforcement, so difficult behaviour comes from reinforcement." A stronger statement is: "I hold that a young person's behaviour that continues despite the discomfort it produces is usually being maintained by a consequence that the young person values more than the discomfort — often relief from a demand, sometimes attention, occasionally access to a tangible item. My assessment therefore asks, for each behaviour of concern, what happens immediately afterwards, and who is in a position to change it. Where the answer is that the consequence is delivered by an adult who does not know they are doing it, the intervention is a conversation with that adult before anything is done with the young person."

The second statement is longer, and its length is the point: it specifies the mechanism, it specifies the assessment question, it specifies the priority, and it names the parties whose involvement is required. It is falsifiable.

## 11. Case Illustration: Deliberating on a Ninth Grader's Refusal to Attend School

The following composite case is used throughout the course to illustrate the consequences of different theoretical positions. It appears here in full because the case is the instrument, not an illustration of it.

**The presenting problem.** Nadia is fourteen, in the ninth grade of a large suburban high school. She has been absent for eleven of the last fourteen school days. Her mother brought her to the counseling office on a Tuesday morning, having spent the previous evening, by her own account, "begging and then screaming." Nadia sat in the corridor for the whole appointment and spoke for a total of perhaps ninety seconds, in a low, flat voice, looking at her shoes. The referral, written by the school attendance officer, states: "Refusing school. Mother concerned about a decline since around March. Previous year no issues. Two calls from school to mother, no response."

**Additional information obtained with consent.** Nadia's mother, Denise, is a nurse who works rotating shifts. Nadia's father left the household when Nadia was eleven and has recently, in response to a message through Nadia's phone, begun making regular contact again, mostly on weekends. Nadia's grades in the first two terms of the year were good. In March she sustained a fractured wrist in a fall during physical education, was off school for three weeks, and returned to a PE class in which, in her own words later, "everyone was looking at me and I couldn't breathe." Her mother reports that since March "she won't go in her bedroom, she won't eat with us, she cries in the bathroom." Nadia has one close friend, Ruth, who has stopped replying to messages. There is a family history of anxiety in the maternal line, and an older sister, 19, who left home in the previous year.

**What is not known.** Whether Nadia is safe at home. Whether anything has happened in the PE class. Whether her father's renewed contact has been discussed with her mother. Whether Nadia has any medical or sensory explanation for the breathing difficulty. Whether Nadia is eating. Whether she is using any substance. Whether she has told anyone else what happened.

### 11.1 The question the case poses

The question is not "what is wrong with Nadia." It is: **what should the counsellor do first, and why?** The theoretical position determines the answer, and the answer determines what happens to a fourteen-year-old over the following weeks.

### 11.2 What a behavioural position would do first

The behavioural counsellor is interested in what happened immediately before each refusal and immediately after. There is a good chance that the absences are maintained: if Nadia's anxiety is relieved by remaining at home, and if the relief and the parental pleading are the immediate consequences, then the pattern may be self-perpetuating, whatever began it. The behavioural assessment would include a detailed functional analysis of one or two absences, the identification of the antecedents (the bus, the entrance, a particular corridor, a particular student) and the consequences, and an examination of what can be changed in the environment — because the person who controls the bus and the school gate and the timetable is not Nadia.

That analysis is indispensable and is not in dispute. What is in dispute is what it licenses. The behavioural position as stated in Chapter 23 would hold that changing the consequences will change the behaviour, and that the referral for a medical assessment of the breathing difficulty and for a safety check should occur in parallel, because a theory of behaviour does not relieve a practitioner of the duty to exclude an organic or safeguarding cause.

### 11.3 What a cognitive position would do first

The cognitive counsellor is interested in what Nadia thinks when she approaches the school gate, and in the beliefs that the March incident installed: that she will be seen as an impostor, that her body will betray her, that her friends have withdrawn because she has become strange. The cognitive position would propose to begin with a graded re-entry plan in which the first task is small and non-anxiety-provoking, paired with behavioural experiments that test the feared predictions, and with the explicit teaching that panic is a wave that rises and falls rather than a state to be defeated.

That is a coherent and evidence-supported plan. Its vulnerability is one that the cognitive position itself identifies: the young person must be able to participate in it, and Nadia, on the evidence available, is not yet in a position to do so. The cognitive formulation of the problem includes the young person's avoidance of the very activity required to test the cognitive hypotheses.

### 11.4 What a humanistic position would do first

The humanistic counsellor is interested in the quality of the contact and in what Nadia is experiencing. The first session, from this position, is about establishing a relationship in which Nadia's own account can be told without being immediately interpreted, corrected, or problematised. The humanistic position would be the least comfortable with the case because it offers least in the way of a plan, and it would be criticised for offering least.

It would also be the position most likely to notice that Nadia is in the corridor rather than in the room, and that the two minutes of speech in the corridor contained more information than the rest of the assessment. Whether the relationship can be built in a corridor, in two minutes, on a Tuesday morning, is a practical question that the humanistic position often underestimates.

### 11.5 What a systemic position would do first

The systemic counsellor is interested in the family system. The timing of Nadia's absence — beginning in March, three weeks after leaving school, in a household where a father has recently re-entered contact — invites the question of what the absence is doing for the family. It may be that Nadia's presence at home and her visible distress have provided a focus around which the parents' unresolved separation can be negotiated. It may equally be that the father's return has destabilised the household and that the school is where Nadia would have been safe.

The systemic position would want to see the parents separately before seeing them together, would want to know what has not been said between them about the father's return, and would want to understand who else in the family has an interest in Nadia remaining at home. It is also the position most likely to identify the school gate as a member of the system, and to ask what the attendance officer's referral has already communicated to the family.

### 11.6 What a trauma-informed position would do first

The trauma-informed counsellor is interested in what happened in the PE class in March, and would want to establish safety, predictability, and control before anything else, and would want to know what the school has done and not done in the intervening months. The position would also be attentive to the fact that a fourteen-year-old who stopped eating with her family and cries in the bathroom is displaying a substantial change in functioning, and would treat the possibility of ongoing threat — from a peer, from within the school, or at home — as requiring exclusion rather than assumption.

The weakness of the trauma-informed reading in this case is the danger of over-determination. Not every withdrawn adolescent has been traumatised, and a position that treats all withdrawal as trauma will produce unnecessary and potentially harmful treatment.

### 11.7 What the case teaches

The case teaches that the theoretical decision and the clinical decision are the same decision. The counsellor who chooses the behavioural reading will make a different phone call in the first hour than the counsellor who chooses the systemic reading. Both may be right; they will not both be pursued; and the consequences of the choice will play out in the life of a fourteen-year-old over the following weeks.

It also teaches that the first action of a competent counsellor in this case is not theoretical at all. It is to establish that Nadia is safe, to establish what supports exist, and to establish contact with someone who can act on her behalf while the theoretical work proceeds. The ethical floor is not a position within the field; it is the floor on which the field stands.

## 12. Evidence and Research Support

The claim that theory is necessary is supported by several converging lines of evidence, which should be stated carefully and without overstatement.

**The alliance-outcome relationship.** Across a large literature, the quality of the therapeutic alliance is one of the most consistent predictors of outcome across a wide range of therapies and populations, including young people. Meta-analytic reviews report moderate to strong associations. The relationship is often interpreted as evidence for common factors and against technique, but a more careful reading supports a different conclusion: the alliance is a necessary condition for the operation of technique, and its quality is itself influenced by the theoretical stance the counsellor holds and the coherence of the work that stance generates. A counsellor who is confused about what they are doing produces an incoherent relationship.

**The failure of pure technique.** The psychotherapy manuals project, in which well-trained clinicians delivered well-specified manualised treatments, produced outcomes no better than the outcomes produced by the community treatments against which they were compared, and it produced them at substantially greater cost. The interpretation that has held up best in subsequent work is that manuals are insufficient without the common factors, not that manuals are unnecessary.

**Differential effectiveness.** While most therapies produce broadly similar outcomes, differences have been demonstrated for specific conditions and populations. Behavioural and cognitive-behavioural treatments have more favourable outcomes than brief supportive treatments for anxiety disorders and for many conduct problems; family-based and multisystemic approaches outperform individual treatment for antisocial behaviour and for eating disorders; and trauma-focused treatments outperform nonspecific treatment for post-traumatic stress symptoms. These differences matter for theory, because they indicate that the mechanisms proposed by particular theories are operative in identifiable conditions.

**The literature on the limits of the comparison.** At the same time, the methodological quality of much of this literature is low by the standards of evidence-based medicine, and the strongest claim the evidence supports is that certain approaches, applied competently to well-defined problems in appropriately selected populations, produce better outcomes than alternatives that are less well matched to the problem. This is a claim about fit, not about a universal hierarchy of theories.

**Practice-based evidence.** Routine outcome monitoring in real services has repeatedly shown substantial variability in outcome between providers working in the same model with the same protocol, which is evidence for the importance of practitioner skill, relationship quality, and attention to the specific characteristics of the person. It also shows that outcome improves when practitioners receive systematic feedback, which is the strongest available argument for the integration of measurement into routine practice.

## 13. Strengths and Limitations of a Theory-Directed Practice

**Strengths.** A theory-directed practice is coherent, teachable, auditable, and generative. It supports supervision, because a supervisor and a supervisee who share a framework can examine a case rather than merely exchange impressions. It supports research, because a coherent model can be manualised and tested. It supports professional identity and public accountability. And it supports the practitioner in the moment of difficulty, because a framework reduces the number of decisions that must be made from scratch.

**Limitations.** Theory can narrow attention, and a practitioner who has been thoroughly trained in one tradition can fail to see phenomena that another tradition foregrounds. Theory can be misapplied in the name of expertise. Theory can ossify, and a student who learned Rogers in 1965 may be using a version of person-centred practice that Rogers himself would not recognise. Theory can be used to protect the practitioner from the anxiety of not knowing, by providing a certainty that the situation does not warrant. And theory in the hands of an unskilled practitioner can be an instrument of considerable harm: a confident application of a poorly suited model to a young person is worse than an uncertain, attentive, unstructured approach.

None of these limitations is an argument against theory. Each is an argument for the disciplined, provisional, self-correcting commitment described in Section 9.

## 14. Cultural and Ethical Considerations

Two cultural points require statement at the outset of a graduate course, because they condition everything that follows.

First, the theoretical traditions surveyed in this book were developed predominantly in the United States, predominantly within a Euro-American cultural frame, and predominantly with adult clients. The conceptual apparatus of individual pathology, the primacy of the individual as the unit of intervention, the emphasis on verbal self-expression, the reliance on insight, and the assumption that the therapeutic relationship is a dyad are all cultural products rather than cultural universals. Individualism, the nuclear family as the normative family structure, the culturally specific norms of emotional display, the prioritisation of future orientation over present obligation, and the high value placed on self-authorship are additional examples. Chapter 8 develops this argument systematically, and the student should read it before reading any of the theory chapters as though the theory were universal.

Second, the ethical codes of the profession require cultural responsiveness as a matter of obligation rather than of optional excellence, and they define it in terms of the practitioner's self-examination: the codes require the counsellor to examine their own assumptions and biases, to understand the cultural context of the client, and to guard against the imposition of the counsellor's own values. The examination is not a one-off training exercise. Chapter 48 provides a model of the self-examination in the form of an analysis of power, and Chapter 49 applies it to a population for whom the harm of misreading is most severe.

## 15. Key Terms

:::keyterms
- **Theory** — A systematic, coherent, and testable set of propositions about persons, psychological difficulty, and the processes of change; in counseling, the organising framework of professional practice.
- **Model** — A simplified visual representation of the central features of a theory, usable without accepting every proposition of the theory from which it derives.
- **Framework** — An organising structure for practice that draws on several theories and specifies their relationships.
- **Technique** — A specific, replicable procedure for doing something in the counseling room, ordinarily embedded in a theory.
- **Protocol** — A manualised sequence of procedures with specified timing, order, and fidelity requirements, designed for replication.
- **Formulation** — A theory-laden account of the processes that maintain a young person's difficulties, written so that it can be tested and revised.
- **Mechanism of change** — The process by which an intervention is supposed to produce improvement; the causal claim that distinguishes a treatment from a procedure.
- **Common factors** — The elements shared across all recognised therapies, principally the therapeutic alliance, empathy, goal agreement, and expectancy.
- **Technical eclecticism** — The selection of techniques from any available tradition on the basis of fit, without requiring a unified theoretical account of their operation.
- **Theoretical integration** — The use of more than one theory together, with an explicit account of their agreements, conflicts, and the resulting coherent mechanism of change.
- **Pluralism** — The professional acknowledgement that different theories suit different people and problems, with the choice made on the case rather than on allegiance.
- **Dogmatism** — The treatment of a theory as a revelation rather than a hypothesis, with the consequent inability to hear disconfirming evidence.
- **Fidelity** — The degree to which an intervention is delivered as specified in the theory or protocol from which it derives.
- **Committed openness** — The professional stance of holding a theory strongly enough to act and weakly enough to be disconfirmed.
- **Telling** — The technique of commenting on the therapeutic process in order to elicit information from it.
- **Present-centred therapy** — A stance that attends to and works with the immediate moment of the counseling session rather than with historical material.
- **Common factors model** — The account of therapy that locates the primary determinants of outcome in the nonspecific elements shared by all recognised therapies.
- **Treatment manual** — A document specifying the rationale, sequence, timing, and fidelity criteria of a treatment.
- **Case conceptualisation** — See *formulation*; the two terms are used interchangeably in the contemporary literature, with the British spelling increasingly preferred in the chapters of this book.
:::

## 16. Chapter Summary

Theory in counseling is a systematic and testable account of persons, difficulty, and change. It performs four functions that no other professional resource can perform: it explains, it directs attention and sequences, it predicts, and it supplies the criteria by which the work is evaluated. These functions are converted into practice through a clinical loop in which observation leads to formulation, formulation to prediction, prediction to intervention, and intervention to evaluation, with revision at every step.

Theory is also ethically necessary. The ethics of informed consent, competence, the avoidance of harm, justice, and public accountability all presuppose a practitioner who can state what he or she believes is happening and why, and can show the basis of that belief. The absence of an articulated theoretical position is not freedom from constraint; it is constraint without examination.

The two failure modes of theoretical engagement are dogmatism, which forecloses correction, and nihilism, which mistakes the importance of the relationship for the dispensability of method. The defensible position is committed openness: a strong theoretical commitment held provisionally, with named conditions for revision.

The theory chosen has consequences. In the case of Nadia, a fourteen-year-old who has withdrawn from school, the behavioural, cognitive, humanistic, systemic, and trauma-informed readings each propose a different first action, and each first action changes the trajectory of the following weeks. That is the measure of theory's importance, and it is why the subject is worth a full course rather than a week of orientation.

## 17. Review and Discussion Questions

:::review
1. Define a theory of counseling in your own words, and state what the definition excludes. What would be lost if the phrase "theory" were used to refer to any belief about human behaviour?
2. A colleague tells you that she has been trained in one theory and sees no need to look at the others because "the research shows the common factors are what matter." Identify at least three errors in her position.
3. Explain the difference between a technique and a mechanism of change, and give an example of a technique that has been used outside the theory that generated it. What is the risk of that transfer?
4. Using the Nadia case, specify what a behavioural and what a systemic practitioner would each do in the first hour, and state what evidence would distinguish which of them was correct.
5. Why is it legitimate to say that a formulation should be written so that it can fail? What goes wrong, in the case of a young person, when a formulation cannot be disconfirmed?
6. Distinguish technical eclecticism from theoretical integration, and give an example of a practice that is clearly one and clearly the other.
7. The chapter argues that the absence of a theoretical position is an ethical problem. Make the case against that argument as strongly as you can, and then state your own conclusion.
8. What would you include in the element of a personal theoretical position that you find hardest to write, and why?
9. Identify one widely used intervention in counseling with young people for which the evidence base is weaker than its popularity would suggest. How would a theory-committed practitioner respond to that finding?
10. How does the requirement that a theory be culturally responsive change the definition of theory given in Section 1?
:::

## 18. Practice Activities

:::practice
### Activity 1.1 — The Five-Column Theory Notebook (2 hours, individual, then paired)

Draw five columns on a page: *assumptions about human nature*; *how difficulty develops*; *how difficulty is maintained*; *mechanism of change*; *techniques that follow*. For each of the following, complete the row: cognitive theory, behavioural theory, person-centered theory, family systems theory, and trauma-informed practice. Do not consult the chapters of this book; use your prior knowledge and mark the cells you are unsure of. Then compare with a partner, marking in a second colour the cells where you disagreed. Keep this notebook: it is the primary revision instrument for the course.

### Activity 1.2 — The Same Case, Five Theories (90 minutes, groups of four to five)

Assign each group a theoretical position from the Nadia case. Each group has forty minutes to prepare a five-minute briefing answering: what is happening with Nadia, what should happen in the next seventy-two hours, and what in your formulation could be wrong. In the second half hour, the groups hear each other's briefings without interruption and then identify the single point on which the competing positions most sharply disagree. Record that point; it is the raw material of the integrative argument in Chapter 51.

### Activity 1.3 — Writing Your Own Theoretical Position (3 hours, individual, submitted)

Complete the seven elements of Section 10 in no more than 1,500 words. Expect to find elements 3, 6, and 7 substantially harder than the others. In particular, do not submit a position whose element 7 you cannot write; the seminar will ask.

### Activity 1.4 — The Disconfirming Evidence Exercise (60 minutes, pairs)

Take a position that you personally hold about adolescent behaviour — about what causes school refusal, about what makes therapy work, about what a child needs from a parent. Write, in one paragraph, the observation that would most damage your position, and identify where you would look for it. Then discuss with a partner whether you would actually act on the finding if you encountered it. This is a small exercise about intellectual honesty, and it is deliberately uncomfortable.
:::

## 19. Annotated Reading Pathways

This chapter's central claim — that theory is necessary, that it must be held provisionally, and that the commitment must be articulable — is best pursued through a small number of well-chosen works rather than through breadth. The following four pathways are suggested.

### Pathway A: The nature of theory in counseling

Begin with Tatum (2017) for a clear and compressed comparison of the major orientations, which will give you a map to hold in mind while reading anything else. Read Pope and Vasquez (2021) selectively: the opening chapters on the professional context and the closing chapters on integration are worth more than the middle. Then read the two chapters in this book that you find most difficult, and ask why you find them difficult.

### Pathway B: Common factors, the alliance, and the limits of technique

Frank (2015) remains the most thorough scholarly treatment of the common factors question and of the historical development of the therapeutic relationship; it is long, and the chapters on the placebo literature and on the treatment of the relationship as a necessary condition are the ones to prioritise. Read Wampold (2015) immediately afterwards, as a direct, short, and somewhat argumentative counterweight, and then read the chapter on the therapeutic relationship in Part I of this book. Finish by reading the meta-analytic reviews of the alliance-outcome relationship in adolescents that your library holds; the adolescent literature is thinner than the adult literature and is worth seeking out.

### Pathway B continued: Practice-based evidence

For the argument that outcomes depend on practitioner skill and on attention to the individual, read the primary practice-based research literature rather than the summaries. This strand of the literature grows more persuasive the more of it one reads, and it is the empirical foundation for the emphasis in this book on formulation, on measurement, and on the deliberate development of relational skill.

### Pathway C: Culture and theory

Read Chapter 8 in this book as an introduction, and then read the chapters on culture in the standard survey texts alongside it. Where the survey texts and this book disagree, read the primary sources cited in the notes. Pay particular attention to the material on the cultural embeddedness of the concept of the individual and on the limits of the dyadic therapy model.

### Pathway D: The integrative stance

Read Chapter 51 of this book, then read the integrative chapters of the standard survey texts, and then read a small number of integrative case formulations written by practitioners who show their reasoning. The most useful single exercise is to take one case, write a formulation from three different theoretical positions, and identify what each position sees that the others miss. That exercise is the reason the course is worth the effort, and Chapter 52 sets it up in detail.
