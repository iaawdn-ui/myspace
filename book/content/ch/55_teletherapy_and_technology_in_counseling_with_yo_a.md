# Teletherapy and Technology in Counseling with Youth

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the student should be able to:

- Compare video, telephone, text-based, asynchronous, app-supported, and blended counselling.
- Assess suitability and risk for remote work with children and adolescents.
- Establish privacy, identity, location, emergency, and safeguarding procedures.
- Adapt alliance building, play, family work, and outcome monitoring to digital settings.
- Evaluate apps, platforms, digital records, and artificial intelligence claims.
- Address digital equity, accessibility, online exploitation, and technology-related presenting problems.

## Chapter Overview

Technology is no longer an add-on to adolescent life or counselling. Young people form friendships, learn, play, seek help, experience bullying, explore identity, and manage distress through digital environments. Teletherapy can increase access for rural families, disabled young people, those with transport barriers, and students who find remote contact less intimidating. It can also create privacy risks, weaken observation, complicate safeguarding, and exclude those without devices, connectivity, or a safe room.

## 1. Modalities

**Synchronous video** most closely resembles in-person work and supports observation, family sessions, and visual materials.

**Telephone** may feel safer for some adolescents and avoids appearance concerns, but removes visual cues and can make silence harder to interpret.

**Live text or chat** can help young people who struggle to speak, but slows affect recognition and creates records requiring careful governance.

**Asynchronous messaging** permits reflection between contacts but risks boundary confusion, delayed crisis response, and expectations of continuous availability.

**Apps and digital tools** may support mood tracking, skills practice, exposure hierarchies, sleep routines, and outcome measures. They should supplement—not silently replace—clinical judgement.

**Blended care** combines remote and in-person sessions according to purpose: assessment or family work in person, brief reviews remotely, school consultation by secure video, and digital homework between sessions.

## 2. Evidence and Suitability

Research generally supports teletherapy effectiveness for several mental health conditions, including many anxiety and depression interventions, with outcomes often comparable to in-person treatment when appropriately delivered. Youth evidence is growing but varies by age, condition, platform, and degree of caregiver involvement.

Suitability is individual. Consider developmental level, attention, language, neurodivergence, sensory needs, risk, home privacy, technology access, family conflict, and the nature of interventions. Remote work may be unsuitable where there is acute risk without local support, severe dissociation, unsafe home monitoring, complex safeguarding investigation, or need for physical observation and intervention. Unsuitability is not permanent; reassess as circumstances change.

## 3. Before the First Remote Session

A technology-specific assessment and agreement should cover:

- identity verification and who else may be present;
- the young person's physical location at each session;
- privacy and interruptions;
- emergency contact and local crisis resources;
- a backup plan if connection fails;
- recording prohibition or consent;
- chat, email, and messaging boundaries;
- parent or carer involvement;
- school or service network security;
- what happens if abuse or risk is observed or disclosed.

The practitioner should also test camera, audio, accessibility features, captions, screen sharing, and data storage. Professional backgrounds and attire matter less than audibility, lighting, confidentiality, and freedom from household interruptions on the practitioner's side.

## 4. Privacy in Real Homes

A bedroom is not automatically private. Siblings may listen, parents may monitor, doors may lack locks, and devices may be shared. Ask what the young person can safely say and what should be avoided. Agree signals for “I can't speak freely”, options to switch to chat, or rescheduling without giving a reason that creates danger.

Headphones can increase privacy but reduce awareness of surroundings. The practitioner should periodically check who is nearby, especially before family conflict, sexuality, identity, abuse, or self-harm discussions.

School-based teletherapy requires a private room, not a corridor or shared office. Staff must know a session is occurring without receiving content.

## 5. Alliance and Presence Online

Digital presence requires explicit warmth. The practitioner names delays, looks toward camera periodically, summarises more often, checks understanding, and tolerates silence without filling it anxiously. Young people may prefer camera off at first; explore reasons rather than impose visibility unless assessment or safeguarding requires otherwise.

Creative adaptations include shared whiteboards, drawing applications, digital scaling, screen-shared worksheets with consent, music or images chosen by the young person, and movement breaks. The practitioner should not record, screenshot, or save shared material without agreement and a record-keeping rationale.
