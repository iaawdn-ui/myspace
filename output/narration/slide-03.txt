This slide gives us the Programme Architecture at a Glance, the complete curriculum map on a single page. If this lecture is a guided tour, then this slide is the map of the whole city before we walk its streets. Take time to study its shape, because its logic will guide your planning for the next three years.

The first principle of the architecture is sequencing. The programme runs from Year One to Year Three, from foundations, through culture and sectors, to depth and research. Nothing is placed randomly. In Year One you will study courses numbered nine zero one to nine zero eight, the scientific and managerial foundations: OHS Management Systems, Occupational Epidemiology, Occupational Toxicology, Occupational Hygiene and Exposure Assessment, Risk Assessment and Management, OHS Laws and Regulations, Auditing and Inspection, and Training and Education. These eight courses answer the most fundamental questions of the discipline. How are management systems built? How do we measure disease and injury in populations? How do toxic agents harm the body? How do we measure exposure? How do we assess risk? What does the law require? How do we verify performance? And how do we teach others to work safely?

Year Two, courses nine zero nine to nine one five, turns to Culture and Sectors. It opens with OHS Culture and Behaviour, and OHS Programme Evaluation and Improvement, and then enters the industries: Construction, Manufacturing, Healthcare, and Mining, with Oil and Gas completing this sector group on the following slide. The logic here is powerful. First you learn how human values, leadership and group norms shape safety, and how to judge whether a safety programme is actually working. Only then do you enter specific industries, so that when you arrive in a construction site, a factory, a hospital or a mine, you see not only the machines and hazards, but also the culture and the management system surrounding them.

Year Three, courses nine one six to nine two one, provides Depth and Research. Four further industries complete the panorama: Transportation, Agriculture, Chemical, and Nuclear. Then comes Emergency Response and Disaster Management, and finally Advanced Research Methodology, which serves as the gateway to your dissertation. Notice how research methodology is deliberately placed after you have accumulated disciplinary breadth. By that point you will know the field well enough to identify a genuine gap worth investigating, rather than choosing a topic in the dark.

On the right-hand side of the map you see two elements that complete the architecture. The first is the Elective Strand, courses nine two two to nine three one: ten specialised electives across ergonomics, psychosocial risk, physical agents, biological, chemical and environmental themes. The elective strand runs like a customisable layer across your degree. It is where a class of candidates, all following the same core road, can branch into different scholarly territories. One candidate may emerge as a specialist in ergonomics and human factors. Another in radiation protection. Another in environmental sustainability. The core gives you a common professional language. The electives give you a distinctive voice.

The second element is the Dissertation, OHSE nine eight zero: original supervised research defended before an academic panel. On the map it looks like a single box, but in reality it is the destination toward which every other box points. Every literature search you practise, every exposure you learn to measure, every law you learn to interpret, and every evaluation design you learn to build is preparation for producing and defending your own thesis.

There is also an important lesson about workload and credit. Thirty-one courses at three credit hours each means a substantial cumulative credit load, and you should respect it from the first week. Doctoral success is rarely destroyed by one dramatic failure; it is far more often eroded by poor pacing and late starts. Use this map to plan ahead. Look forward to the sectors that match your experience. Preview the electives early. And begin keeping a research ideas file from today. By the time you reach Advanced Research Methodology in Year Three, that humble file may contain the seed of your dissertation.

In short, remember the shape: Year One builds the scientist, Year Two builds the sector specialist, Year Three builds the researcher, the electives build your signature, and the dissertation proves the scholar. That is the architecture. Now let us walk through it, year by year.

 Let me suggest a practical exercise using this map. Draw three columns headed knowledge, skill, and research identity. For each year, write what new knowledge you expect, what practical skill you should be able to demonstrate, and how your identity as a researcher should change. At the end of Year One, you should be able to assess a workplace systematically. At the end of Year Two, you should compare sectors and evaluate programmes. During Year Three, you should frame an original question and defend a design for answering it. Revisit this exercise at the start of every term. It converts the curriculum map from a poster into a personal development plan.
