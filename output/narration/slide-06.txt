We now enter the Second Year, introduced on this slide under the theme Culture, Performance, and Industry. The second year opens with the human and organisational dimensions of safety, and then applies them to the first industrial sectors of the programme. This order is educationally deliberate. Year One taught you what hazards are and how systems control them. Year Two begins by asking why intelligent people, working inside documented systems, still sometimes take risks, ignore procedures, or remain silent when something looks unsafe. Until you understand people and organisations, technical knowledge alone will not keep anyone safe.

The year opens with OHSE nine zero nine, Occupational Health and Safety Culture and Behaviour. This course examines how shared values, leadership, and group norms shape safety outcomes, drawing on behavioural science. Safety culture is often described as the way we do things around here, especially when nobody senior is watching. Does a supervisor thank a worker who stops a job, or criticise the delay? Do new workers learn the official procedure, or the shortcut everyone really uses? Do people believe incident reports lead to improvement, or to punishment? You will study safety climate surveys, maturity models that move organisations from reactive blame through calculative compliance towards proactive and generative learning, behavioural safety observation, habit formation, risk perception, and the difference between work as imagined in head office and work as actually done on the front line. You will also study just culture: the difficult balance between accountability for reckless behaviour and psychological safety, so that honest errors and near misses are reported and learned from. A doctoral scholar in this area learns to treat culture not as a slogan on a poster, but as measurable patterns of behaviour that leadership creates, day after day.

Alongside it, OHSE nine one zero covers OHS Programme Evaluation and Improvement: evaluating the effectiveness of safety interventions with indicators, metrics, and evaluation designs. This course asks a question every board should ask and few can answer well: is our safety programme actually working? Counting injuries after they happen is necessary, but it is not evaluation. You will distinguish leading indicators, such as inspection completion, near-miss reporting quality, training competence verification and corrective-action closure, from lagging indicators such as injury rates and lost time. You will study logic models that connect inputs, activities, outputs and outcomes, comparison designs that help separate programme effects from luck or economic cycles, and the common traps of perverse incentives, under-reporting, and celebrating a zero-harm month that simply reflects silence. Continual improvement depends on honest evaluation. Without it, organisations repeat activities that feel effective while harm continues underneath.

With those human and evaluative lenses in place, the programme enters its first sector: OHSE nine one one, OHS in the Construction Industry. This course addresses falls from height, struck-by events, structural collapse, and rigorous site control in construction operations. Construction is one of the most hazardous industries on earth because the workplace itself changes daily. Edges move. Openings appear. Cranes lift loads over people. Temporary works hold real structures until permanent strength is achieved. Multiple contractors and languages share one site. You will study work at height planning, scaffolding, excavation support, lifting operations, site traffic management, temporary electrical safety, design-stage risk reduction, permit systems, and the coordination duties of clients, designers and contractors. International evidence shows that a small number of fatal mechanisms, sometimes called the fatal four or focus four, account for a large share of construction deaths. Your scholarly challenge is to understand why an industry that knows its fatal risks so well still struggles to eliminate them, and what organisational, economic and regulatory conditions would finally change that.

The sector sequence continues with OHSE nine one two, OHS in the Manufacturing Industry, focusing on machinery guarding, production-line ergonomics, and process reliability in manufacturing environments. Manufacturing harm is often repetitive, patterned and therefore preventable: amputations at unguarded presses, musculoskeletal disorders from forceful and repetitive assembly, hearing loss around noisy plant, dermatitis and respiratory effects from process chemicals, and injuries during maintenance when stored energy is not isolated. You will study machine risk assessment, fixed and interlocked guards, presence-sensing devices, lockout and tagout, safe systems for intervention and cleaning, line-speed and workload design, and the integration of safety into lean and quality systems rather than treating it as a competitor to productivity. Manufacturing also introduces process reliability: the insight that well-maintained, stable, well-instrumented processes are usually both safer and more productive.

Step back and see the pattern of Year Two. Culture explains behaviour. Evaluation tests performance. Construction and manufacturing then become living laboratories in which those ideas can be observed. When you study each new industry from now on, develop a doctoral habit: always ask four questions. What are the signature hazards? Who is most exposed and most vulnerable? What controls does good practice demand? And what is still unknown that research could answer? That habit will serve you in every remaining sector, and eventually in your dissertation.

 There is a warning hidden inside the study of culture and behaviour. Organisations sometimes use behavioural language to return responsibility to the worker while avoiding investment in safer design, staffing, maintenance or planning. Critical doctoral scholars should resist that misuse. Behaviour always occurs inside conditions that leadership creates. Observe behaviour, yes, but also observe workload, incentives, equipment, competence, supervision and the distance between procedures and real work. A mature safety culture does not ask, who failed, as its first question. It asks what made that action make sense at the time, and what in the system should change. That question produces learning instead of fear.
