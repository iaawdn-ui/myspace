We now focus entirely on the Capstone, OHSE nine eight zero: The Dissertation Journey. This slide shows a supervised pathway from first idea to public defence, run in parallel with third-year coursework. Its final line states the purpose of the journey: from candidate to contributor, research that changes practice. Print those words in your mind. A dissertation is not a long essay produced to satisfy a requirement. It is the moment you stop being mainly a consumer of other people's knowledge and become a contributor whose work others may cite, use, challenge and build upon. The six steps on this slide will organise several years of effort, so let us walk through each one slowly and honestly.

Step one is Topic and Proposal: identify a genuine gap in OHSE knowledge and frame the research question. This stage sounds simple and is often the hardest. A good topic sits at the intersection of four circles. It must matter in the real world. It must be incompletely understood in the scholarly literature. It must be researchable with data you can ethically and practically obtain. And it must sustain your interest through difficulties. During proposal development you will move from a broad interest, such as construction safety or hospital infection control, to a precise question: which population, which exposure or intervention, which outcome, which setting, and which comparison or experience are you studying? You will define key concepts, justify significance, draft aims, objectives and research questions or hypotheses, sketch your methods, consider feasibility and timeline, and present all of this in a proposal that your school can approve. Learn from this stage that a narrow question answered rigorously is far more valuable than a grand question answered vaguely. Your supervisors will push you to narrow. That pressure is a gift.

Step two is Ethics Review: approval for research involving human participants and workplace data. Ethics is not paperwork that delays research. Ethics is the researcher keeping faith with the people who trust them. Workplace research creates special obligations because employees may feel unable to refuse a study supported by their employer, junior staff may fear identification, and accident, health and exposure records can be sensitive and legally significant. Your ethics application will explain purpose, recruitment, voluntary informed consent, the right to withdraw, confidentiality and anonymisation, data storage and retention, risks and benefits, arrangements for referring participants who disclose serious risk or distress, and how results will be reported so that small teams cannot be identified. Research involving patients, children, migrant workers, or people who have experienced injury, bereavement or trauma demands still greater care. You will also consider the ethics of findings: what will you do if your fieldwork uncovers an imminent danger to workers? A doctoral researcher plans for that duty before entering the field.

Step three is the Literature Review: situate the study within the international scholarly evidence base. A doctoral literature review is not a summary of everything you have read. It is a critical map that shows what is known, what is contested, what methods have been used, whose voices and settings are missing, and precisely where your study enters the conversation. You will search systematically across databases and grey literature, manage references accurately, appraise study quality rather than treating every publication as equal, compare findings across countries and industries, and identify whether your gap is empirical, methodological, theoretical or contextual. Perhaps an intervention worked in European manufacturing but has never been tested in West African construction. Perhaps exposure has been measured but workers' experiences have never been heard. Perhaps safety climate has been surveyed repeatedly but never linked to verified exposure or health outcomes. The review must end with your study becoming almost inevitable: because this remains unknown, the research question follows. Expect to revise this chapter until submission. New literature appears, and your own findings will change what earlier studies mean to you.

Step four is Data Collection: field, laboratory or organisational data gathered under supervision. This is where scholarship leaves the library and meets reality, with all its richness and resistance. Organisations delay access. Machines break down. Participants withdraw. Shift patterns change. Rain closes the construction site. Sensors need calibration. Interviews reveal questions you should have asked from the beginning. Doctoral resilience is built here. Depending on your design, you may administer validated questionnaires, conduct interviews or focus groups, observe work, measure dust, noise, heat, ventilation or biological markers, audit records, run an intervention, or combine several sources so that weaknesses in one are balanced by strengths in another. Keep meticulous records. Preserve raw data securely. Record decisions and deviations in a research journal. Treat every participant's time as a gift. Supervision meetings during data collection are not progress theatre; bring problems early, because small design threats caught in month one are correctable, while the same threats discovered after fieldwork may be fatal to a thesis.

Step five is Analysis and Thesis: rigorous analysis using methods mastered in OHSE nine two one, drafted as a thesis. Analysis is where data become evidence, and evidence must be handled without wishful thinking. Quantitative candidates will clean and check data, describe samples honestly, test assumptions, select statistics that match the questions and measurement levels, report effect sizes and uncertainty, and avoid claiming causation beyond the design. Qualitative candidates will transcribe accurately, code systematically, search for disconfirming cases, test alternative interpretations, and show a transparent path from raw words to themes. Mixed methods candidates must genuinely integrate findings, explaining where they converge, complement or contradict each other. Then comes the writing of the thesis itself: introduction, literature review, methodology, results, discussion, conclusions and recommendations, each chapter earning its place. In discussion, you will place findings back into international literature, explain possible mechanisms, acknowledge limitations without undermining your own work, and draw implications for practice, policy and future research. Writing is not what happens after thinking. Writing is thinking, at doctoral depth. Give it protected time, accept critical feedback without taking it personally, and remember that examiners judge the thesis you submit, not the brilliant study you intended.

Step six is the Defence: public examination and defence before an academic panel. The defence, often called the viva, is a scholarly conversation in which you demonstrate ownership. Examiners may ask why you chose the topic, why those methods, why that sample, what else could explain your findings, what is original, what you would do differently, and how the work should change practice. Prepare by rereading your thesis critically, rehearsing a concise opening summary, anticipating hard questions, and practising honest answers, including the words, that is a limitation, and here is how I addressed it. A good defence is not a battle to hide weakness. It is evidence that you understand your study more deeply than anyone else in the room. When corrections are required, receive them professionally; they are the final quality gate of the doctorate.

The slide also makes a timing point that demands planning: this pathway runs in parallel with third-year coursework. You cannot wait until every taught course is perfectly finished before thinking about research. Use Year One to notice problems, Year Two to read deeply around sectors and electives, early Year Three to finalise topic and proposal, and OHSE nine two one to sharpen method and ethics. Keep a research journal, maintain a reference library from the beginning, back up your work in more than one secure place, and meet supervision deadlines even when drafts feel imperfect. A submitted imperfect draft can be improved. An imaginary perfect dissertation that exists only in your head cannot be examined. Move forward step by step, keep faith with participants, keep faith with evidence, and this six-step pathway will carry you from candidate to contributor.
