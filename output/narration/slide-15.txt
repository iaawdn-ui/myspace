And beneath everything, the words that can govern your professional life: protecting people, promoting health, preserving the environment. Let us use this conclusion not merely to end the lecture, but to convert information into decisions, habits and commitments that you can act on from today.

First, let us travel the complete map one more time, because repetition with understanding is how a map becomes memory. In Year One, the scientific core, OHSE nine zero one introduced management systems and corporate governance. Nine zero two taught epidemiology, how disease and injury are distributed and explained in working populations. Nine zero three taught toxicology, how agents harm through dose, route and duration. Nine zero four taught occupational hygiene and exposure assessment, how hazards are recognised, measured and controlled. Nine zero five developed risk assessment and management. Nine zero six placed protection within laws and regulations. Nine zero seven taught auditing and inspection, the verification of conformity and performance. And nine zero eight taught training and education, because knowledge must reach behaviour. If Year One has a single sentence, it is this: learn to see hazards scientifically, judge them systematically, and organise their control.

Year Two then added people and sectors. OHSE nine zero nine examined culture and behaviour. Nine one zero taught programme evaluation and improvement. Then the industry journey accelerated through construction in nine one one, manufacturing in nine one two, healthcare in nine one three, mining in nine one four, and oil and gas in nine one five. If Year Two has a single sentence, it is this: systems succeed or fail through culture, sectors differ in their hazards but not in the discipline required to control them, and leaders must prove with evidence that programmes work.

Year Three deepened mastery and turned decisively toward research. Transportation came in nine one six, agriculture in nine one seven, chemical industry in nine one eight, and nuclear industry in nine one nine, completing twelve sectors from the farm to the reactor. OHSE nine two zero prepared you for emergency response and disaster management when prevention fails. OHSE nine two one, Advanced Research Methodology, opened the gateway to independent scholarship. OHSE nine eight zero, the dissertation, then asked you to create and defend original knowledge. If Year Three has a single sentence, it is this: having learned from many sectors and many scholars, now add one rigorous study of your own.

The elective strand then personalises the degree. Cluster One, the human workplace, offers ergonomics and human factors, psychosocial factors, noise and vibration, and heat and cold stress. Cluster Two, invisible agents and measured risk, offers radiation protection, biological hazards, and indoor air quality. Cluster Three, from the workplace to the world, offers hazardous material management, waste management, and environmental sustainability. And the career horizon that follows graduation reaches into universities, corporate EHS leadership, government and regulation, consultancy, international agencies and research institutes. Hold that whole architecture in view whenever a single assignment feels disconnected. No course is an island. Each one is preparing a capability your dissertation, your employers and, most importantly, the workers and communities you serve will one day require.

Now consider the first next step on this slide: review the handbook. That sounds administrative, but scholars treat governing documents seriously. Read the programme handbook for admission and progression rules, course prerequisites, assessment and grading, credit requirements, elective selection procedures, research milestones, supervision arrangements, ethics and academic integrity, plagiarism and the use of artificial intelligence, examination and defence procedures, appeals, timelines and the offices responsible for each decision. Highlight every deadline. Enter milestones into a calendar that looks three years ahead, not three weeks ahead. Distinguish what you control directly, what requires supervisor approval, and what requires school approval. Many doctoral difficulties are not intellectual at all. They are capable students discovering a rule, a prerequisite or a deadline too late. A professional reads the handbook before needing mercy from it.

The second next step is to choose your elective cluster. Begin now, even if formal selection comes later. Build a simple decision matrix. In one column list each elective. Then score, honestly and with evidence, your scholarly curiosity, your prior experience, the needs of your sector and country, access to field sites and data, likely supervisors and literature depth, and the career pathway you most want to strengthen. Talk to lecturers and advanced candidates. Read one recent journal article from each elective you are seriously considering and notice which article makes you ask the most questions. That response is data about yourself. Avoid choosing only what sounds prestigious or what friends choose. Doctoral study is too long for borrowed curiosity. Also consider coherence: can you explain in two sentences how your electives support a future research identity? If you can, you have a specialisation. If you cannot yet, keep exploring, but keep moving toward an answer.

 The third next step is to prepare a statement of research interest. This is not yet your full dissertation proposal, and it should not pretend to be. It is a focused scholarly introduction to the problem that is beginning to claim your attention. A strong statement names the workplace problem and who suffers from it, summarises what existing evidence shows and what remains uncertain, proposes a preliminary research question, suggests the setting and methods that might answer it, explains why the answer matters for workers, organisations, policy or theory, and connects the interest to your experience and elective direction. Write it in clear language, support it with recent literature, and expect it to change. A research interest is supposed to mature through reading, coursework and supervision. What matters at this stage is intellectual honesty and direction, not false certainty. Bring this statement to supervisor discussions as an invitation to dialogue. A good supervisor will test its importance, narrow its scope, challenge its methods and help you discover whether a viable doctorate lives inside it.

 The final panel on this slide speaks about your community: supervisor allocation and induction confirm your place among OHSE scholars. Pause over the word community, because doctoral study is often misrepresented as a solitary contest. It is demanding and it requires independence, but nobody completes a doctorate entirely alone. Your supervisor will challenge and guide you. Lecturers will open literatures and methods. Fellow candidates will share the strange experience of caring deeply about questions that people outside the programme may not initially understand. Librarians, methodologists, ethics reviewers, field-site gatekeepers, participants and examiners will all shape the work. Treat this community as a scholarly responsibility as well as a support. Attend induction fully. Learn the communication channels and meeting rhythms. Respond professionally. Prepare for supervision meetings with written updates and specific questions. Keep records of agreed actions. Seek help early, whether the issue is statistical, personal, financial, ethical or simply confusion. Independence at doctoral level does not mean silence. It means taking responsibility for mobilising the right help at the right time.

 Let me also leave you with practical habits that separate candidates who finish strongly from those who merely begin enthusiastically. Read actively, with a question, and keep an annotated reference library from the first month rather than reconstructing one in panic before submission. Write little and often, because dissertations are completed by scholars who write paragraphs repeatedly, not by those who wait for a mythical free month. Maintain a research journal for ideas, field decisions, supervisor advice and reflections. Back up everything, in more than one secure location, with clear file names and dates. Protect your health with the same seriousness you will demand for other workers: sleep, movement, medical care, boundaries and relationships are not distractions from scholarship; they are part of its safety management system. Finally, practise the central professional ethic of this field inside your own studies. Anticipate problems, recognise them early, evaluate honestly, and control them before they become crises.

 So we return to the motto: protecting people, promoting health, preserving the environment. Protecting people is the immediate duty, that no worker, patient, visitor, responder or community member should be treated as an acceptable cost of production. Promoting health is the larger ambition, that work should not merely avoid destroying health but should support physical, mental and social wellbeing across a working life. Preserving the environment is the intergenerational duty, that our generation's workplaces must not purchase prosperity with poisoned air, water, land and climate for those who follow. You now hold the complete map: three years, thirty-one taught courses, ten elective specialisations, and one dissertation. The map is not the journey, but no serious journey begins well without one. Review the handbook. Choose your direction. Prepare your research interest. Enter your community fully. And when the work becomes difficult, remember the worker on the opening slide, the human being at the centre of every course code.
