Welcome, and congratulations on taking this important step in your academic and professional journey. Welcome to the Doctoral Orientation Lecture for the PhD in Occupational Health, Safety and Environment, a guided tour of the complete doctoral curriculum, presented by the School of Graduate Studies, Triune Biblical University Global Extension, in the United States of America.

Let us begin by understanding what this moment really means. An orientation lecture is not simply an administrative briefing. It is your first scholarly map. It shows you where you are starting from, where you are going, how the journey is structured, and what will be expected of you at every stage. By the end of this lecture, you should be able to describe the architecture of the entire programme, explain the purpose of every year of study, identify your future specialisation options, and understand how the taught courses prepare you for the single most important achievement of the degree, your doctoral dissertation.

Notice the title on this opening slide very carefully. This is a PhD, a Doctor of Philosophy, in Occupational Health, Safety and Environment. Each part of that title carries meaning. The Doctor of Philosophy signals that this is a research degree at the highest level of scholarship. You are not only here to learn what is already known. You are here to learn how to question what is known, how to test it, and ultimately how to add something new to it. Occupational Health concerns the protection and promotion of the physical and mental health of people at work. Safety concerns the prevention of accidents, incidents, injuries and catastrophic loss. Environment concerns the protection of air, water, land, ecosystems and communities from the harmful effects of industrial and organisational activity. When you bring these three together, you get a distinctive scholarly identity: a scholar who protects people, promotes health, and preserves the environment at the same time.

Now look at the four numbers displayed on this slide, because together they summarise the whole programme. First, three years of study. This is a structured three-year doctoral journey. Second, thirty-one taught courses. That is a substantial body of coursework, designed to give you breadth across the whole discipline before you specialise in depth. Third, three credit hours each. Every taught course carries equal weight, three credit hours, which means you should give each course serious and equal scholarly attention. Fourth, ten elective specialisations. Alongside the core curriculum, you will be able to shape your own intellectual identity by choosing from ten specialised elective directions, from ergonomics and psychosocial risk to radiation protection, waste management and environmental sustainability.

The image on this slide is also deliberate. You see a worker in a hard hat and high-visibility vest, looking thoughtfully across an industrial environment. That image represents the human being at the centre of this entire degree. Behind every statistic, every standard, every audit and every research paper is a real person who deserves to return home from work in the same health as when they arrived, and indeed in better health wherever the workplace can promote wellbeing. As doctoral scholars, we must never lose sight of that human centre, even when our work becomes highly technical, statistical or theoretical.

So how should you use this lecture? Treat it as a tour guide. We will move step by step through the programme overview, the full curriculum map, the first year foundations, the second year focus on culture, performance and industries, the third year of advanced sector mastery, the three elective clusters, the dissertation journey, and finally the career pathways that open to you after the degree. Keep a notebook beside you. Whenever a course title catches your attention, write it down. Whenever a research idea crosses your mind, capture it immediately. Doctoral research questions often begin exactly like that, as small sparks of curiosity during an orientation lecture.

Finally, a word about expectation. Doctoral study will stretch you. It should stretch you. You will read more critically, write more precisely, and think more independently than ever before. But you will not walk alone. You will have lecturers, supervisors, fellow candidates and an academic community around you. This orientation is your invitation into that community. So settle in, open your mind, and let us begin our guided tour of the complete doctoral curriculum in Occupational Health, Safety and Environment.

 One more orientation habit will help you throughout this programme: learn to connect every lecture to a workplace you have seen. When we discuss a management system, picture a real organisation. When we discuss epidemiology or toxicology, picture real workers and real exposures. When we discuss electives and dissertation topics, picture the setting where you might one day collect data. Doctoral learning becomes durable when theory is repeatedly tested against remembered reality. Bring your experience into seminars, but hold it openly, ready to revise what you thought you knew. Experience gives doctoral study its questions; scholarship gives experience its discipline. Together, they will make this degree far more than a certificate.
