This slide completes the First Year with the theme From Science to Practice, covering OHSE nine zero five to nine zero eight. If the first four courses built your scientific core, these four courses teach you how science becomes decisions, law, verification, and education in the real world. Many safety programmes fail not because nobody knows what the hazard is, but because risk is judged poorly, legal duties are misunderstood, nobody verifies that controls still work, or workers were never trained effectively. This block addresses each of those failure points directly.

We begin with OHSE nine zero five, Risk Assessment and Management in OHS. This course covers hazard identification, likelihood and severity estimation, and the defensible selection of controls. Risk assessment is the disciplined process of asking three questions: What can go wrong or cause harm? How likely is it, and how bad could it be? And what should we do about it, in what order of priority? You will study qualitative matrices, semi-quantitative scoring, and where appropriate quantitative risk assessment, together with techniques such as job hazard analysis, fault tree thinking, failure mode analysis, and bow-tie models that connect threats, preventive barriers, the hazardous event itself, recovery barriers, and consequences. At doctoral level, you must also learn the limitations of these tools. A risk matrix can hide uncertainty. Two assessors can score the same task differently. Rare but catastrophic events are notoriously difficult to estimate from short operating histories. Your scholarly task is to make judgements that are transparent, precautionary where uncertainty is high, and defensible to workers, managers, regulators, and, if the worst happens, to investigators and courts.

Next, OHSE nine zero six examines Occupational Health and Safety Laws and Regulations: the statutory duties, standards, and enforcement mechanisms that govern workplace protection across jurisdictions. Law turns moral duty into enforceable duty. You will learn how constitutions, acts of parliament, regulations, approved codes of practice, and technical standards fit together, and how general duties to provide a safe workplace, safe plant, safe systems of work, information, training and supervision are applied in practice. You will compare jurisdictions, because this is a global programme: the approach of the United States Occupational Safety and Health Administration, the United Kingdom Health and Safety Executive, the European framework directives, the International Labour Organization conventions, and the developing regulatory systems across Africa, Asia and Latin America. Enforcement matters equally with content. An excellent law that is never inspected is a promise without power. You will evaluate inspectorates, improvement and prohibition notices, administrative penalties, criminal liability, workers compensation, and corporate accountability after fatal incidents, and you will debate the balance between compliance-based and risk-based regulation.

Then, OHSE nine zero seven takes us to Occupational Health and Safety Auditing and Inspection: the systematic conformity evaluation built on document review, site walkthroughs, interviews, and corrective-action tracking. Learn to distinguish the terms. An inspection looks for hazards and unsafe conditions in the physical workplace. An audit examines the management system that should be preventing those hazards. A good auditor triangulates evidence: what the documents say, what the workplace shows, and what people at different levels actually describe. If those three agree, confidence is high. If the policy says one thing and the night shift says another, you have found something important. You will study audit planning, protocols, sampling, interviewing technique, grading of non-conformities, root-cause thinking, and how to write findings that organisations cannot politely ignore. You will also examine auditor competence, independence and ethics, and the danger of audits becoming comfortable rituals in which everyone performs for the visit and nothing changes the following week.

The year closes with OHSE nine zero eight, Occupational Health and Safety Training and Education: adult learning, curriculum design, and the evaluation of training programmes that shift organisational behaviour. This course is your reminder that every control ultimately depends on people understanding something. Adults do not learn like children. They bring experience, they need to know why something matters, and they learn most deeply by doing and reflecting. You will design learning outcomes, select methods from toolbox talks and simulation to e-learning and coaching, and evaluate training beyond the end-of-course smile sheet, by asking whether knowledge was gained, behaviour changed, and results improved. You will also confront the uncomfortable evidence that training alone rarely fixes a badly designed job. If workers are still exposed after excellent training, the answer may be engineering redesign rather than another refresher course.

By the end of Year One, you possess the complete professional cycle: manage, measure disease, understand toxicity, assess exposure, judge risk, satisfy the law, verify by audit, and teach people to work safely. Reflect on this question before moving forward: in workplaces you know, which link in that chain is weakest? Keep it in mind. It may matter later when you choose electives and a dissertation topic.

 Consider also how these four practice courses teach professional courage. A risk assessor must sometimes recommend stopping a profitable task. A legally literate manager must disclose duties that are uncomfortable to hear. An auditor must record a non-conformity even when relationships are warm and pressure to soften language is real. A trainer must tell senior leaders that another classroom session will not repair a dangerous design. Technical competence without courage produces elegant reports and unchanged workplaces. As you study this block, examine not only the methods, but also the character required to use them when the findings are unpopular, expensive or urgent.
