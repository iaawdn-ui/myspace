We now turn to the programme overview: About the Doctoral Programme. The opening sentence on this slide defines the degree precisely, so let us unpack it together. The PhD in Occupational Health, Safety and Environment is a research-intensive doctoral degree that prepares scholars to protect the health, safety and wellbeing of workers while safeguarding the environments in which they operate.

The phrase research-intensive is crucial. This programme does not treat research as an afterthought, or as something you only think about in your final year. From the very first courses, you will be trained to ask researchable questions, to read evidence critically, to interpret data honestly, and to evaluate interventions scientifically. The taught courses build the knowledge base. The research training builds the method. The dissertation then brings both together in an original contribution to knowledge. That is what distinguishes a doctoral graduate from every other level of graduate. A masters graduate can apply advanced knowledge. A doctoral graduate can create new knowledge and defend that creation before experts.

The definition also tells us who this programme serves. It prepares scholars, not only practitioners. Of course, many of you will continue to work as practitioners, managers, regulators, consultants and executives, and this degree will make you far more effective in those roles. But it asks something more of you: to think like a scholar in those roles. To base decisions on evidence rather than habit. To measure outcomes rather than assume them. And to publish, teach and lead so that your learning benefits workplaces far beyond your own.

Now consider the three complementary pillars that the slide places side by side, because together they form a single scholarly identity. The first pillar is Occupational Health. This is described as the science of anticipating, recognising, evaluating and controlling workplace health hazards. Memorise those four verbs, because they describe the professional method of occupational hygiene and occupational health practice everywhere in the world. Anticipate hazards before work even begins. Recognise hazards that are present, including those that cannot be seen. Evaluate them by measuring exposure and comparing it with health-based standards. And control them, preferably by eliminating them or engineering them away, rather than relying only on personal protective equipment. Occupational health deals especially with exposures whose effects may take months or years to appear, such as dusts, chemicals, noise, radiation and infectious agents.

The second pillar is Safety Science, described here through engineering and the systematic prevention of accidents, incidents and loss events in complex operations. If occupational health often deals with slow, cumulative harm, safety science often deals with sudden harm: the fall, the explosion, the collision, the structural collapse, the loss of containment. Modern safety science studies how accidents emerge from interacting technical, human and organisational factors, especially in complex systems where no single person sees the whole picture. In this programme you will learn to move beyond blaming the last person who touched the machine, towards designing systems in which errors are caught, contained and learned from.

The third pillar is Environmental Stewardship: the responsible management of industrial impacts on air, water, land and communities. A workplace does not end at its fence line. Emissions travel. Effluents flow. Waste goes somewhere. Noise crosses boundaries. Communities live with the consequences of industrial decisions for generations. Environmental stewardship therefore widens your responsibility from the worker, to the community, to the planet itself. It also connects this degree to the global sustainability agenda, climate action, and the expectations now placed on responsible corporations.

At the bottom of this slide, three summary numbers reinforce the opening slide: thirty-one taught courses, ten elective specialisations, and one supervised dissertation. Hold that pattern in mind: breadth, choice, and original research. Thirty-one courses give you breadth. Ten electives give you choice and depth. One dissertation gives you authorship. That one dissertation will, in time, become the single most important scholarly work of your life so far, and everything before it is designed to prepare you for it.

Before we move on, reflect on one question. Which of these three pillars most closely matches your background, and which one is least familiar to you? Your strength will become your foundation, and your gap will become one of your most valuable learning opportunities. A good doctoral candidate knows both.

 As you reflect on these three pillars, also notice how often real problems cross their boundaries. A solvent can injure a worker's nervous system, create a fire risk, and pollute a river after disposal. Noise can damage hearing, mask alarms, and disturb a neighbouring school. A poorly managed contractor can threaten employees, visitors and the surrounding community in the same incident. That is why this programme refuses to separate health, safety and environment into isolated careers too early. Integration is not administrative convenience. It reflects how harm actually moves through the world. Train yourself, from this first week, to ask who else is affected whenever you identify any single hazard.
