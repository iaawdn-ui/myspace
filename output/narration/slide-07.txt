This slide continues the Second Year sector focus with courses OHSE nine one three to nine one five, under the title Three Industries, Three Hazard Profiles. Healthcare, mining, and oil and gas could hardly look more different. One cares for patients, one extracts minerals from the earth, and one extracts and processes hydrocarbons. Yet studying them together teaches the most transferable lesson of the entire sector strand: the method of analysis remains stable even when hazards change completely. In every sector you identify the energy sources and agents, trace the pathways of exposure, identify the people at risk, design layered controls, and verify those controls under real operating pressure.

First, OHSE nine one three, OHS in the Healthcare Industry, examines biological exposures, sharps injuries, patient-handling ergonomics, and the psychological demands placed on clinical staff. Healthcare presents a moral paradox: the people society depends on to heal others work in one of the more hazardous service environments. Nurses, doctors, laboratory staff, cleaners, porters, ambulance teams and waste handlers face bloodborne pathogens, airborne infections, needle-stick and sharps injuries, cytotoxic drugs, disinfectants, latex and other allergens, radiation in imaging and therapy, workplace violence, fatigue from shift work, and intense emotional labour. Notice the three labels on this slide: biohazard, sharps, and ergonomics. Patient handling deserves special emphasis, because manually lifting, transferring and repositioning patients remains a major cause of musculoskeletal injury, and the solution lies in patient-handling equipment, team techniques, early mobility planning and safe staffing, not in telling nurses to bend their knees. You will also study infection prevention and control, exposure control plans, post-exposure prophylaxis, vaccination, safe sharps devices and sharps waste, together with burnout, moral distress and violence prevention. The doctoral question here is how hospitals can protect staff with the same seriousness with which they protect patients, because a depleted and injured workforce cannot deliver safe care for long.

Second, OHSE nine one four takes us into OHS in the Mining Industry: ground control, airborne dust and respirable crystalline silica, mobile equipment, and the emergency readiness unique to underground and surface operations. Mining concentrates enormous energies in environments shaped by geology, which never behaves exactly as predicted. Underground, the roof and walls themselves can fail. Pillars can collapse. Gas can accumulate, floodwater can inrush, heat increases with depth, and escape routes may be long and steep. On the surface, high walls can fail, haul trucks of extraordinary size share roads with light vehicles, and blasting, drilling and crushing generate dust and noise. Study the three labels: ground control, silica dust, and mobile plant. Respirable crystalline silica deserves your closest attention, because silica-related lung disease, including silicosis, remains a global occupational health scandal in mining, quarrying, tunnelling, construction and stone work. It is preventable by wet methods, ventilation, isolation, enclosed cabs, housekeeping that avoids dry sweeping, respiratory protection as a backup, and health surveillance, yet it still disables and kills workers when controls are weak. You will also examine strata control, support design, gas monitoring, ventilation planning, collision avoidance, fatigue in long shifts, contractor management, mine rescue, refuge chambers and the lessons of major mining disasters. Mining teaches humility: the earth always has a vote.

Third, OHSE nine one five introduces OHS in the Oil and Gas Industry, covering process safety, hydrocarbon releases, confined-space entry, and high-consequence environments across drilling, refining and distribution. Here the defining feature is stored energy and flammable inventory at scale. A loss of containment can, within seconds, become a jet fire, a flash fire, a vapour cloud explosion or a toxic release, which is why this sector distinguishes personal safety, measured by slips, trips and injuries, from process safety, measured by the integrity of barriers that prevent catastrophic events. The labels name process safety, hydrogen sulphide, usually shortened to H two S, and confined space. Hydrogen sulphide is a toxic gas that can deaden the sense of smell at dangerous concentrations, so workers must trust instruments rather than noses. Confined spaces such as vessels, tanks and pits can contain toxic or oxygen-deficient atmospheres and have killed would-be rescuers again and again, which is why entry permits, atmospheric testing, ventilation, attendants and rehearsed rescue arrangements are non-negotiable. Across upstream drilling, midstream transport and downstream refining, you will study hazard and operability studies, layers of protection, safety instrumented systems, management of change, permit-to-work, isolation, simultaneous operations, well control, asset integrity, and the organisational lessons of disasters such as Piper Alpha, Texas City and Deepwater Horizon, used in this programme as scholarly case studies in how technical and organisational failures align.

The note at the bottom of this slide is your progress marker: by the end of Year Two, candidates have examined eight industries in depth and can transfer safety principles across dramatically different working environments. That transferability is the real qualification. Employers and governments do not need graduates who can only recite one industry's rules. They need leaders who can enter an unfamiliar operation, learn its hazard profile quickly, ask the right scientific and organisational questions, and build controls that respect both human life and operational reality. Before moving on, compare these three sectors in your notes. Ask where the hazard is biological, where it is geological, and where it is chemical energy. Ask who bears the risk: the caregiver, the miner, the process operator, the contractor, the patient, or the neighbouring community. That comparative habit is doctoral thinking in action.

 A further lesson connects healthcare, mining, and oil and gas: never ignore low-frequency, high-consequence events merely because recent performance looks good. A hospital may go years without a major infection outbreak, a mine without a collapse, a refinery without a large release, while weak barriers quietly accumulate. Injury statistics alone can create false confidence. These sectors therefore rely on barrier management, critical control verification, emergency exercises and learning from precursors. As scholars, study the near miss with the same respect you give the disaster. The near miss is tuition paid without casualties. Organisations that waste it often pay again, at a far higher price.
