Welcome to First Year, the Scientific Core. Year One is described on this slide as the foundation, and the first four courses, OHSE nine zero one to nine zero four, each carrying three credit hours, will establish how you think about the entire discipline. Do not underestimate foundations simply because they come first. In doctoral study, foundations are not elementary material. They are the deepest material, revisited at a higher level. A building can only rise as high as its foundation allows, and your research can only be as strong as the scientific core beneath it.

We begin with OHSE nine zero one, Occupational Health and Safety Management Systems. This course examines the design, implementation, and continual improvement of structured OHS management frameworks and corporate governance. Think of a management system as the operating system of safety in an organisation. Without it, safety depends on individual heroics and good intentions, which fail the moment a key person is absent or production pressure rises. With it, safety becomes planned, resourced, assigned, measured, and improved as a normal part of management. You will study the plan, do, check, act cycle, leadership and worker participation, policy, objectives, competence, operational control, emergency preparedness, performance monitoring, internal audit, and management review, in line with international frameworks such as ISO forty-five thousand and one. At doctoral level, you will not merely memorise these elements. You will critique them. You will ask whether management systems genuinely reduce harm or sometimes become paper compliance exercises, and how scholars can tell the difference. That critical question could itself become a research direction.

Next is OHSE nine zero two, Occupational Epidemiology. This course teaches you to measure and interpret the distribution and determinants of disease and injury in working populations. Epidemiology is the science that tells us whether a pattern of illness is real or coincidental. If workers in one department develop more respiratory disease than workers elsewhere, is that caused by their exposure, their age profile, smoking, previous employment, or chance? Epidemiology gives you the tools to answer: incidence and prevalence, rates and ratios, cohort, case-control and cross-sectional designs, exposure classification, bias, confounding, and the careful interpretation of association before anyone claims causation. These skills are indispensable at doctoral level, because your own dissertation data will only be as credible as your understanding of study design. Epidemiology also teaches intellectual humility: the difference between finding a difference and proving a cause.

Then comes OHSE nine zero three, Occupational Toxicology. Here you will study how chemical, physical, and biological agents produce adverse health effects through dose, route, and duration of exposure. The founding principle of toxicology, often traced to Paracelsus, is that the dose makes the poison. Almost any substance can be harmful at a sufficient dose, and many dangerous substances can be present at doses too low to cause measurable harm, although for some agents no safe threshold is assumed. You will examine inhalation, skin absorption, ingestion and injection as routes of entry; acute effects that appear quickly and chronic effects that emerge after years; target organs such as the lungs, liver, kidneys, nervous system and skin; and concepts such as occupational exposure limits, biomarkers and dose-response relationships. Toxicology turns an abstract safety data sheet into a human story: what enters the body, where it travels, what it damages, and how we can prevent that damage.

The fourth course, OHSE nine zero four, is Occupational Hygiene and Exposure Assessment. If toxicology explains how agents harm, occupational hygiene is the practical science of recognising workplace hazards, measuring exposures, and evaluating the effectiveness of controls. This is where science meets the workplace floor, with sampling pumps, direct-reading instruments, noise dosimeters, thermal indices and ventilation measurements. You will learn to build a qualitative exposure profile, to group workers into similar exposure groups, to design a sampling strategy, to interpret results statistically despite day-to-day variability, and to communicate findings to managers and workers in language that leads to action. Crucially, you will apply the hierarchy of controls: elimination first, then substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment last. A doctoral hygienist understands not only how to measure, but also when a measurement is trustworthy and what decision it should trigger.

Together, these four courses form a chain. The management system asks the questions. Epidemiology counts the harm. Toxicology explains the harm. Hygiene measures the exposure that precedes the harm. By the end of this first block, you should be able to walk into almost any workplace, structured by a management system, identify who may be harmed, reason about the agents involved, and propose a defensible plan to measure and control them. That is a powerful professional capability, and it is only the first half of Year One.

 Before leaving this scientific core, notice the ethical weight carried by measurement. An exposure result can trigger expensive engineering controls, change a worker's duties, influence compensation, or reassure people who remain at risk. A wrong measurement is therefore not a harmless academic error. Sampling the wrong day, placing a monitor incorrectly, using an uncalibrated instrument, or averaging away peak exposures can produce confident falsehood. Doctoral scholars protect the chain from field method to laboratory analysis to statistical interpretation to management decision. When a result matters to health, ask how it was obtained before asking what it says. That scepticism is central to scientific integrity.
