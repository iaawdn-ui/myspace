## SEGMENT 3 — The Hook: A Family Walks In

`[SLIDE: Simple illustration — a mother and a teenage boy sitting in a waiting room. Minimal text: "The Adebayo Family".]`

Imagine it is Tuesday afternoon. A mother, Mrs. Adebayo, arrives at your office with her fourteen-year-old son, Tunde. She sits down, and before you can finish introducing yourself, she says: "Please, help me fix him. He has stopped going to school. He shouts at me. He locks himself in his room. Something is wrong with this boy." [PAUSE]

Tunde sits with his arms folded, looking at the floor.

Now, pause for a moment and notice your own first instinct. [PAUSE] Many of us — especially those trained in medicine, teaching, or ministry — would instinctively turn to Tunde. What is wrong with him? Is he depressed? Is he being bullied? Is it defiance? Is it a spiritual problem?

Those are reasonable questions. But a marriage and family therapist asks a different first question. Not only, "What is wrong with Tunde?" but, "What is happening in the relationships around Tunde, such that his behaviour makes sense?" [PAUSE]

As the session unfolds, you learn that Mr. Adebayo lost his job four months ago. He now spends most evenings out of the house. Mrs. Adebayo has taken on extra work and is exhausted. Tunde's younger sister has become very quiet and very obedient. And Tunde's school refusal began almost exactly when his father stopped coming home for dinner.

`[SLIDE: Same illustration, but now the father, the sister, and lines connecting all four family members fade in.]`

Suddenly the picture is bigger. Tunde's symptom is real, and he needs help. But his symptom is also a signal — a message from a family under strain. That shift in seeing is the heart of this entire profession. Hold on to the Adebayo family. We will return to them.

## SEGMENT 5 — Node 1: Definition

`[SLIDE: Node 1 enlarged — "Definition". Text: "A mental health profession that treats individuals, couples and families from a relational, systemic perspective."]`

Marriage and Family Therapy is a distinct mental health profession. It is not a technique that other professionals occasionally use. It is a discipline with its own training standards, its own code of ethics, its own licensure, and its own body of research.

Here is a working definition: Marriage and Family Therapy is the diagnosis and treatment of mental, emotional and relational problems, in which human difficulties are understood and treated within the context of relationships — especially the family. [PAUSE]

Notice three things in that definition.

First, MFTs do diagnose and treat mental and emotional disorders. We are trained in depression, anxiety, trauma, psychosis, substance use — the full range. You will study that in Psychopathology in Families and Assessment and Diagnosis.

Second, the word "relational". Our unit of attention is not only the person in front of us, but the relationships that person lives inside.

Third, the phrase "within the context of relationships" does not mean we only see families together in the room. Many marriage and family therapists see individuals. What makes the work systemic is not how many people are in the room — it is how the therapist thinks. [PAUSE]

Which brings us to node two.

---

## SEGMENT 6 — Node 2: The Systemic Lens

`[SLIDE: Node 2 enlarged — "The Systemic Lens". Illustration: a hanging mobile with five pieces balanced on strings.]`

Picture a mobile — the kind that hangs above a baby's cot. Five pieces hang from strings, perfectly balanced. Now reach out and push just one piece. [PAUSE] What happens? Every other piece moves. The whole mobile swings, adjusts, and eventually finds a new balance.

A family is like that mobile. When Mr. Adebayo lost his job, one piece moved. And every other piece — his wife, his son, his daughter — shifted in response. Tunde's school refusal, his sister's silence, his mother's exhaustion: these are not four separate problems. They are one system finding a new, painful balance.

This is the systemic lens, and it rests on a few core ideas you will meet again and again.

Wholeness: the family is more than the sum of its members. You cannot understand Tunde by studying Tunde alone.

Circular causality: instead of asking "who started it," we ask how each person's behaviour feeds the next. The father withdraws, the mother over-functions, the son acts out, the father withdraws further. Round and round.

Homeostasis: systems resist change and seek balance — even an unhealthy balance. This is why families sometimes seem to sabotage the very improvement they asked for.

And the idea that the symptom often serves a function. Tunde's crisis may be the one thing that has forced his parents to sit in the same room again. [PAUSE]

If you take only one idea from today, take this one: the family therapist thinks in patterns and relationships, not only in individuals and diagnoses.

## SEGMENT 7 — Node 3: History in Brief

`[SLIDE: Node 3 enlarged — "History". A compact timeline: 1940s Marriage counselling and child guidance → 1950s Palo Alto, Bateson → 1960s–70s Founding schools → 1980s–90s Postmodern turn → Today: evidence-based, global profession.]`

Node three is history, and next week is devoted entirely to it, so today is just a glimpse.

Marriage and Family Therapy is young. It was born in the middle of the twentieth century, when several streams flowed together: the marriage counselling movement, the child guidance clinics, and — most importantly — a group of researchers in Palo Alto, California, led by the anthropologist Gregory Bateson. They were studying communication in families where one member had schizophrenia, and they noticed something that psychiatry had missed: the patient's symptoms made a strange kind of sense once you watched the whole family interact. [PAUSE]

From that insight, the founding schools emerged through the 1960s and 70s: Murray Bowen, Salvador Minuchin, Jay Haley, Virginia Satir, Carl Whitaker and others. Then, in the 1980s and 90s, a postmodern turn brought narrative and solution-focused approaches. And today, MFT is an evidence-based profession practised on every continent — including a growing presence across Africa.

## SEGMENT 8 — Node 4: The Major Schools

`[SLIDE: Node 4 enlarged — "Major Schools". Two columns: "First generation: Bowenian, Structural, Strategic, Experiential" and "Postmodern: Solution-Focused, Narrative, Collaborative". A third line: "Integrative and attachment-based: EFT".]`

Node four: the major schools. Again, only a preview — you will spend weeks six and seven here, and all of MFT 702 on theory.

Think of the schools as different lenses for looking at the same family. Each one would notice something different about the Adebayos.

A Bowenian therapist would ask about patterns across generations — did Mr. Adebayo's own father withdraw under stress?

A Structural therapist would look at boundaries and hierarchy — has Tunde been pulled into a parental role? Has Mrs. Adebayo and her son formed an alliance that excludes the father?

A Strategic therapist would map the repeating cycle and design a task to interrupt it.

An Experiential therapist would focus on the feelings no one is saying aloud — the father's shame, the mother's fear.

A Solution-Focused therapist would ask about the days Tunde did go to school, and what was different on those days.

And a Narrative therapist would help the family separate Tunde from "the problem" — speaking of the school refusal as something the family is fighting together, rather than something Tunde is. [PAUSE]

None of these is simply right or wrong. They are tools. Part of your journey in this programme is discovering which ones fit your hands.

## SEGMENT 9 — Node 5: Professional Identity and Scope

`[SLIDE: Node 5 enlarged — "Professional Identity and Scope". Logos/text: AAMFT, COAMFTE, State Licensing Boards. Comparison strip: MFT / Counsellor / Psychologist / Social Worker / Pastor.]`

Node five is about who we are as a profession, and what we are permitted to do.

In the United States, Marriage and Family Therapy is one of the core licensed mental health professions. The American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy, the AAMFT, is the professional body. It publishes the Code of Ethics you will study closely in MFT 704. The Commission on Accreditation, known as COAMFTE, sets training standards. And each state has a licensing board that defines the scope of practice and the hours of supervised experience required before you can practise independently. [PAUSE]

So how does an MFT differ from a counsellor, a psychologist, a social worker, or a pastor? The honest answer is that there is overlap — we often work side by side. But the distinctive of the MFT is the systemic lens we discussed earlier. A psychologist is trained primarily in individual assessment and treatment. A social worker brings a strong focus on social systems and resources. A pastor brings spiritual authority and community. The MFT is trained, from day one, to see and treat relationships as the primary focus. [PAUSE]

For those of you studying from Nigeria and other parts of the world, note that licensure structures differ by country. We will address international pathways in week thirteen. But the professional identity — the systemic way of thinking and the ethical standards — travels with you wherever you practise.

## SEGMENT 10 — Node 6: The Person of the Therapist

`[SLIDE: Node 6 enlarged — "The Person of the Therapist". Simple image: an empty chair facing the family illustration from earlier.]`

Node six is the one most easily forgotten, and in some ways the most important: the person of the therapist. You.

Every therapist walks into the room carrying their own family. Your experiences of marriage, of parents, of conflict, of forgiveness — these shape what you notice and what you miss. Virginia Satir called this the "use of self". Murray Bowen insisted that therapists must work on their own differentiation before they can help others. You will do your own family-of-origin work in this programme, and it will not always be comfortable. [PAUSE]

For us at Triune, this node also includes faith. You are training at a biblical university, and many of you come with a conviction that marriage and family are not merely social arrangements but part of God's design for human flourishing. That conviction can be a tremendous resource. It gives you a deep respect for covenant, for reconciliation, for the dignity of every family member. [PAUSE]

It also carries responsibilities. Clinical practice requires that we never impose our values on clients, that we serve families of every faith and none with equal care, and that we hold our theology and our clinical training in honest conversation rather than letting one silence the other. Week fifteen is devoted to this integration. For now, simply begin to notice: how does your own story and your own faith shape the way you saw the Adebayo family a few minutes ago?

## SEGMENT 12 — Returning to the Adebayos

`[SLIDE: The Adebayo family illustration, all four members, now with a therapist's chair facing them.]`

So, what happens to the Adebayo family?

A marriage and family therapist does not dismiss Mrs. Adebayo's request to "fix" Tunde. Her distress is real and her son does need support. But the therapist gently widens the frame. Perhaps the therapist asks whether Mr. Adebayo would be willing to attend the next session. Perhaps the therapist notices aloud that Tunde's trouble began when the family's routines changed. Perhaps, for the first time in months, the family begins to talk about the job loss, the shame, and the fear — instead of only talking about Tunde. [PAUSE]

Nothing has been "fixed" yet. But the mobile has started to move in a new direction. That is the beginning of family therapy.
