## SEGMENT 2 — Nodes 1 and 2: What MFT Is, and Where It Came From

`[MAP: Nodes 1 and 2 light up.]`

Node one, week one: what marriage and family therapy is. The sentence: MFT is a licensed mental health profession that treats people in the context of their relationships, using a systemic lens. You met the Adebayos, the mobile, the six nodes of the profession — definition, lens, history, schools, identity and the person of the therapist — and you learned that an MFT asks not only "what is wrong with Tunde?" but "what is happening around Tunde such that his behaviour makes sense?"

Node two, week two: history. The sentence: the field was born in the 1950s when researchers in several places, independently, began seeing whole families — and discovered that the family was the patient. The streams before 1950: psychoanalysis, child guidance, social work, the marriage counselling movement from 1929, pastoral care. Palo Alto, 1952 to 1962: Bateson, Jackson, Haley, Weakland, the double bind, the Mental Research Institute. The pioneers across America. The 1960s coalescing. The 1970s golden age of the schools. The 1980s and 90s critique and postmodern turn. And today's evidence-based, integrative field. [PAUSE]

How do these two nodes relate? Node one tells you what the profession is; node two tells you that it did not have to exist. It was a discovery — several discoveries — and every school you have learned since is a descendant of someone in that timeline. When you can place a school on the timeline, you understand what problem it was trying to solve.

## SEGMENT 3 — Node 3: The Paradigm Shift

`[MAP: Node 3 lights up; the six shifts appear around it.]`

Node three, week three: systemic thinking as a paradigm shift. The sentence: systemic therapy is not a technique added to individual therapy; it is a different set of assumptions about where problems live. Two coaches watching the same match: one sees a striker in a slump; the other sees a team that stopped delivering the ball.

The six shifts. From the individual to the system. From linear to circular causality. From content to process. From why to what — function, feedback, homeostasis. From observer to participant. And outward to context and meaning — Bronfenbrenner's rings, and the limits of the analogy: people are not footballers; where there is harm, circularity stops and responsibility is named. [PAUSE]

How does node three relate to the others? It is the hinge of the whole course. Nodes one and two describe the profession; node three gives it its mind. Everything after — the concepts of weeks four and five, the schools of weeks six and seven — is the paradigm worked out in detail. If you are short of revision time, revise week three first. Every exam question in this course can be answered by asking, "which shift is this testing?"

## SEGMENT 4 — Nodes 4 and 5: The Core Concepts — Dynamics and Structure

`[MAP: Nodes 4 and 5 light up together, joined by a bracket labelled "the concepts".]`

Nodes four and five belong together, and the exam will treat them that way.

Node four, week four, on the whiteboard: the concepts of dynamics — how a system moves. Wholeness: the whole is more than its parts; we treat the lines between the dots. Circular causality and punctuation. Homeostasis: the family thermostat, with rules as set point and anxiety as sensor. Feedback: negative steadies, positive amplifies, and the therapist uses both on purpose. And the supporting cast — equifinality, family rules, redundancy.

Node five, week five, in layers: the concepts of structure — how a system is arranged. Members. Subsystems: spousal, parental, sibling. Boundaries: clear, diffuse, rigid, on the enmeshment–disengagement continuum, judged by function not closeness. Hierarchy and the cross-generational coalition. And Bowen's triangle, the engine that keeps rearranging the structure. [PAUSE]

Here is the relationship. Week four is the physics; week five is the anatomy. A structural map is a snapshot of a system; feedback and homeostasis explain why the snapshot keeps reproducing itself. Students lose marks when they describe a structure without its dynamics — "Tunde is in a coalition with his mother" — and stop. The full answer continues: "and the loop that maintains it runs like this, and the thermostat setting it protects is this." Structure plus dynamics is a formulation. Either alone is a label.

## SEGMENT 5 — Nodes 6 and 7: The Schools, Classic and Postmodern

`[MAP: Nodes 6 and 7 light up; the comparison grid appears in miniature.]`

Node six, week six: the four classic schools. Bowen — anxiety across generations, differentiation, the calm coach. Structural — a structure that has not adapted, restructuring, the stage director. Strategic — the stuck solution, the solved problem, the strategist. Experiential — suppressed feeling, growth, the therapist's own self. Five criteria, four doors, one family.

Node seven, week seven: the postmodern schools. Solution-Focused — exceptions and a described future. Narrative — the problem-saturated story re-authored. Collaborative — problems in language, dissolved in dialogue. And the sixth row that separated the generations: who is the expert? [PAUSE]

How do the schools relate to the concepts? Each classic school took one or two of the core concepts and built a practice around them. Bowen built on triangles and multigenerational transmission. Minuchin built on boundaries and hierarchy. The strategists built on feedback loops and homeostasis — the stuck solution is a positive feedback loop. Satir and Whitaker built on process over content and the therapist as participant. The schools are not alternatives to the concepts. They are the concepts, specialized.

And the postmodern schools? They kept the first shift — problems live between people, not inside them — and questioned the fifth more radically than anyone before: if the therapist is a participant, who gave the therapist the right to define the problem? That question is the whole postmodern turn in one sentence.

## SEGMENT 6 — Five Apparent Contradictions, Resolved

`[SLIDE: Five pairs of terms facing each other, each with a one-line resolution.]`

Good synthesis names the places where the material seems to contradict itself. Here are five, with their resolutions.

One: homeostasis versus change. If families resist change, how does therapy work? Resolution: every living system does both. Negative feedback keeps the set point; positive feedback moves it. Therapy does not fight homeostasis; it changes what the thermostat is set to.

Two: circular causality versus responsibility. If everyone is part of the loop, is nobody responsible? Resolution: circularity is a tool for understanding patterns, not a theory of morality. Where there is violence or abuse, the tool is put down and responsibility is named. You have heard this in weeks three, four and seven, and the exam will expect you to say it.

Three: expert versus not-knowing. Minuchin directs; Anderson refuses to. Resolution: separate two kinds of expertise. Expertise about the content of a family's life belongs to the family. Expertise about process — how conversations change, when to slow a spiral, when a child is being pulled upward — belongs to the therapist and cannot be given away.

Four: structure versus story. Is a family a structure to restructure or a story to re-author? Resolution: both are maps, and a map is not the territory. Use the map that opens the most doors for this family, and hold it as a hypothesis.

Five: brief versus multigenerational. Three sessions or three years? Resolution: it depends on the goal. A solved problem can be brief; a changed self is slow. Tell the family which one you are offering.

## SEGMENT 7 — The One Big Idea

`[MAP: All seven nodes lit; the centre fills in: "The problem lives between people — and so does the solution."]`

Now the centre of the map. If you remember one sentence from the first half of this course, let it be this: the problem lives between people — and so does the solution.

Watch it run through all seven nodes. Week one: the Adebayos' symptom is a signal from a family under strain, not a defect in a boy. Week two: a whole field was founded when researchers saw that the patient was the relationship. Week three: two coaches; the second one wins because she treats the passing, not the striker. Week four: we treat the lines between the dots. Week five: the map is of boundaries, hierarchies and triangles — all of them between people. Week six: four classic schools, each intervening in the space between members. Week seven: even when the postmodern schools moved from system to story, the story was still told between people, in conversation, and changed there. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Three practical implications.]`

If the big idea is true, three things follow for how you will practise. Assessment is relational: you cannot formulate a case from one person's account. Intervention is relational: the smallest useful change is a change in a pattern, not in a person. And the therapist is in the room, part of the pattern, responsible for what their presence adds. That third implication is where we go next week, when we study the therapist's stance.

## SEGMENT 8 — The Adebayos: A One-Page Formulation

`[SLIDE: A formulation template with seven lines — Presenting problem · System · Pattern · Structure · Hypothesis · Story · First moves.]`

Let me show you what the first half of the course has given you, by writing a one-page formulation of the family you have followed for eight weeks. This is the format I recommend for the exam and for the rest of your training.

Presenting problem: Tunde, fourteen, has refused school for several weeks; mother requests that he be "fixed."

System: father, unemployed four months, out most evenings; mother, two jobs, exhausted; Tunde; Bisi, ten, quiet and compliant; a grandmother active by telephone.

Pattern: father withdraws, mother over-functions, Tunde acts out, father withdraws further. Each person punctuates the loop from the step after their own.

Structure: diffuse boundary mother–son, rigid boundary around father; Tunde elevated into a coalition with mother against father; Bisi isolated in the sibling subsystem; a triangle in which marital anxiety has settled on the mother–son side.

Hypothesis — not a diagnosis: the family reorganized around the job loss; Tunde's symptom keeps the parents in the same room and holds the new structure in place; improvement in Tunde may be followed by symptoms elsewhere.

Story: a problem-saturated account — "a difficult boy" — competing with unique outcomes: the Tuesday his father drove him to school.

First moves: see the whole family; join the father; an enactment between the parents about tomorrow morning; amplify the exception; name "the Heaviness." [PAUSE]

Seven lines. Every one of them comes from a different week. That page is what synthesis looks like.

## SEGMENT 9 — Preparing for the Mid-Term

`[SLIDE: Exam format and study strategy.]`

The mid-term has three parts, and each maps onto what we have done. Part one: short definitions — fifteen key terms, two sentences each, with an example. Part two: a case formulation, in exactly the seven-line format you have just seen, from a short vignette you have not met before. Part three: one comparison essay — two schools on the five criteria, applied to the vignette.

`[SLIDE: Twenty-five terms to know, listed.]`

Study strategy. First, build a glossary of twenty-five terms and write each from memory. Here they are: systemic lens, circular causality, punctuation, wholeness, emergent property, homeostasis, set point, negative feedback, positive feedback, equifinality, family rules, subsystem, boundary, enmeshment, disengagement, hierarchy, cross-generational coalition, triangle, detriangling, differentiation, enactment, directive, externalizing, miracle question, not-knowing stance. Second, redraw your own family map from week five and write its formulation in seven lines. Third, practise the five contradictions out loud until the resolutions are yours.

`[SLIDE: Five common mistakes.]`

Five mistakes that cost marks every year. Confusing negative and positive feedback — negative is not bad, positive is not good. Calling every close family enmeshed — the test is function. Describing structure without dynamics. Treating hierarchy as a synonym for patriarchy. And listing techniques without the theory that explains why they should work. Avoid those five and you are most of the way to a distinction.
