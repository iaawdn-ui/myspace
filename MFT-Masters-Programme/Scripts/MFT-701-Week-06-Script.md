## SEGMENT 2 — Column One: Bowen Family Systems Theory

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Murray Bowen, psychiatrist; NIMH family study 1954–59; Georgetown Family Center; "Family Therapy in Clinical Practice" (1978).]`

Column one. Murray Bowen was a psychiatrist who, in the 1950s, admitted whole families to the research wards of the National Institute of Mental Health to watch schizophrenia in its relational context. What he built was less a set of techniques than a theory of human emotional functioning.

`[GRID: Bowen column fills row by row.]`

View of the problem: symptoms arise from chronic anxiety in a family with low differentiation of self — the capacity to stay connected to others while holding your own thinking and feeling. That anxiety is handled through triangles and transmitted across generations, so Bowen reads a problem in three generations, not one.

Goal of therapy: raise the level of differentiation, a little, in at least one person. Not symptom relief as such — a calmer, more thoughtful person in a calmer system.

Therapist's role: a calm coach and researcher. Bowen deliberately kept emotional distance, asking questions rather than giving directions, staying out of the family's triangles, and modelling a non-anxious presence.

Signature techniques: the genogram; process questions — "what do you do when your mother gets anxious?"; coaching individuals to take an I-position; and sending adults back to their families of origin to change their own part in old patterns.

Time and pace: past and present together — multigenerational — and slow. Bowen often worked for years with the most motivated adult, and considered that sufficient to change the system.

## SEGMENT 3 — Column Two: Structural Family Therapy

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Salvador Minuchin; Wiltwyck School for Boys; Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic; "Families and Family Therapy" (1974).]`

Column two. Salvador Minuchin, an Argentine-born child psychiatrist, built his model with poor, multi-problem families in New York and then at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic. Insight-oriented talk had not helped those families; action in the room did.

`[GRID: Structural column fills.]`

View of the problem: symptoms are produced by a dysfunctional family structure — boundaries too diffuse or too rigid, an unclear hierarchy, a cross-generational coalition — usually a structure that has failed to adapt to a developmental change or an outside stress. You drew this last week.

Goal of therapy: restructure. Clear boundaries, a functioning executive subsystem, siblings free to be siblings. The symptom is expected to fade when the structure changes.

Therapist's role: active, directive, a stage director. Minuchin first joins the family — accommodating to its style until he is accepted — and then uses that position to challenge the structure, sometimes deliberately unbalancing it by siding with one member.

Signature techniques: joining; mapping; enactment — having the family do its pattern in the room rather than describe it; boundary making — rearranging chairs, blocking interruptions; intensity and unbalancing; and reframing the symptom as a family matter.

Time and pace: firmly present-focused. The important action happens in the session. Moderate length — typically months rather than years.

## SEGMENT 4 — Column Three: Strategic Family Therapy

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Jay Haley & Cloé Madanes (Washington); the MRI Brief Therapy Center — Watzlawick, Weakland, Fisch, "Change" (1974); roots in Bateson and Milton Erickson.]`

Column three — in fact a family of related models. Jay Haley and Cloé Madanes in Washington; the Mental Research Institute's Brief Therapy Center in Palo Alto; and, with a different flavour, the Milan team. What they share is a root in Gregory Bateson's research project and in the hypnotherapist Milton Erickson, from whom Haley learned that people can be moved to change without understanding why.

`[GRID: Strategic column fills.]`

View of the problem: the problem is the problem — and it is kept going by the family's repeated attempted solutions. The MRI version: more of the same solution produces more of the same problem. Haley's version: the symptom is a move in a sequence, often protecting the family or reflecting a confused hierarchy.

Goal of therapy: solve the presenting problem. Interrupt the sequence. Insight is not required; a change in behaviour is.

Therapist's role: the therapist takes responsibility for designing change. Planful, directive, sometimes indirect — a strategist, as the name says.

Signature techniques: directives; between-session tasks; reframing; and paradoxical interventions — prescribing the symptom, restraining change, "go slowly" — used when direct advice would be resisted. Madanes added playful pretend techniques for children.

Time and pace: present and brief. MRI set a ten-session limit. Change was expected to happen between sessions, at home.

## SEGMENT 5 — Column Four: Experiential Family Therapy

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Virginia Satir, "Conjoint Family Therapy" (1964), "Peoplemaking" (1972); Carl Whitaker, symbolic-experiential therapy, "psychotherapy of the absurd".]`

Column four. Experiential therapy drew from the humanistic movement of the 1960s and had two very different founders. Virginia Satir, a social worker and the warmest figure in the field, who believed that every family could grow. And Carl Whitaker, a psychiatrist who worked by intuition and provocation and called his approach the psychotherapy of the absurd.

`[GRID: Experiential column fills.]`

View of the problem: families suffer from emotional suppression and incongruent communication. Satir described four defensive stances — placating, blaming, super-reasonable, irrelevant — adopted when self-esteem is low, and a fifth, congruent or levelling, as the goal. Whitaker saw symptoms as the result of avoiding experience.

Goal of therapy: growth, not just symptom relief. Congruence, authentic emotional contact, higher self-esteem, families that can feel and say what is true.

Therapist's role: warm, spontaneous, personally present. The therapist's own self is the main instrument. Whitaker insisted on winning the battle for structure — who comes, when — and then losing the battle for initiative, letting the family lead.

Signature techniques: family sculpting — positioning bodies in space to show relationships; Satir's family reconstruction and parts party; communication coaching; and, for Whitaker, humour, absurdity, self-disclosure and co-therapy.

Time and pace: emphatically here and now. The important event is an emotional experience in the room, not a plan for the week.

## SEGMENT 6 — Reading Across the Rows

`[GRID: Complete grid; rows highlight one at a time.]`

Now read across rather than down. Row one, view of the problem: anxiety across generations; a structure that has not adapted; a stuck solution; a feeling nobody is allowed to have. Four different answers to the same question — why is this family in pain?

Row two, the goal: a more differentiated self; a restructured family; a solved problem; a growing family. Notice that the goal already tells you how each school will judge its own success.

Row three, the therapist: a calm coach; a stage director; a strategist; a fellow human being in the room. Place yourself on that row for a moment. Which chair feels like yours?

Row four, techniques: questions and genograms; enactment and chairs; directives and paradox; sculpting and self. Row five, time and pace: multigenerational and slow; present and moderate; present and brief; here-and-now and intense.

`[SLIDE: A two-by-two map. Horizontal axis: past ↔ present. Vertical axis: insight and experience ↔ action and direction. Bowen top-left; Experiential bottom-left; Structural and Strategic to the right, Strategic lowest.]`

A simpler way to hold all this is a two-by-two map. Across: does the school look mainly to the past or to the present? Down: does it work through insight and experience, or through action and direction? Bowen sits past-and-insight. Experiential sits present-and-experience. Structural sits present-and-action, inside the room. Strategic sits present-and-action, outside the room. Keep this map; next week the postmodern schools will land on it too.

## SEGMENT 7 — The Same Family, Four Doors

`[SLIDE: Four panels — the Adebayo family seen through four first sessions.]`

Let us put the Adebayos in front of each school and watch the first session begin.

Door one, Bowen. The therapist may well see the parents without the children, and may start with Mr. Adebayo if he is willing. A genogram goes on the wall. "How did your own father handle it when work dried up? Who in your family gets anxious first, and who gets the phone call?" Calm, curious, no instructions. The therapist is already watching for the triangles, and refusing to join them.

Door two, Structural. Everyone is in the room, and the therapist notices where they sit: Tunde beside his mother, father nearest the door. After joining — a little small talk with each person, some warmth for the father — the therapist asks the parents to discuss between themselves what should happen about school tomorrow morning. When Tunde interrupts, the therapist gently blocks him. Within ten minutes the structure is visible and the first boundary has been drawn.

Door three, Strategic. The therapist asks for a precise description of the problem: how many days missed, what happens at seven in the morning, step by step. Then: "What have you tried?" Pleading, shouting, the pastor, confiscating the phone. All of it has failed and all of it is more of the same. The session ends with a directive — perhaps that father, and only father, will wake Tunde and walk him to the school gate this week. Something new in the sequence.

Door four, Experiential. Satir might begin with how each person feels sitting here today, and might ask Tunde to sculpt the family — to place each member in the room as he experiences them. He puts his father facing the door. Nobody needs to interpret it. Whitaker might ask, half-joking, what would happen if nobody in the house ever went to work or school again, and watch who laughs and who cannot. [PAUSE]

Four doors, one family. Each door opens on something real.

## SEGMENT 8 — What They Share, and When to Choose Which

`[SLIDE: "What the four share" — list.]`

Before we choose, notice what the four have in common, because it is more than what divides them. All four locate the problem in the pattern rather than in the person. All four work with what is happening now, even Bowen, who reads the past in order to change the present. All four expect the therapist to be active — none of them is a blank screen. And all four believe that a small change in one part of the system can move the whole.

`[SLIDE: "Choose … when …" cards.]`

Now, when might you lean toward each? Lean toward Bowen when the pattern is clearly multigenerational, when an adult is motivated and the rest of the family will not attend, or when the presenting issue is cutoff from a family of origin. Lean toward Structural when the presenting problem is a child or adolescent, when hierarchy and boundaries are visibly confused, or when the family is chaotic and needs organization. Structural ideas are the backbone of the best-evidenced adolescent programmes today — Brief Strategic Family Therapy, Multisystemic Therapy, Functional Family Therapy. Lean toward Strategic when there is one clear, specific symptom, when the family wants brief work, or when every sensible piece of advice has already failed. Lean toward Experiential when a family is emotionally frozen or over-rational, when grief is unspoken, or when the words are fine and the feeling is absent. [PAUSE]

And in practice? Most experienced therapists are integrative. They hold one home model and borrow deliberately. The danger is not borrowing; it is borrowing without knowing why.

## SEGMENT 9 — Critiques and a Brief Reflection

`[SLIDE: "Fair criticisms" — four lines.]`

Each school has earned criticism, and you should know it. Bowen's differentiation can sound like a Western ideal of the autonomous individual; in collectivist cultures it must be translated, not imported. Structural therapy has been accused of a normative family ideal and, as we saw last week, of restoring hierarchy by restoring fathers. Strategic therapy's paradoxical directives raised ethical questions about manipulation and informed consent, and the field has largely moved toward transparency. And experiential therapy depended heavily on the charisma of its founders, which is hard to teach and harder to research. None of these criticisms cancels a school. They tell you where to be careful.

`[SLIDE: Reflection — one line per school.]`

For those who practise from faith, a short reflection. Each school echoes something Scripture already knew. Bowen's transmission across generations is the language of Exodus — the consequences of the fathers visited on the third and fourth generation — and also of its reversal, steadfast love to a thousand generations. Structural therapy's concern for order in the household is the concern of the Pastoral Epistles. Strategic therapy's pragmatism is the voice of Proverbs: wisdom is what works. And Satir's levelling — truth spoken with love from the inner being — is the prayer of Psalm fifty-one. No school is baptized by this; each is simply answerable to something older than itself.
