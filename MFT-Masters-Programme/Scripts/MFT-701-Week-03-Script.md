## SEGMENT 1 — Welcome, Objectives and the Analogy

`[SLIDE: Title card — "Week 3: The Systemic Paradigm Shift".]`

Welcome back to MFT 701. Last week we walked the history of our profession. This week we slow down and examine the single idea at the centre of that history: the shift from seeing problems inside individuals to seeing patterns between people. We call it the systemic paradigm shift. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Learning objectives.]`

By the end of this lecture you will be able to explain what a paradigm is and why the shift to systemic thinking was so radical; describe six specific shifts in how a systemic therapist thinks; apply those shifts to a family case; and recognize the limits of systemic thinking, including when it must not be used to excuse harm.

`[SLIDE: Illustration — a coach at a tactics board with players; caption "The team that stopped scoring".]`

To do this, I am going to use one extended analogy for the whole lecture: a football team. Imagine a club that has gone eight matches without a win. The striker has not scored in two months. The fans are furious, and the newspapers have a simple explanation: the striker has lost it. Drop him. Sell him. [PAUSE]

Now imagine two coaches watching the same match replay. The first coach watches only the striker: his touches, his movement, his confidence. The second coach watches the whole pitch: where the midfield is positioned, who passes to whom, how the team's shape changes when they concede. The first coach is thinking individually. The second is thinking systemically. Hold that image. Every idea today will return to it.

## SEGMENT 2 — What Is a Paradigm? The Individual Paradigm

`[SLIDE: "What is a paradigm?" — Kuhn, 1962.]`

The word paradigm was made famous by the philosopher Thomas Kuhn in 1962. A paradigm is the set of assumptions that a community takes for granted — the lens through which it sees, so familiar that it is almost invisible. A paradigm shift happens when the lens itself changes, and suddenly the same facts look different. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "The individual paradigm" — four assumptions.]`

For most of the twentieth century, mental health worked within what we can call the individual paradigm. It rests on four assumptions. First, the problem is located inside the person — in their biology, their thoughts, or their unconscious. Second, causality is linear: something caused the problem, and treating the cause will fix it. Third, the task of the clinician is to diagnose and treat that one person. And fourth, the clinician stands outside the problem as an objective observer.

`[SLIDE: "On the pitch" — the striker-only view.]`

On the pitch, this is the first coach. The problem is in the striker. Something caused his slump — fitness, confidence, technique. Fix the striker and the team will win again. [PAUSE]

Let me be clear: this paradigm has real strengths. It gave us psychopharmacology, cognitive therapy, and most of what we know about individual disorders. A systemic therapist does not throw it away. But it is incomplete — and when the striker is sold and the new striker also stops scoring, its limits become visible.

## SEGMENT 3 — Shift 1: From the Individual to the System

`[SLIDE: Shift 1 of 6 — "Unit of analysis: from the player to the team".]`

The first shift is the most fundamental: the unit of analysis moves from the individual to the system. [PAUSE]

Watch the second coach. She notices that since the club sold its creative midfielder, almost no passes reach the striker in dangerous areas. He is not failing; he is starved. The problem the fans see in one player is actually a property of the whole team.

`[SLIDE: "In the family" — the identified patient.]`

In the family, the person who carries the visible symptom is called the identified patient. The family identifies him or her as the problem — "fix our son." The systemic therapist respects that distress but quietly widens the frame: what is the whole team doing, such that this player is the one who struggles? [PAUSE]

Remember the Adebayo family from week one. Tunde is the striker who has stopped scoring. The family came to sell him — to have him fixed. The systemic therapist watched the whole pitch and saw a father who had withdrawn, a mother who was exhausted, and a sister who had gone silent. Tunde's symptom was the family's symptom, carried by one member. [PAUSE]

This does not mean the individual disappears. Bowen insisted that a differentiated self within the system is the goal of therapy. The player still matters. But you cannot understand the player without the team.

---

## SEGMENT 4 — Shift 2: From Linear to Circular Causality

`[SLIDE: Shift 2 of 6 — a straight arrow A→B transforms into a loop.]`

The second shift is from linear to circular causality.

Linear causality says A causes B. The midfielder stopped passing, so the striker stopped scoring. But watch the match more closely. Because the striker stopped scoring, he stopped making runs. Because he stopped making runs, the midfielders had no one to pass to, so they passed sideways. Because they passed sideways, the striker became more isolated and more discouraged. Who caused the slump? [PAUSE] Everyone, and no one. It is a loop.

`[SLIDE: "In the family" — the pursue–withdraw loop; Watzlawick's punctuation.]`

Families run on loops. Paul Watzlawick described a classic one: a wife complains that her husband withdraws, so she pursues him for connection; the husband says he withdraws because she nags. Each one punctuates the sequence differently — each believes the other started it. The systemic therapist does not take sides on who started it. She asks: how does each person's move invite the next? [PAUSE]

In the Adebayo family: the father withdraws, so the mother over-functions; the mother over-functions, so Tunde acts out; Tunde acts out, so the father feels like a failure and withdraws further. Round and round.

Here is the practical gift of circular thinking: if the loop has no single cause, then it also has no single place where change must begin. Any member who changes their move can change the pattern.

## SEGMENT 5 — Shift 3: From Content to Process

`[SLIDE: Shift 3 of 6 — "Content vs Process".]`

The third shift is from content to process.

Content is what is being argued about: the pass that went astray, the goal that was missed. Process is the pattern that repeats regardless of the content: every time the team concedes, the defenders blame the midfield, the midfield blames the striker, and the shape collapses. A good coach stops arguing about individual passes and studies the recurring shape. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "In the family" — tracking sequences.]`

Families bring content: money, in-laws, the children's school, who forgot what. The systemic therapist listens respectfully to content but tracks process: who speaks first, who interrupts, who goes quiet, who rescues whom, and what happens next. Minuchin called this tracking, and he taught therapists to watch sequences the way a coach watches tactics. [PAUSE]

Here is a simple exercise you will practise in MFT 706. In a session, when a family tells you about an argument, do not ask only what the argument was about. Ask: and then what happened? And then? And then? Within five steps you will usually see the loop. The content changes every week. The process rarely does — and process is where the therapy happens.

---

## SEGMENT 6 — Shift 4: From Why to What — Function, Feedback and Homeostasis

`[SLIDE: Shift 4 of 6 — "From why to what and how".]`

The fourth shift is from asking why to asking what and how. The individual paradigm digs for root causes in the past. The systemic paradigm asks: what is this symptom doing in the system right now, and how is the system keeping it going? [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "On the pitch" — the team that sits back after scoring.]`

Watch a team that scores first and then, every single time, drops deep and defends until they concede an equaliser. Nobody decided this. It is the team's automatic way of restoring a familiar state — anxious, cautious, level. This is homeostasis: the system's tendency to return to its accustomed balance, even when that balance is losing.

`[SLIDE: "In the family" — negative and positive feedback loops.]`

In a family, homeostasis is maintained by feedback. Negative feedback reduces change: when a teenager begins to grow independent, a parent's anxiety rises and rules tighten, pulling the family back to its old pattern. Positive feedback amplifies change: a small criticism provokes a sharper reply, which provokes a shout, which provokes a slammed door — escalation. [PAUSE]

And so we ask what a symptom does. Tunde's school refusal, painful as it is, may be the only thing that has put both parents in the same room. The symptom serves a function: it holds the system together while hurting the one who carries it. Understanding that function is often the first step toward finding a healthier way to meet the same need.

---

## SEGMENT 7 — Shift 5: From Observer to Participant

`[SLIDE: Shift 5 of 6 — "The coach is on the pitch too".]`

The fifth shift concerns you: the move from objective observer to participant.

The individual paradigm imagined the clinician standing outside the problem, like a scientist watching cells under a microscope. But a coach is not outside the team. Her substitutions, her shouting from the touchline, her mood after a defeat — all of it shapes how the players play. She is part of the system she is trying to change. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "In the family" — first- and second-order cybernetics; the therapeutic system.]`

Family therapists learned the same lesson in the 1980s, in the move from first-order to second-order cybernetics. The moment you sit down with a family, you are in the loop. Who you look at first, whose story you believe, what you find interesting — the family responds to all of it. There is now a new system in the room: the therapeutic system, which includes you. [PAUSE]

This is humbling, and it is also useful. It means your own reactions are information. If you find yourself wanting to rescue the mother or discipline the son, the family may be inviting you into a role that someone at home already plays. The systemic stance that follows is curiosity rather than certainty, and multipartiality — being on everyone's side at once — rather than neutrality that pretends to stand nowhere.

---

## SEGMENT 8 — Shift 6: Context and Meaning — and the Limits of the Analogy

`[SLIDE: Shift 6 of 6 — concentric circles: player, team, club, league, culture.]`

The sixth shift widens the lens once more: from the system to its context.

No team plays in a vacuum. The club's finances, the league's rules, the fans' expectations and the culture of the country all shape what happens on the pitch. Urie Bronfenbrenner described human development in the same way, as nested systems: the family inside the community, inside the economy, inside the culture. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "In the family" — culture, economy, faith community.]`

For the Adebayos, the father's job loss is not a private failure but an economic event. The family's silence about it is shaped by cultural beliefs about what a father should be. And their church may be either a source of shame or a source of support. A systemic therapist asks about all of it. For those of us who practise from faith, this shift feels familiar. Scripture never imagines a person apart from relationship; Paul writes that if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. That is systems thinking, two thousand years early. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "Where the analogy breaks — and a warning".]`

Now, every analogy has limits, and this is where I must be careful. Families are not teams. There is no league table, and the goal is not winning but flourishing. And one warning is essential: circular causality must never be used to excuse violence or abuse. When one person is hitting another, the systemic therapist does not say "you both contribute to the pattern." Safety comes first, responsibility is individual, and the systemic lens is set aside until it is safe to use. You will study this in depth in MFT 710 and MFT 712.

## SEGMENT 9 — Putting It Together: The Adebayos Through Both Lenses

`[SLIDE: Split screen — "Individual formulation" vs "Systemic formulation" for Tunde.]`

Let us put all six shifts together by writing two formulations for the same case.

Through the individual lens: Tunde, fourteen, presents with school refusal, irritability and social withdrawal of four months' duration. Possible adjustment disorder or depressive episode. Plan: individual therapy for Tunde, possible referral for medication, and parent guidance. [PAUSE]

Through the systemic lens: a family in transition following the father's job loss four months ago. The father has withdrawn, the mother has over-functioned, the daughter has become invisible, and Tunde's symptoms have organized the family around him, bringing the parents into a shared concern while keeping the real issue — the father's loss and shame — unspoken. The symptom is maintained by a loop of withdrawal, over-functioning and acting out. Plan: conjoint sessions including the father; track and interrupt the loop; help the family speak directly about the job loss; support Tunde's return to school as the family reorganizes. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "Both lenses together".]`

Notice that the two formulations are not enemies. Tunde may well be depressed, and he may need individual care. The systemic formulation does not deny that; it places it in context and opens doors that the individual formulation leaves closed — above all, the door through which the father walks back into the room. The best family therapists hold both lenses, and know which one to lift at which moment.

## SEGMENT 10 — Summary, Review and Close

`[SLIDE: Summary — the six shifts.]`

Let's summarize the six shifts. From the individual to the system. From linear to circular causality. From content to process. From why to what and how — function, feedback and homeostasis. From observer to participant. And from the system to its context. Together they make up the paradigm shift that created our profession — the move from watching the striker to watching the whole pitch. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Review questions.]`

Three review questions. One: explain punctuation in your own words, using an example from a family you know or have read about. Two: what does it mean to say that a symptom serves a function, and why does this not mean the family wants the symptom? Three: name one situation in which circular causality must not be applied, and explain why.

Next week we go deeper into the core systems concepts themselves: wholeness, circular causality, homeostasis and feedback. Note which questions each lens makes you ask. [PAUSE]
