## SEGMENT 1 — Welcome, Objectives, and "What Is a System?"

`[BOARD: Title — "Week 4: Core Systems Concepts I" in marker.]`

Welcome back to MFT 701. Over the last two weeks we have talked about systemic thinking as a history and as a paradigm shift. Today I am going to pick up a marker and draw it. We will build the four foundational concepts of family systems theory on the whiteboard, one line at a time: wholeness, circular causality, homeostasis and feedback. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: Four objectives written as a short list.]`

By the end of this lecture you will be able to define each of the four core concepts precisely, draw a simple diagram of each, recognize each one when it appears in a therapy session, and explain their limits.

`[BOARD: Two dots appear. Then a line connecting them. Then a third dot and two more lines, forming a triangle.]`

Let's begin with the simplest possible drawing. Here is one person — a dot. Here is another. On their own, two dots are just two individuals. But the moment I draw a line between them, something new exists that belongs to neither of them: a relationship. [PAUSE]

Ludwig von Bertalanffy defined a system as a set of elements standing in interaction. The elements are the dots. The system is the dots and the lines together. Now watch what happens when I add a third person. One more dot, but two more lines — and now three relationships, plus the relationships between the relationships. A family of five has ten lines. A family of seven has twenty-one. That is why families are complex: not because the people are complicated, but because the lines multiply. Family therapy is the discipline that treats the lines.

## SEGMENT 2 — Concept 1: Wholeness and Nonsummativity

`[BOARD: Heading "1. Wholeness". Four circles drawn in a cluster; then the spaces between them are shaded — "the pattern".]`

Our first concept is wholeness, sometimes called nonsummativity. The principle is ancient — Aristotle wrote that the whole is more than the sum of its parts — but systems theory made it precise. A system has properties that none of its elements has on its own. These are called emergent properties. [PAUSE]

Here is a clinical example that every experienced couples therapist has seen. Two people who are, individually, kind, reasonable and generous, form a marriage that is cruel. Neither of them is cruel. The cruelty is a property of the pattern between them. If you interviewed each one alone, you would meet two lovely people and have no idea where the cruelty comes from. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: An arrow pointing at the shaded spaces: "We treat THIS".]`

Wholeness has three practical consequences for how we work.

First, you cannot understand a family by adding up individual interviews. Something is missing — the pattern — and the pattern only shows itself when the people are together. This is why systemic therapists try to see the whole family together at least once, even when they go on to work with individuals.

Second, a change in any one member changes the whole. You already know this from the mobile in week one.

Third, the family has a character of its own. Experienced therapists speak of a family's mood, its rhythm, its climate. Those are not poetic phrases. They are descriptions of emergent properties — of the whole.

## SEGMENT 3 — Concept 1 continued: Organization, Subsystems and Open Systems

`[BOARD: Nested boxes — a large box "Family"; inside it smaller boxes "Parents" and "Children"; outside it a dotted larger box "Extended family / Community".]`

Wholes are organized. Inside every family there are smaller wholes — subsystems. The couple or spousal subsystem. The parental subsystem, which may or may not be the same people. The sibling subsystem. And the family as a whole is itself part of larger systems: the extended family, the church, the community. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: The lines of the boxes drawn in three styles — solid, dashed, and a thick wall.]`

Each of these boxes has an edge, and the edge is called a boundary. Boundaries have different degrees of permeability, and this brings us to one of the most useful distinctions in systems theory: open and closed systems.

A closed system exchanges nothing with its environment. A completely open system has no boundary at all. Living systems — and families are living systems — are neither. They are semi-permeable. Information, people, energy and resources flow in and out, but selectively.

`[BOARD: Two sketches — a sealed box labelled "too closed", a box with no edges labelled "too open".]`

Clinically, trouble appears at both extremes. A family that is too closed becomes rigid and isolated: no outsiders, no new ideas, secrets kept, help refused. A family that is too open has no protected interior: neighbours, relatives and strangers walk in and out of its decisions, and the children have no secure centre. Healthy families breathe — they have a clear edge and a working door. We will spend all of next week on boundaries; for now, notice that wholeness always implies an edge.

## SEGMENT 4 — Concept 2: Circular Causality

`[BOARD: Heading "2. Circular Causality". A straight arrow A → B is drawn and then crossed out. Beneath it, a loop with four numbered steps.]`

Our second concept is circular causality. You met it last week through the football team. Today we draw it precisely.

Linear causality is a straight arrow: A causes B. Circular causality is a loop in which each step is both an effect of the previous one and a cause of the next. Let me draw a classic. [PAUSE]

Step one: he comes home late and says little. Step two: she feels shut out and asks a series of questions. Step three: he experiences the questions as interrogation and goes quiet. Step four: she experiences the silence as rejection and raises her voice. And we are back to step one, because tomorrow he will stay out a little later. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: Two brackets drawn on the loop at different points — "his story starts here", "her story starts here".]`

Notice where each person begins the story. He begins at step two: "she interrogates me." She begins at step three: "he shuts me out." This is punctuation. Both are telling the truth, and both are leaving out the step before their own.

`[BOARD: A third dot drawn outside the loop with a dotted line pulling it in — "triangle".]`

One more line. When a two-person loop carries more anxiety than it can hold, it tends to pull in a third person: a child, a mother-in-law, a pastor, a drink, a job. Murray Bowen called this triangulation, and we will draw it fully in week five. For today, remember that loops rarely stay two-person for long.

How do we work with a loop? We ask circular questions. Not "why do you shout?" but "when he goes quiet, what do you do? And when you do that, what does he do next?" The question itself teaches the family to see the circle.

## SEGMENT 5 — Concept 3: Homeostasis

`[BOARD: Heading "3. Homeostasis". A thermostat drawn: a dial with a set point, a thermometer, a heater, arrows forming a cycle.]`

Our third concept is homeostasis. The word was coined by the physiologist Walter Cannon in 1932 to describe how the body keeps its temperature, its blood sugar and its chemistry within narrow limits. Don Jackson borrowed it in 1957 to describe families. [PAUSE]

The simplest picture is a thermostat. There is a set point — say, twenty-two degrees. There is a sensor that measures the room. When the temperature drops below the set point, the heater switches on; when it rises above, the heater switches off. The room is never exactly twenty-two degrees. It oscillates around twenty-two, and that oscillation is the system working.

`[BOARD: The thermostat relabelled: set point = "family rules"; sensor = "anxiety"; heater = "corrective behaviour".]`

Now let me relabel the drawing for a family. The set point is the family's rules — most of them unspoken — about how close, how loud, how honest, how independent people are allowed to be. The sensor is anxiety: when someone drifts beyond the rules, somebody feels it. And the heater is corrective behaviour: a joke, a frown, a symptom, a crisis, anything that brings the drifting member back toward the set point. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: A small sketch — a husband labelled "sober", a wife labelled "depressed", an arrow between them.]`

Here is the classic observation that made the concept famous. A man who has drunk heavily for years becomes sober. The family has prayed for this. And within months, his wife becomes depressed, or a child begins to fail at school. Nobody wants this. But the family had organized itself around the drinking — the roles, the alliances, the rules — and when the drinking stopped, the thermostat registered a change and the system worked to restore a familiar balance. Homeostasis is not malice. It is stability doing its job in the wrong direction.

## SEGMENT 6 — Concept 4: Feedback

`[BOARD: Heading "4. Feedback". Two loops side by side: one with a minus sign, one with a plus sign.]`

Our fourth concept is the mechanism behind homeostasis: feedback. Feedback is simply output that returns as input — the system's behaviour influences the system's next behaviour. There are two kinds, and the names confuse everyone, so let me draw them carefully. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: Left loop labelled "Negative feedback = deviation-reducing". A wavy line that settles toward a flat line.]`

Negative feedback reduces deviation. It is the thermostat. A teenager stays out an hour late; the parents respond; the next night he is home closer to the agreed time. The system corrected toward its set point. Negative here does not mean bad. It means it counteracts change. Negative feedback is what gives a family stability.

`[BOARD: Right loop labelled "Positive feedback = deviation-amplifying". A line that spirals outward.]`

Positive feedback amplifies deviation. A small criticism is met by a sharper reply, which is met by a shout, which is met by a slammed door. Each turn makes the next turn bigger. Positive does not mean good. It means more of the same. Left alone, positive feedback produces a runaway — a crisis, a separation, a breakdown. But positive feedback is also how systems grow: a small act of kindness that is noticed and returned and enlarged can transform a marriage. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: A therapist stick figure drawn beside the loops, holding a marker.]`

Now here is the important part for you. The therapist uses both kinds of feedback deliberately. When a couple escalates in session, you become negative feedback: you slow the pace, lower your voice, interrupt the spiral. And when a family reports a small improvement, you become positive feedback: you notice it, enlarge it, ask what made it possible — amplifying the deviation you want. Solution-focused therapists built an entire model on that second move.

## SEGMENT 7 — Supporting Concepts: Equifinality, Rules and Redundancy

`[BOARD: Three starting dots with arrows converging on one endpoint — "Equifinality". Then one starting dot with arrows diverging to three endpoints — "Equipotentiality".]`

Three supporting concepts complete today's toolkit.

The first is equifinality: in open systems, the same end state can be reached from very different starting points. Two families may both arrive at a child who refuses school by completely different routes — one through a parent's job loss, another through a bereavement, another through bullying. Its mirror is equipotentiality: the same beginning can lead to very different ends. Two children raised in the same home turn out utterly differently. [PAUSE]

Why does this matter? Because it frees us from the hunt for the single original cause. If many roads lead here, then the most useful question is not "where did this begin?" but "what is keeping it going now, and what would help it change?" Systemic therapy is largely a present-tense discipline.

`[BOARD: A list — "Rules: overt / covert". Example rules written in marker.]`

The second supporting concept is family rules. Jackson observed that family interaction is governed by rules, most of them never spoken. Overt rules are stated: no phones at dinner. Covert rules are lived: we do not mention Father's drinking; Mother's feelings come first; boys do not cry. Covert rules are the set point of the family thermostat, and much of therapy consists of making them visible so the family can decide whether to keep them.

The third is the redundancy principle. Out of all the behaviours a family could use, it actually uses a small, repeating set. That is good news for the therapist: you do not need to see everything. Watch a family for twenty minutes and the main patterns will appear, because they repeat.

## SEGMENT 8 — A Field Guide: Seeing the Concepts in the Room

`[BOARD: A two-column table — "Concept" / "What it looks like in session".]`

Let me turn the whiteboard into a field guide, so that you can recognize each concept when it walks into your office.

Wholeness looks like a family mood you can feel the moment they sit down — heavy, brittle, jokey, frozen — before anyone has said anything of substance.

Circular causality sounds like "he always" and "she never." Whenever you hear "always" and "never," you are hearing one person's punctuation of a loop.

Homeostasis looks like improvement followed by a new problem somewhere else. The son goes back to school, and two weeks later the parents are fighting, or the daughter develops stomach aches. The family thermostat has switched on. Expect this; warn families about it in advance.

Feedback looks like escalation in the room — voices rising turn by turn — or, more hopefully, like a small change that a family has noticed and begun to repeat. [PAUSE]

`[BOARD: The Adebayo family sketched — four dots, with lines; the loop; a thermostat icon.]`

And the Adebayos? Wholeness: the family arrived with a shared heaviness that belonged to no one person. Circular causality: the withdrawal, over-functioning, acting-out loop. Homeostasis: the family had reorganized around the father's silence, and Tunde's symptom was holding that arrangement in place. And feedback: if the therapist amplifies the first small moment when the father speaks about his loss — positive feedback in the right direction — a new pattern can begin to grow.

## SEGMENT 9 — Limits of the Concepts, and a Reflection

`[BOARD: A big question mark drawn beside the thermostat.]`

Before we finish, we need to be honest about the limits of what we have drawn.

These concepts came from physiology and engineering, and families are not thermostats. People have intentions, meanings, memories and moral agency; a heater does not. In 1982, the family therapist Paul Dell argued that homeostasis had become a lazy explanation — whenever a family did not change, therapists said "homeostasis," as if naming it explained it. Feminist critics added that talk of balance and circularity can hide real differences in power, and can quietly blame a victim for "participating in the pattern." [PAUSE]

So use these concepts as lenses, not as laws. They help you see; they do not tell you what is right. And remember the rule from last week: where there is violence or abuse, the language of circularity is set aside and responsibility is named.

`[BOARD: The four circles from Concept 1 redrawn; beside them, in marker: "If one member suffers…".]`

For those of us who practise from faith, I want to close the drawing with a reflection. The apostle Paul, writing to a divided community, reached for an image of wholeness long before systems theory: one body, many members, and if one member suffers, all suffer together; if one is honoured, all rejoice. That is nonsummativity. Scripture also holds together the two things a family needs — stability and transformation. Covenant is a set point: a promise that steadies a family when anxiety rises. And the call to be transformed is permission for the set point itself to change, for a family to find a new, healthier balance rather than returning forever to the old one. Systems theory describes how that happens. Faith, for many of our clients, supplies the courage to let it.
