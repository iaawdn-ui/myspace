## SEGMENT 1 — Welcome, Objectives and the Postmodern Turn

`[SLIDE: Title — "Week 7: The Postmodern Schools".]`

Welcome back to MFT 701. Last week we compared the four classic schools. They disagreed about much, but they shared a picture: the family is a system, the therapist is an expert who can see its patterns, and therapy changes the system. In the 1980s and 90s that picture was challenged from inside the field. This week: the three schools that emerged — Solution-Focused, Narrative and Collaborative therapy.

`[SLIDE: Learning objectives.]`

By the end of this lecture you will be able to explain the postmodern turn in family therapy; compare the three schools on our five criteria plus one new one; describe how each would open a first session with the Adebayos; and weigh their strengths and their critics fairly.

`[SLIDE: "The postmodern turn" — three shifts: from system to story · from pattern to meaning · from expert to collaborator.]`

What changed? Three things. First, from cybernetics to language. The early field borrowed from engineering; the new generation borrowed from philosophy — social constructionism, the idea that the realities we live in are built in conversation. Second, from observing systems to observed systems — second-order cybernetics: the therapist is not outside the family looking in, but part of the system being created in the room. Third, from the therapist as expert on families to the client as expert on their own life. Harlene Anderson and Harry Goolishian put it sharply: the problem is not in the system; the system is organized around a problem in language. Dissolve the problem in conversation, and the system around it dissolves too. [PAUSE]

`[GRID: Same grid as Week 6 — three new columns, a faded fourth column "Classic schools", and a sixth row: "Who is the expert?".]`

So here is our grid again. Three new columns. A faded fourth column summarizing the classic schools, for contrast. And a sixth row that last week's schools never needed: who is the expert?

## SEGMENT 2 — Column One: Solution-Focused Brief Therapy

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Steve de Shazer & Insoo Kim Berg, Brief Family Therapy Center, Milwaukee (1978); "Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy" (1985); roots in MRI and Milton Erickson.]`

Column one. Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg founded the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee in 1978, out of the MRI tradition. Their discovery was almost accidental: they noticed that when they stopped analysing problems and started asking about the times the problem did not happen, clients changed faster.

`[GRID: SFBT column fills.]`

View of the problem: the problem does not need to be understood for a solution to be built. Problem talk keeps people stuck; solution talk moves them. There are always exceptions — times the problem is absent or smaller — and they are the raw material of change.

Goal of therapy: a small, concrete, client-defined goal. "What will be different when this is no longer a problem?" Not insight, not restructuring. A described future, and the first step toward it.

Therapist's role: leading from one step behind. Not-knowing, curious, relentlessly respectful of the client's competence, generous with genuine compliments.

Signature techniques: the miracle question — "suppose tonight, while you sleep, a miracle happens and the problem is solved; what is the first thing you would notice tomorrow?"; exception questions; scaling questions — "on a scale of zero to ten, where are you today, and what would one point higher look like?"; coping questions for people in despair; and the instruction to do more of what already works.

Time and pace: brief, by design — often three to six sessions — and future-focused. Who is the expert? The client, on their life and their goals. The therapist is expert only in asking useful questions.

## SEGMENT 3 — Column Two: Narrative Therapy

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Michael White (Dulwich Centre, Adelaide) & David Epston (Auckland); "Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends" (1990); influences: Michel Foucault, Jerome Bruner.]`

Column two. Michael White in Adelaide and David Epston in Auckland published Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends in 1990, and brought the philosopher Michel Foucault into the therapy room. Their question was not "what is wrong with this family?" but "what story has this family been recruited into, and who benefits from it?"

`[GRID: Narrative column fills.]`

View of the problem: people make sense of their lives through stories. When a problem-saturated story becomes dominant — "Tunde is a difficult boy" — it edits out everything that does not fit, and it is usually propped up by wider cultural stories about boys, about success, about failure. The slogan: the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem.

Goal of therapy: re-authoring. Thickening an alternative, preferred story — one with enough evidence and enough witnesses to be lived in.

Therapist's role: de-centred but influential. Not the author, but a skilled co-editor: curious, transparent about their own thinking, politically awake to power and discourse.

Signature techniques: externalizing conversations — the problem is given a name and a place outside the person; relative-influence questions — "how much of your life has the Silence taken, and how much have you kept?"; unique outcomes — moments the problem did not win; re-authoring and re-membering conversations; outsider-witness ceremonies; and therapeutic letters and certificates that document the new story.

Time and pace: present and past re-read, moderate length. Who is the expert? The person, on their own life; the therapist on the craft of conversation — and never on what the preferred story should be.

## SEGMENT 4 — Column Three: Collaborative Therapy

`[SLIDE: Spotlight — Harlene Anderson & Harold Goolishian, Houston Galveston Institute; "Conversation, Language, and Possibilities" (1997); Tom Andersen's reflecting team (Tromsø, 1987).]`

Column three. Harlene Anderson and Harry Goolishian at the Houston Galveston Institute took the postmodern turn furthest. Their close kin is Tom Andersen in northern Norway, who in 1985 turned the lights on in the observation room and let the family listen to the team talk about them — the reflecting team.

`[GRID: Collaborative column fills.]`

View of the problem: problems exist in language. A "problem-organizing system" is simply the set of people who are talking about something as a problem. There is no structure underneath to fix.

Goal of therapy: a conversation in which new meaning can emerge, until the problem dissolves — it is no longer something anyone needs to talk about.

Therapist's role: the not-knowing stance — the most famous phrase of the postmodern movement. Not ignorance, but a genuine curiosity that refuses to understand too quickly. The therapist is a conversational partner, a host, present with rather than acting on.

Signature techniques: Anderson disliked the word. But in practice: dialogical conversation; questions that come from inside the client's story rather than from a theory; making the therapist's inner talk public; and the reflecting team — colleagues speaking tentatively, in the family's hearing, about what moved them.

Time and pace: present, open-ended, unhurried. Who is the expert? Nobody and everybody. Expertise is shared; the therapist's responsibility is for the quality of the dialogue, not for its outcome.

## SEGMENT 6 — The Same Family, Three Doors

`[SLIDE: Three panels — the Adebayos through three first sessions.]`

Door one, Solution-Focused. The therapist asks what needs to happen in this session for the family to say it was worth coming. Then the miracle question — to everyone, including Bisi. Mrs. Adebayo says that tomorrow Tunde would get up without a fight. "Has there been any morning, even one, that was a little like that?" There was — the Tuesday his father drove him. Scaling: where are we today, out of ten? What would one point higher look like? The session ends with compliments and a small task: notice what is already working.

Door two, Narrative. The therapist asks Tunde what he would call the thing that keeps him home. He calls it "the Heaviness." How long has the Heaviness been around? Did it arrive before or after his father's job ended? Does it visit anyone else in the house? — and suddenly the father is nodding. Relative influence: how much of the week does the Heaviness run, and when has Tunde outwitted it? A letter follows the session, addressed to Tunde, documenting the morning the Heaviness lost.

Door three, Collaborative. No agenda, no intake form. "Where would you like to begin?" The therapist listens, asks only from inside what each person says, and resists forming a hypothesis. Slowly the conversation moves from Tunde's school to the morning Mr. Adebayo was told not to come back to work. A reflecting team, listening behind the family, speaks about what moved them — and the family listens to themselves being heard. [PAUSE]

Three doors, one family — and in all three, nobody was the problem.

## SEGMENT 7 — What Changed, What Stayed, and When to Choose Which

`[SLIDE: Split screen — "Classic" vs "Postmodern": system ↔ story · pattern ↔ meaning · expert ↔ collaborator · homeostasis ↔ possibility · pathology ↔ competence.]`

Put the two generations side by side. From system to story. From pattern to meaning. From therapist-as-expert to therapist-as-collaborator. From homeostasis to possibility. From what is wrong to what is strong. And yet notice what stayed: the problem is still relational, not located inside one person; the work is still present-focused and comparatively brief; nobody is blamed; and the therapist is still active — asking is as active as directing.

`[SLIDE: "Lean toward … when …" — three cards.]`

When might you lean toward each? Solution-Focused: when the family wants brief, goal-oriented work; with mandated or reluctant clients, who respond to being treated as competent; in schools and agencies with few sessions; and when problem talk is spiralling and needs interrupting. It also has the strongest research base of the three. Narrative: when the problem has become an identity — "I am a failure," "we are a bad family"; where shame is heavy; where cultural or religious discourse is doing damage; and with children, who take to externalizing instantly. Collaborative: when the stuck system includes professionals — school, social services, church — and the hierarchy of expertise itself is the obstacle; and in consultation and organizational work.

One boundary: where safety is at stake — violence, abuse, suicide risk — the therapist's expertise does not dissolve. The postmodern humility is about meaning, not about duty of care.

## SEGMENT 8 — Fair Criticisms

`[SLIDE: "Fair criticisms" — four lines.]`

The postmodern schools attracted sharp critics, and the sharpest was Salvador Minuchin. In a 1998 article he asked, "Where is the family in narrative family therapy?" His charge: in attending to stories and discourse, the new schools had lost the family as a living unit in the room, and had let the therapist off the hook for producing change.

Second, relativism. If every story is a construction, is a story of abuse just one story among others? Narrative therapists answer that they are not neutral about injustice — Foucault was about power, after all — but the question has to be faced honestly, and we faced it in week three: circularity and construction stop where harm begins.

Third, Solution-Focused therapy has been accused of superficiality — of skipping over grief and trauma on the way to a goal. Its best practitioners do not, but the risk is real.

Fourth, the not-knowing stance can hide power rather than remove it. A therapist who claims no expertise still chooses which question to ask next. Honest practice names that influence rather than denying it.

And fifth, evidence. Solution-Focused therapy has a respectable outcome literature; Narrative and Collaborative therapy have far less, partly because they resist being standardized. That is a philosophical choice with a practical cost.

## SEGMENT 9 — A Brief Reflection: Story, Hope and Truth

`[SLIDE: Reflection — "The gospel is a story, and it re-authors."]`

For those who practise from faith, the postmodern schools are both a gift and a question.

The gift. Scripture is not a textbook of systems; it is a story — and a re-authoring one. "If anyone is in Christ, there is a new creation: the old has passed away." That is the thickest alternative story ever told, and narrative therapy's instinct that identity is re-written through story, witnesses and documents is deeply familiar to anyone who has watched a baptism. Solution-Focused therapy's orientation toward hope — "whatever is true, whatever is honourable, think on these things" — and the collaborative insistence on dialogue — "come, let us reason together" — each echo something the church has long practised.

The question. Postmodernism says all stories are constructed; the Christian faith says some stories are true. You do not have to choose. Hold the therapist's humility — I do not know this family's life better than they do — without surrendering the conviction that truth exists and that some stories, like the story that a child is worthless, are simply false. The not-knowing stance is a discipline of listening, not a doctrine of relativism. Practise the discipline. Keep the conviction.
