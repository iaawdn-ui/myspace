## SEGMENT 1 — Welcome, Recap, Objectives and Hook

`[SLIDE: Title card — "Week 2: Historical Roots — The Birth of a Profession".]`

Welcome back to MFT 701. Last week we built our course map, and we visited node three, history, only briefly. This week we travel the whole road — from the early twentieth century to today — to see how Marriage and Family Therapy came to exist. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Learning objectives — four bullets.]`

By the end of this lecture you will be able to, first, trace the main streams that flowed together to form the profession; second, explain what happened in Palo Alto in the 1950s and why it mattered; third, name the founding pioneers and the schools that grew from them; and fourth, describe how critiques, research and professionalization shaped the field we practise in today.

`[SLIDE: Hook — a 1950s hospital corridor illustration. Caption: "The patient who got better… until she went home."]`

Let me begin with a puzzle that troubled psychiatrists in the early 1950s. A young woman is admitted to hospital with severe symptoms of schizophrenia. After months of treatment, she improves remarkably. She is discharged and goes home to her family. Within weeks, she relapses. She is readmitted, improves again, goes home again — and relapses again. [PAUSE]

For decades, the explanation was simple: the illness lives inside the patient. But a handful of clinicians began asking a dangerous question: what if something at home is part of the pattern? That question is the seed from which our whole profession grew.

## SEGMENT 2 — Era 1: Before 1950 — Streams Converging

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "Before 1950". Title: "Streams Converging".]`

Before there was family therapy, there were several separate streams.

`[SLIDE: Card — "Psychoanalysis: the individual alone".]`

The first stream is psychoanalysis, which dominated early twentieth-century mental health. Sigmund Freud focused entirely on the inner life of the individual. In 1909 he treated a five-year-old boy, Little Hans, almost entirely through conversations with the boy's father — yet he never developed this into family work. In fact, analysts were trained not to see a patient's relatives at all, for fear of contaminating the treatment. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Card — "Child guidance clinics (Adler, 1920s)".]`

The second stream is the child guidance movement. In 1920s Vienna, Alfred Adler opened clinics where children were seen together with their parents and teachers. The idea travelled to America, where child guidance clinics typically assigned a psychiatrist to the child and a social worker to the mother — separately. The family was in the building, but not yet in the same room.

`[SLIDE: Card — "The marriage counselling movement (1929–1942)".]`

The third stream is marriage counselling. In 1929, Abraham and Hannah Stone opened a marriage consultation centre in New York. In 1930, Paul Popenoe founded the American Institute of Family Relations in Los Angeles. In 1932, Emily Mudd established the Marriage Council of Philadelphia. And in 1942, these practitioners formed the American Association of Marriage Counselors — the organization that, decades later, would become the AAMFT.

## SEGMENT 3 — Era 1 continued: Pastoral Roots and New Ideas

`[SLIDE: Card — "Pastoral care and clinical pastoral education (1925)".]`

There is a fourth stream that is often left out of secular histories, and it matters especially for us at Triune. Long before psychiatry took interest in families, clergy were the people communities turned to for marriage and family trouble. In 1925, Anton Boisen began the clinical pastoral education movement, training ministers in hospitals. Many early marriage counsellors were pastors, and churches were among the first institutions to offer structured premarital and marital guidance. The integration of faith and family care is not a modern add-on; it is part of the profession's ancestry. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Card — "Ideas from outside psychiatry: Lewin, Wiener, von Bertalanffy (1940s)".]`

Finally, in the 1940s, powerful new ideas arrived from outside psychiatry altogether. Kurt Lewin's field theory argued that behaviour is a function of the person and their environment together. The biologist Ludwig von Bertalanffy proposed General Systems Theory — that living systems share common principles of organization. And in 1948 the mathematician Norbert Wiener published Cybernetics, the science of feedback and self-regulation in machines and living things. Between 1946 and 1953, the Macy Conferences in New York brought mathematicians, biologists and social scientists together around these ideas. One regular participant was an anthropologist named Gregory Bateson. [PAUSE]

Hold these streams in mind: individual psychology, child guidance, marriage counselling, pastoral care, and systems and cybernetics. In the 1950s, they converged.

## SEGMENT 4 — Era 2: Palo Alto, 1952–1962

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "1950s — Palo Alto". Title: "The Bateson Project".]`

In 1952, Gregory Bateson received a grant to study communication at the Veterans Administration hospital in Palo Alto, California. He assembled an unusual team: Jay Haley, a young communications student; John Weakland, a chemical engineer turned anthropologist; William Fry, a psychiatrist; and later Don Jackson, a psychiatrist who had worked with families of patients with schizophrenia. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Primary-source slide — paper title "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia", Bateson, Jackson, Haley and Weakland, 1956. Key phrase: "the double bind".]`

In 1956 the team published a paper called "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia." It introduced the concept of the double bind: a situation in which a person receives contradictory messages at different levels — for example, a mother who says "come and hug me" while stiffening and pulling away, and then asks, "why are you so cold toward me?" — with no way to comment on the contradiction and no way to leave. The team proposed that repeated exposure to such binds could contribute to the kind of confused communication seen in schizophrenia. [PAUSE]

Today we know the double bind does not cause schizophrenia, and the field has rightly abandoned the blaming of mothers that this era sometimes encouraged. But the paper's deeper contribution survived: it was the first serious attempt to understand a symptom as a product of a communication system rather than a defect inside one person.

## SEGMENT 5 — Era 2 continued: Jackson, Satir and the Mental Research Institute

`[SLIDE: Card trio — Don Jackson "Family homeostasis, 1957"; MRI founded 1959; Virginia Satir and Paul Watzlawick.]`

Don Jackson was the clinician of the group, and he gave us one of our most enduring ideas. In 1957 he described family homeostasis: the tendency of a family to maintain its balance, so that when one member changes, others act — often without realizing it — to restore the old pattern. Remember our young woman who relapsed every time she went home? Jackson's concept explained why. [PAUSE]

In 1959 Jackson founded the Mental Research Institute, the MRI, in Palo Alto. Among the early staff was a social worker named Virginia Satir, who had been seeing whole families together in Chicago since the early 1950s, and who would become the field's most beloved teacher. Paul Watzlawick joined in 1960, and the MRI's work on communication produced the famous axiom, "one cannot not communicate." [PAUSE]

From the MRI would later come the Brief Therapy Center in 1967, and the 1974 book Change by Watzlawick, Weakland and Fisch — the foundation of brief and strategic therapy. Palo Alto was the field's laboratory. But it was not the only place where something was stirring.

## SEGMENT 6 — Era 3: The Pioneers of the 1950s Across America

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "1950s — Pioneers". Map of the US with pins: Topeka/Bethesda (Bowen), New Haven (Lidz), Bethesda (Wynne), New York (Ackerman), Atlanta (Whitaker), Philadelphia (Boszormenyi-Nagy).]`

Across the country, independently of each other, other clinicians were making the same discovery.

Murray Bowen, a psychiatrist at the Menninger Clinic and then at the National Institute of Mental Health, did something radical in 1954: he hospitalized entire families alongside the patient with schizophrenia, and observed them for months. He saw emotional triangles, fusion, and patterns passing down generations. He moved to Georgetown University in 1959 and spent the rest of his life developing Bowen Family Systems Theory.

Theodore Lidz at Yale described marital schism and marital skew — patterns of parental conflict that organized the whole household. Lyman Wynne, also at NIMH, described pseudomutuality — families presenting a false harmony that left no room for real difference. [PAUSE]

In New York, Nathan Ackerman — a child psychiatrist who had written about "the unity of the family" as early as 1938 — published The Psychodynamics of Family Life in 1958, the first book-length treatment of family therapy, and opened what became the Ackerman Institute in 1960.

In Atlanta, Carl Whitaker was already seeing families with a provocative, deeply personal style that became experiential family therapy. And in Philadelphia, Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy was developing contextual therapy, built on loyalty, fairness and the ledger of obligations across generations.

## SEGMENT 7 — Era 4: The 1960s — A Field Coalesces

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "1960s". Timeline markers: 1962 Family Process; 1963 California licence; 1964 Conjoint Family Therapy; 1965 Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic; 1967 Families of the Slums.]`

In the 1960s these scattered pioneers discovered one another and became a field.

In 1962, Ackerman and Jackson founded the journal Family Process, with Jay Haley as its first editor. For the first time, family therapists had a shared conversation in print. [PAUSE]

In 1963, California became the first state to license marriage, family and child counsellors — the beginning of legal recognition.

In 1964, Virginia Satir published Conjoint Family Therapy, which became the field's most widely read introductory text, and she carried family therapy into the human potential movement at Esalen.

And in 1965, an Argentine psychiatrist named Salvador Minuchin became director of the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic. He had been working at the Wiltwyck School with delinquent boys from poor families in New York, and in 1967 he and his colleagues published Families of the Slums. Minuchin discovered that insight-based talk did little for these families, but changing the structure of their interactions in the room — boundaries, alliances, hierarchy — produced real change. Jay Haley joined him in Philadelphia in 1967, and the clinic became the most influential training centre in the world. [PAUSE]

Notice the pattern of this decade: a journal, a licence, a textbook, a training institution. These are the signs of a profession being born.

## SEGMENT 8 — Era 5: The 1970s — The Golden Age of the Schools

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "1970s". Six book covers as cards: Change (1974); Families and Family Therapy (1974); Problem-Solving Therapy (1976); Paradox and Counterparadox (1978); Family Therapy in Clinical Practice (1978); plus "AAMFT, 1978".]`

The 1970s were the golden age of the schools, and almost every major model produced its defining book in this decade.

In 1974, Minuchin published Families and Family Therapy — the classic statement of Structural Family Therapy. In the same year, Watzlawick, Weakland and Fisch published Change, the manifesto of MRI brief therapy. In 1976, Jay Haley published Problem-Solving Therapy and, with Cloé Madanes, founded the Family Therapy Institute of Washington, launching Strategic Family Therapy. [PAUSE]

In Italy, Mara Selvini Palazzoli and her Milan team published Paradox and Counterparadox in 1978, introducing circular questioning and the systemic hypothesizing you will meet in MFT 702. And in the same year, Murray Bowen gathered his life's work into Family Therapy in Clinical Practice. [PAUSE]

The profession also organized itself. In 1970 the American Association of Marriage Counselors became the American Association of Marriage and Family Counselors, and in 1978 it took its present name, the American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy. That same year, the United States government recognized the Commission on Accreditation for Marriage and Family Therapy Education — COAMFTE — as the official accrediting body for training programmes. The field now had its schools, its texts, its association and its standards.

## SEGMENT 9 — Era 6: The 1980s and 90s — Critique and the Postmodern Turn

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "1980s–90s". Two columns: "The critiques" (feminist, multicultural) and "The new models" (solution-focused, narrative, collaborative, EFT, Gottman research).]`

Every healthy profession must face honest criticism, and the 1980s brought it.

Feminist family therapists — Rachel Hare-Mustin in a landmark 1978 article, and the Women's Project led by Marianne Walters, Betty Carter, Peggy Papp and Olga Silverstein — pointed out that "neutral" systems thinking often ignored real differences in power between men and women, and sometimes held mothers responsible for everything. In 1982, Monica McGoldrick and colleagues published Ethnicity and Family Therapy, challenging the field to take culture seriously. These critiques did not destroy systems thinking; they matured it. [PAUSE]

At the same time, a philosophical shift — social constructionism and what was called second-order cybernetics — reminded therapists that they are never neutral observers outside the family, but participants in a conversation.

From this soil grew the postmodern models. In Milwaukee, Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg developed Solution-Focused Brief Therapy, asking about exceptions and preferred futures rather than problems. In Australia and New Zealand, Michael White and David Epston published Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends in 1990, teaching therapists to externalize problems and help families re-author their stories. Harlene Anderson and Harry Goolishian developed collaborative language systems therapy in Texas. [PAUSE]

And two research-driven streams began that would shape couples therapy for decades: Susan Johnson and Leslie Greenberg introduced Emotionally Focused Therapy in 1985, and John Gottman's laboratory began the longitudinal studies of couples that now underpin much of what we know about marital stability.

## SEGMENT 10 — Era 7: 2000s to Today; Lessons; Review; Close

`[SLIDE: Era strip highlights "2000s–Today". Cards: Evidence-based practice; Integration & common factors; Global profession (IFTA 1987, Africa); Faith integration; Technology.]`

Since the year 2000, the field has consolidated around evidence. Models such as Functional Family Therapy, Multisystemic Therapy, Multidimensional Family Therapy, Emotionally Focused Therapy and Behavioural Couples Therapy have been tested in rigorous trials. Common-factors research has shown that what unites effective family therapists matters as much as what divides their schools. Medical family therapy, neuroscience and telehealth have widened our scope. And the profession has gone global: the International Family Therapy Association was founded in 1987, and training programmes now exist across Africa, Asia and Latin America — which is exactly why you are studying this course from wherever you are today. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: "Lessons from the timeline" — four lines.]`

What does this history teach us? First, the profession was born from careful observation — clinicians who refused to ignore what they saw when the patient went home. Second, its best ideas came from outside psychiatry: anthropology, biology, engineering and, yes, the pastoral tradition. Third, critique made the field stronger, not weaker. And fourth, the story is not finished. Somewhere on this timeline there is a space for your contribution. [PAUSE]
