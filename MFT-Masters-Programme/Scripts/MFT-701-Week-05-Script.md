## SEGMENT 1 — Welcome, Objectives and the Plan: One Diagram, Five Layers

`[SLIDE: Title — "Week 5: Core Systems Concepts II".]`

Welcome back to MFT 701. Last week you drew four concepts on a whiteboard. This week we build one diagram. By the end of the lecture you will have a complete structural map of the Adebayo family, and you will have earned every line on it.

`[SLIDE: Learning objectives.]`

By the end of this lecture you will be able to describe the three family subsystems and what each requires; classify boundaries as clear, diffuse or rigid, and locate a family on the enmeshment–disengagement continuum; explain hierarchy and recognize a cross-generational coalition; and define Bowen's emotional triangle and identify one in a case.

`[SLIDE: Five empty layer chips: Members · Subsystems · Boundaries · Hierarchy · Triangles.]`

Here is the plan. We will add five layers to the diagram, in this order: members, subsystems, boundaries, hierarchy and triangles. Each layer answers a question the previous one could not. Members tell us who is in the system. Subsystems tell us how the members are grouped. Boundaries tell us how open those groupings are. Hierarchy tells us who is in charge. And triangles tell us how anxiety moves between people. [PAUSE]

Two names will be with us throughout. Salvador Minuchin, the founder of Structural Family Therapy, gave us the first four layers and the notation we will use to draw them. Murray Bowen gave us the fifth. Pick up your pencil — you should draw this map as we go.

## SEGMENT 2 — Layer 1: Members

`[LAYER 1: Four circles appear — F (Father), M (Mother), T (Tunde, 14), B (Bisi, 10) — spaced evenly, no lines yet.]`

Layer one is the simplest: who is in the system? Here are the Adebayos. Mr. Adebayo, the father, out of work for four months. Mrs. Adebayo, the mother, now holding two jobs. Tunde, fourteen, who has stopped going to school. And his younger sister — let us call her Bisi — ten years old, very quiet and very obedient.

Even this first layer requires a judgement. Who counts as a member? The household is not always the family. If Mr. Adebayo's mother telephones every evening and advises on every decision, she may be a more active member of this system than anyone living in the house. Minuchin's rule of thumb is functional: a member is anyone whose behaviour regularly shapes the pattern. For today, we will start with four and keep the grandmother in reserve. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Minuchin's definition — "Family structure is the invisible set of functional demands that organizes the ways in which family members interact."]`

Minuchin defined family structure as the invisible set of functional demands that organizes the ways in which family members interact. Notice the word invisible. You will never see a structure; you infer it from repeated transactions — who speaks for whom, who sits next to whom, who is interrupted, who is obeyed.

Why is layer one not enough? Because four circles tell us who, but nothing about how they are organized. A list of names is a census, not a map. We need to see the groupings.

---

## SEGMENT 3 — Layer 2: Subsystems

`[LAYER 2: Two rounded boxes enclose the circles — F and M together ("spousal / parental"), T and B together ("sibling").]`

Layer two groups the members into subsystems. Minuchin named three that every family must carry out.

The spousal subsystem is the two adults as partners. Its task is mutual support, intimacy and the negotiation of difference, and it needs some protection from the children and from the in-laws to do that work.

The parental subsystem is the same two people, or whoever raises the children, in their executive role: nurture, guidance, limit-setting. It is the same couple wearing a different hat — and families get into trouble when the two hats become one, when the only thing the couple ever talks about is the children.

The sibling subsystem is the children among themselves. Minuchin called it the first social laboratory: it is where a child learns to negotiate, compete, make alliances and lose gracefully. [PAUSE]

Every person belongs to several subsystems at once, and each one asks for different skills. Mrs. Adebayo is a wife in one, a mother in another, a daughter in a third, and the competence she shows in one does not automatically transfer to the others.

`[SLIDE: Note — "One person, many subsystems; each demands different skills."]`

Why is layer two not enough? Because a box tells us membership, but not how open the box is. Two families can have identical subsystems and be completely different in how much information, affection and influence crosses from one box to the other. For that we need boundaries.

---

## SEGMENT 4 — Layer 3: Boundaries

`[LAYER 3: Lines appear between the subsystems. Minuchin notation: clear = dashed line; diffuse = dotted line; rigid = solid heavy line. In the Adebayo map, a dotted (diffuse) line between M and T, a heavy solid (rigid) line around F.]`

Layer three draws the edges. A boundary is the set of rules about who participates in a subsystem, and how. Minuchin described three kinds, and the notation you are drawing is his.

A clear boundary — a dashed line — is firm enough to protect the subsystem's work and open enough to allow contact. Parents can have a private conversation; the children can still knock.

A diffuse boundary — a dotted line — is too open. Everyone is in everyone's business. Minuchin called the result enmeshment: a system so close that a sneeze in one room produces a chorus of blessings in the next. Autonomy is hard to find.

A rigid boundary — a heavy solid line — is too closed. Little passes through. The result is disengagement: members may live under one roof and barely affect one another; distress goes unnoticed until it is loud. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: The enmeshment–disengagement continuum, with "clear" in the middle. Caption: "The test is function, not closeness."]`

Two cautions. First, enmeshment and disengagement are ends of a continuum, not diagnoses; most families lean one way, and every family moves along the line as it goes through stress. Second, closeness is not the same as enmeshment. Many African, Asian and Latin families are far closer than a North American textbook expects, and are entirely healthy. The question is never "how close are they?" It is "does the boundary let each subsystem do its job?"

In the Adebayo map, notice the dotted line between mother and son, and the heavy wall around the father. But a boundary tells us permeability, not authority. Who is actually in charge here? That is layer four.

---

## SEGMENT 5 — Layer 4: Hierarchy

`[LAYER 4: The map rearranges vertically — a generational line appears. F and M should sit above it; T and B below. But in the Adebayo map T has moved UP beside M, with triple lines (over-involvement) between M and T, a coalition bracket around M–T against F, and a zigzag conflict line between F and M.]`

Layer four asks the question that boundaries cannot: who is in charge? Every family has a hierarchy, and Minuchin argued that a functional family keeps a clear generational boundary: the parents form an executive subsystem that holds more authority and more responsibility than the children. This is not about being harsh. It is about children being free to be children because someone else is carrying the weight.

Watch what has happened in the Adebayo map. Tunde has moved up. With his father absent most evenings and his mother exhausted, Tunde has become her confidant and her lieutenant. The triple lines between them mark over-involvement. The bracket marks a coalition — mother and son joined against father. The zigzag between the parents marks open conflict. [PAUSE]

Jay Haley called this configuration the cross-generational coalition, and he considered it the single most common structure behind childhood symptoms: a parent and a child joined against the other parent, usually covertly. The child who is pulled upward is sometimes called a parentified child. He gains status and loses a childhood. And notice the cost on the other side: Bisi is now alone in the sibling subsystem, which may be exactly why she has gone so quiet.

`[SLIDE: Note — "Symptom often appears in the child who holds the structure together."]`

Why is layer four not enough? Because a structure is a snapshot. It shows the arrangement but not the engine that keeps rearranging it. For that we need to see how anxiety moves. We need Bowen.

---

## SEGMENT 6 — Layer 5: Triangles

`[LAYER 5: A large triangle overlays F, M and T. M–T side labelled "insiders (close)"; F at the far corner labelled "outsider". Anxiety arrows circulate.]`

Layer five comes from Murray Bowen. His observation was that a two-person emotional system is unstable. When anxiety between two people rises past what they can hold, they pull in a third — a child, a relative, a pastor, an affair, a bottle, a job. The triangle, Bowen said, is the smallest stable unit of an emotional system.

`[SLIDE: Generic triangle, two states — "calm: two insiders, one comfortable outsider" and "anxious: the outside position becomes desirable; the conflict moves to a new side".]`

A triangle has two insider positions and one outsider. In calm periods, the insiders enjoy closeness and the outsider feels a little left out. Under stress the picture flips: the insiders get uncomfortable with each other, one of them moves toward the outsider, and the conflict shifts to a different side. Anxiety does not disappear; it travels around the triangle. [PAUSE]

Now look at the Adebayos. Mother and Tunde are the insiders; father is on the outside. The marital anxiety has not gone away — it has relocated into the mother–son relationship, and Tunde's symptom is where it has surfaced. Bowen would add that triangles interlock. Father, pushed to the outside, forms his own triangle with his evenings out. Mother forms another with her own mother on the telephone. Pull one triangle and the others move.

One final and very practical point. The moment this family sits down with you, they will try to triangle you in. Mother will want you to agree that father is the problem; father may want you to see that mother spoils the boy. Staying connected to each person without taking a side is what Bowen called detriangling, and it is one of the hardest skills you will learn.

## SEGMENT 7 — Reading the Whole Map, and What the Therapist Does With It

`[SLIDE: The complete five-layer map, all layers visible.]`

Let us read the finished map in thirty seconds, the way you would in supervision. Four members. Two subsystems, but the spousal subsystem has almost no protected space. A diffuse boundary between mother and son; a rigid boundary around father. Hierarchy inverted: the son has risen to the executive level in a coalition with mother against father, who is in open conflict with her. And a triangle in which the marital anxiety has settled on the mother–son side and surfaced as Tunde's school refusal, with Bisi isolated below. [PAUSE]

Two things to say about such a map. First, it is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis. You will revise it in the first session when the family does something you did not expect. Second, it is not a verdict on anyone's character. The map describes an arrangement, not a villain.

`[SLIDE: Two columns — "Structural moves" and "Bowenian moves".]`

What would you do with it? A structural therapist works on the map directly. Joining first: earning enough trust to be allowed to intervene. Then enactment: asking the parents to discuss Tunde's school in the room, and keeping Tunde out of it, so that the spousal subsystem has to function. Boundary-making: moving chairs, blocking interruptions, giving the parents a task to complete without the children. And unbalancing: temporarily siding with the father to pull him back inside.

A Bowenian therapist works more slowly, on the triangle. Lowering the anxiety in the room. Staying in contact with each person without taking sides. Coaching the parents, often one at a time, to deal directly with each other instead of through Tunde. Different routes. The same map.

## SEGMENT 8 — Three Maps You Will Meet Again and Again

`[SLIDE: Three small maps side by side.]`

Before we finish the diagram, let me show you three maps that you will recognize within your first year of practice.

Map one: detoured conflict. The parents cannot face their own disagreement, so they unite in worry or anger about a child. The child becomes the family's project, and sometimes its patient. On the map: a clear-looking parental line, but every arrow points at the child. The marital boundary looks strong only because nobody is allowed behind it.

Map two: the cross-generational coalition we have already drawn. One parent and one child against the other parent. Look for a child who knows too much about the marriage.

Map three: the disengaged father, enmeshed mother. A common pattern across many cultures, and one that families often describe as normal. Father provides and withdraws; mother and children form a dense emotional unit; father is treated as a visitor in his own home. It can function for years, until the children reach adolescence and need both a close base and a clear exit. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: A fourth, "healthy enough" map for contrast — clear boundaries, parents together above the line, children together below, grandparents connected but outside.]`

And here, for contrast, is a healthy-enough map. Not a perfect one. Parents together above the generational line with a protected space of their own. Children together below it, able to reach their parents. Clear dashed lines, not walls and not fog. Grandparents connected to the system but not running it. Hold this picture lightly: healthy structures look different in different cultures and at different stages of life. The question is always function.

## SEGMENT 9 — Limits, Critiques and a Brief Reflection

`[SLIDE: "Limits of the map".]`

Three honest limits. First, culture. Minuchin's map was drawn in New York in the 1960s and 1970s, and some of its assumptions about the ideal family are local. What looks like enmeshment in Philadelphia may be ordinary interdependence in Ibadan or Manila. Always ask what is normative for this family's community before you label a boundary.

Second, gender and power. In 1978, Rachel Hare-Mustin pointed out that structural therapists often restored hierarchy by restoring the father, and that enmeshed mother was frequently a diagnosis given to women who were doing all the caregiving. Hierarchy means a functional executive subsystem — it does not mean a particular person on top.

Third, the therapist. You have a family map of your own, and you will feel its pull in the room. The family that triangles you most easily is usually the one that resembles yours. Supervision exists for this reason. [PAUSE]

`[SLIDE: Reflection — Genesis 2:24; Ephesians 6:1–4.]`

For those who practise from faith, two short reflections. Genesis says that a man shall leave his father and mother and cleave to his wife: leaving and cleaving is a generational boundary, drawn thousands of years before Minuchin, and many of the marital triangles you will see are failures of leaving. And Ephesians chapter six places hierarchy inside reciprocity — children are to honour, and fathers are told not to exasperate. Authority, in that picture, is responsibility turned toward the weaker member. That is a good definition of a healthy executive subsystem in any family.
